# Mentorship Program — Buddy System

> **As a** new hire, **I want to** have a designated buddy who supports my onboarding, **so that** I have a clear point of contact for questions, feedback, and guidance during my first 90 days.

> The buddy system is the single most effective onboarding practice. A good buddy reduces time-to-first-meaningful-contribution by 30-50%. The buddy is not a manager — they are a peer who provides technical guidance, cultural orientation, and a safe space for "dumb" questions.

## Summary

- Buddy assignment: Every new hire is assigned a buddy on day 1. The buddy is a peer (not a manager) on the same or adjacent team.
- Buddy responsibilities: Daily check-ins in week 1, PR reviews, knowledge transfer, cultural orientation, and escalation path
- Duration: Formal buddy relationship for 90 days. Informal relationship continues indefinitely.
- Buddy selection criteria: At least 6 months on the team, strong communication skills, willingness to help, not the new hire's manager
- Key principle: The buddy's job is to be available, not to have all the answers. "I don't know, let's find out together" is a perfectly good buddy response.

## Core viewpoints

### 1. The buddy is a safety net, not a crutch

The buddy exists so the new hire can take risks — ask questions, try things, make mistakes — knowing someone has their back. The goal is not dependency but confidence. By day 90, the new hire should need the buddy rarely, not daily.

### 2. Buddy and manager have different roles

The manager sets expectations, evaluates performance, and makes employment decisions. The buddy provides technical guidance, cultural orientation, and a judgment-free zone. Mixing these roles is the most common buddy system failure — the new hire won't be honest about struggles if the buddy is also evaluating them.

### 3. Being a buddy is a skill, not just availability

Good buddies: ask questions rather than giving answers ("What have you tried?"), normalize struggle ("I was confused by this too"), and connect the new hire to other people ("You should talk to Sarah about this"). Bad buddies: give answers without context, minimize concerns, and act as the sole information source.

### 4. The buddy relationship should be structured, not ad-hoc

Without structure, buddy relationships drift. Daily check-ins in week 1, every-other-day in week 2, weekly thereafter. Each check-in has a simple agenda: what did you work on, what went well, what was confusing, what's next. The structure provides psychological safety — the new hire always knows when they'll next talk to their buddy.

## Key info

### Buddy responsibilities by phase

**Week 1 (Daily check-ins, 15 min each):**
- [ ] Day 1: Welcome, environment setup, team introduction
- [ ] Day 2: Codebase tour, product walkthrough, answer questions
- [ ] Day 3: First PR review, explain code review norms
- [ ] Day 4: Deep-dive into one system, explain architecture
- [ ] Day 5: Week 1 retro, plan week 2

**Weeks 2-4 (Every-other-day check-ins, 15 min each):**
- [ ] Review PRs promptly (within 4 hours)
- [ ] Explain team processes and unwritten rules
- [ ] Introduce to other team members and stakeholders
- [ ] Help navigate organizational complexity
- [ ] Flag any concerns to manager (with new hire's knowledge)

**Months 2-3 (Weekly check-ins, 30 min each):**
- [ ] Weekly 1:1 on progress, blockers, and questions
- [ ] Review larger PRs and design decisions
- [ ] Help with 30-60-90 day goal calibration
- [ ] Encourage independence — "who else could you ask about this?"
- [ ] Prepare for buddy relationship transition at day 90

### Buddy selection criteria

| Criterion | Why |
|---|---|
| 6+ months on team | Knows the codebase, processes, and people well enough to guide |
| Not the manager | Separation of support and evaluation |
| Strong communication | Can explain complex topics clearly and patiently |
| Available | Has bandwidth for daily check-ins in week 1 |
| Voluntarily participating | Wants to help, not assigned against their will |

### Buddy conversation template

```markdown
**Week 1 daily check-in:**
1. What did you work on yesterday?
2. What went well?
3. What was confusing or frustrating?
4. What are you working on today?
5. What questions do you have?
6. Is there anything you need from me?
```

## Action recommendations

1. **Assign the buddy before the new hire's first day**: The buddy should reach out with a welcome message before day 1. This starts the relationship before the new hire feels lost.
2. **Give buddies training, not just responsibility**: A 30-minute session on how to be a good buddy (ask don't tell, normalize struggle, connect to others) pays off massively.
3. **Schedule all week-1 check-ins on day 1**: Put them on the calendar. Don't leave them to chance. Structure provides safety.
4. **Rotate buddy assignments**: Don't make the same person buddy for every new hire. Spread the responsibility and prevent buddy burnout.
5. **Recognize buddy contributions**: Being a good buddy is real work. Acknowledge it in performance reviews and team retrospectives.

## Anti-patterns

- **Manager as buddy**: The manager evaluates performance. The buddy provides judgment-free support. Mixing these roles destroys trust.
- **Buddy as sole information source**: The buddy becomes the only person the new hire talks to. The buddy should actively connect the new hire to others.
- **No structure**: "Let me know if you have questions" with no scheduled check-ins. New hires don't know what they don't know.
- **Burned-out buddy**: The same person is buddy for every new hire. Rotate. Being a buddy is rewarding but draining.
- **Buddy as gatekeeper**: The buddy filters all information and introductions. The buddy should open doors, not guard them.
- **Ghosting after week 1**: Daily check-ins in week 1, then radio silence. The relationship should taper gradually, not drop off a cliff.
