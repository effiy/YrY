<p>I often tell people to think of VPNs like kitchen funnels. Instead of pouring your network traffic directly into the web and spilling your metadata everywhere, you push it down a narrow tube of encryption. You point your connection exactly where it needs to go without leaking all over the place.&nbsp;Of course, the assumption in such an arrangement is that you <em>trust</em> your VPN, which controls the flow of traffic from your device to the web. But if the VPN itself is a honeypot for attackers to pry away your data, you&rsquo;ve just traded several possible security risks for one guaranteed security breach.&nbsp;</p><p>According to the team at <a href="https://socket.dev/blog/chrome-vpn-extension-impersonation" rel="noopener" target="_blank" title="open in a new window"><u>Socket</u></a>, that&rsquo;s what&rsquo;s been happening with over seven hundred fake VPN apps listed on the Chrome Web Store. Many of these extensions are free, some are paid, and some even pretend to be from trusted cybersecurity providers to lure users into handing over unrestricted access to their network and browser.&nbsp;This weekend, I dug through Socket&rsquo;s report to find out exactly what was going on with these apps and how they managed to clear Web Store moderation without getting flagged by Google. I&rsquo;ll share what I found and offer some tips to help you avoid these fake VPNs.&nbsp;</p><h2 id="what-socket-found-when-it-analyzed-hundreds-of-suspicious-chrome-extensions">What Socket found when it analyzed hundreds of suspicious Chrome extensions</h2><p>In short, Socket&rsquo;s threat research team analyzed 737 suspicious Chrome extensions that claim to offer VPN and SOCKS5 proxy servers to protect your online privacy. They uncovered paid apps selling access to VPN servers that do not exist, extensions that hijack your proxy to track online activity, and several attempts to impersonate trusted VPN service providers like NordVPN and Surfshark.</p><p>Socket is a cybersecurity platform with its own GitHub extension, firewall, and CLI to help developers analyze AI-written code for malicious behavior. When they looked into these Chrome extensions, they found that they were all published by a total of 40 developer accounts and had racked up 75,486 installs from users on the Chrome Web Store.&nbsp;</p><p>Of the 737 suspicious extensions they analyzed, Socket&rsquo;s team performed a detailed analysis of the code in 525 of them. In total, these extensions amounted to 58,318 active Chrome installs at the time. As for the other 212, they were already taken off the Web Store before Socket could take a look at them. But the researchers found a number of issues right away in the extensions they did analyze, including:&nbsp;</p><ul><li><p>274 out of 525 extensions plagiarised the branding and logo of one among 66 reputed VPN platforms, including Proton VPN, Surfshark, NordVPN, ExpressVPN, CyberGhost, and TunnelBear.&nbsp;</p></li><li><p>Two extensions specifically impersonated AmneziaVPN and AntiZapret, which are often used to get around internet censorship and surveillance.&nbsp;</p></li><li><p>Each extension pointed to a fixed SOCKS5 proxy without split tunneling or per-site controls, meaning that all your online activity would get routed through the same server once the extension was installed.&nbsp;</p></li><li><p>104 extensions using documented DNS-over-HTTPS evasion techniques to get around Chrome&rsquo;s blocklists by spoofing their DNS record.&nbsp;</p></li><li><p>Many of these VPNs advertise a paid tier with private VPN servers in Japan, Singapore, Canada, Australia, and Turkey. But these servers do not exist, because their hostnames did not resolve when Socket ran a DNS lookup against them.&nbsp;</p></li><li><p>With the premium VPN subscriptions, there was no license verification happening internally to detect if the user had actually paid for a subscription. This is not normal for any web-based app or platform that offers a paid tier.&nbsp;</p></li><li><p>One of these extensions, Bur&#1105;nka VPN, does not route traffic through any servers at all. It&rsquo;s just a fake UI pretending to be a real app.&nbsp;</p></li><li><p>Another extension contains a file with a plaintext comment that simply reads, &ldquo;If Chrome Web Store rejects this because of automatically opening links, we can replace it with a notification offering to go to the Telegram bot.&rdquo; This shows that the developers know that their app violates Web Store policy, but intend to evade reviews instead of fixing the issue.&nbsp;</p></li></ul><h2 id="how-fake-vpns-got-past-chromes-review-process">How fake VPNs got past Chrome&rsquo;s review process</h2><p>I&rsquo;ve repeatedly found that the best laid cyber attacks aren&rsquo;t necessarily the most sophisticated.&nbsp;</p><p>Just so, none of these extensions are particularly difficult to flag in a thorough code review. They got past Chrome&rsquo;s defenses because they spent a lot of time studying Web Store review processes, possibly learning to evade rejections through repeated trial and error. Many of these developers have already had their other extensions removed by Chrome following a similar report from <a href="https://live.paloaltonetworks.com/t5/community-blogs/browser-threat-brief-fake-vpn-extensions-turn-browser-privacy/ba-p/1255456" rel="noopener" target="_blank" title="open in a new window"><u>Palo Alto Networks</u></a> in June, but they immediately went on to publish new extensions using the same playbook.&nbsp;</p><p>Even on the occasion that a developer&rsquo;s account gets pulled from the store, opening a new developer account on the Chrome Web Store costs $5. Between the 40 developer accounts mentioned in the report, it barely cost $200 to publish over 700 fake extensions that reached an audience of tens of thousands.</p><p>It also helped that most of these VPNs were angled toward Russian citizens trying to bypass regional censorship regulations, so any resulting incidents would not have been widely reported outside that specific target demographic. Perhaps even more unsettlingly, in the few occasions that store moderators did flag an extension for further review, they all shared exactly identical &ldquo;privacy justifications&rdquo; that were little more than false reassurances to get their extensions back up.</p><p>It&rsquo;s easy to dismiss these fake VPNs as a regional threat aimed at Russian internet users only, but you&rsquo;d be wrong. Chrome&rsquo;s Web Store policies paint a troubling pattern of lax security reviews, and the same fake VPN playbook could be adapted to target citizens in the U.S., UK, EU, or anywhere else in the world. In fact, <a href="https://www.independent.co.uk/tech/security/google-security-warning-fake-vpn-app-b2863528.html" rel="noopener" target="_blank" title="open in a new window"><u>Google itself issued a critical warning</u></a> in 2025, speaking of similar fake VPN activity on the Play Store for Android users.&nbsp;</p><h2 id="how-to-spot-a-fake-vpn-browser-extension">How to spot a fake VPN browser extension</h2><p>Since you can&rsquo;t rely on app store review processes alone, you need some way to spot fake extensions before they embed spyware into your browser. You can&rsquo;t be expected to reverse engineer every extension&rsquo;s manifest files like Socket did, but thankfully, you don&rsquo;t have to because there are easier ways to tell if something might be amiss.&nbsp;</p><ul><li><p>Install extensions by following a link directly from the VPN provider&rsquo;s official website, not the search bar on Web Store or Google Play.&nbsp;</p></li><li><p>Always read reviews before installing a new browser extension. If too many people are complaining about security concerns, avoid it.</p></li><li><p>Before you install a Chrome Web Store extension, look past the app listing and user reviews to the developer account tied to that app as well. Check if the account is officially associated with the VPN provider and see if Web Store reviews flag security concerns on their other apps too.&nbsp;</p></li><li><p>Use a tool like <a href="http://WhatIsMyIPAddress.com" rel="noopener" target="_blank" title="open in a new window"><u>WhatIsMyIPAddress.com</u></a> to check your currently visible IP address. Confirm that it matches the IP address displayed in your VPN extension&rsquo;s UI.&nbsp;</p></li><li><p>Run a <a href="https://browserleaks.com/dns" rel="noopener" target="_blank" title="open in a new window"><u>DNS leak check</u></a> while your VPN is on to make sure that it&rsquo;s working properly, not merely concealing your IP address but also encrypting your DNS queries. If either your IP address, general location, or internet service provider (ISP) shows up in the test results, that&rsquo;s a red flag.&nbsp;</p></li></ul>
