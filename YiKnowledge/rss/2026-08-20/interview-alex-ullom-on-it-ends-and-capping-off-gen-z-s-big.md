<p><img alt="Alex Ullom on the Long, Winding Road of It Ends" class="attachment-thumbnail-large size-thumbnail-large wp-post-image" height="480" src="https://www.slantmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/interviews_alexullom-720x480.jpg" width="720" /></p><p>Alex Ullom’s freshman feature <em>It Ends</em> has taken an unconventional, circuitous route to a theatrical release. Following an acclaimed festival bow at 2025’s SXSW, the film lingered without distribution for months before it became available to general audiences—albeit as part of a pilot to launch a TVOD service through Letterboxd. Only after this streaming run did Neon bite and sign on to provide a more traditional rollout.</p>
<p>It’s a fitting journey given the subject matter of <em>It Ends</em>: On a late-night drive, four twentysomething friends—James, Day, Fisher, and Tyler—inadvertently detour onto a continuous stretch of country backroad they cannot escape. Ullom has taken to describing his debut as a “hangout horror,” an apt description for a film that balances the foursome’s shooting the breeze inside a cramped Jeep with keeping the mysterious monsters outside the car at bay.</p>
<p>And now, nearly 18 months after its premiere, the film fortuitously arrives at the tail end of a summer movie season dominated by breakout works by Gen Z filmmakers. While <em>It Ends</em> shares a fascination with liminal spaces with Kane Parsons’s <a href="/film/backrooms-review-chiwetel-ejiofor-renate-reinsve-kane-parsons/"><em>Backrooms</em></a>, it’s also of a piece with Curry Barker’s <a href="/film/obsession-review-curry-barker/"><em>Obsession</em></a>’s spotlight on Zoomer anxieties. The film’s <em>Twilight Zone</em>-esque premise renders tangible the more existential dread faced by the group of recent graduates as they embark on an uncharted journey toward an uncertain future.</p>
<p>I spoke with Ullom ahead of the theatrical release of <em>It Ends</em>—and shortly after his follow-up feature, <em>4 x 4: The Event</em>, was announced. Our conversation covered how the film came together through reshoots, why he wants to pick up with the four characters again when they turn 40, and what he makes of the comparisons to the year’s breakout horror films directed by Gen Zers.</p>


<p><strong>Do you have a preference for Alex or Alexander? I’ve seen it written both ways.</strong></p>
<p>This is so stupid, but I’d say that Alex directed <em>It Ends</em> and Alexander directed my next one. </p>
<p><strong>You’ve had a very unusual trajectory with this film’s release. I know you don’t have a baseline to compare it to, but what has this experience been like?</strong></p>
<p>The only noticeable real-life difference is that for the last two years I lived in a walk-in closet, and now I have my own place. That’s the only physical, mental, and emotional difference. Everything else has just been trying to make movies. I’ve tried to release [myself from] what happens to them. I’m very grateful, and the movie only works because of the people that supported it and the performances. But for me, specifically, it’s been a bit of a scrap in the mud.</p>
<p><strong>While making <em>It Ends</em>, did you consciously think about the story being emblematic of the concerns of people your age?</strong></p>
<p>I was just a dumb 22-year-old kid when I first started the process, and I’m 28 now, but he was obviously pulling from…I say “he” as if I’m in the third person!</p>

<p><strong>Alex directed <em>It Ends</em>, and Alexander is now directing <em>4 x 4: The Event</em>! I think you created the ability to talk in third person here.</strong></p>
<p>He was pulling from a very physical and physiological place from his suffering, and it just so happens that’s also more of a universal experience than I realized as I got older. But a lot of the things were more structural, and the comparisons to <em>Backrooms</em> and liminal spaces is just being the fact [there’s no set direction as to where] Gen Z going. The structures that used to hold [people] together, whether it was the nuclear family, religion, or housing, are gone, so there’s this comfort in a transitionary state because it’s become the state that we exist in forever.</p>
<p>However, comparisons to <em>Backrooms</em> and <em>Obsession</em> are ridiculous. Kane is a good friend, and he honed his craft for years on YouTube. That was his <em>It Ends</em>. Curry’s first film, <em>Milk and Cereal</em>, is one that he scrapped together over six years. There’s a trial-and-error period, and you’re learning about what does and doesn’t work. That was <em>It Ends</em> for me. I’m so grateful that it’s coming out in theaters; it’s insane. It wasn’t made for a market like <em>Backrooms</em> and <em>Obsession</em> were. I think that the only real comparison between [them] is just that there are some Gen Z sensibilities that are baked into all three of us just going through growing up like this.</p>


<p><strong>Given that you made <em>It Ends</em> once and ended up reshooting it almost entirely, I’m curious to know about how you see its evolution.</strong></p>
<p>To be honest, I fucked it up the first time. But because of the quality of performances and the love of the crew, we knew we could make it better. I got together about 20 minutes of it, and I hit up one of our original investors, who was a magician. He and a YouTuber friend put up a little bit more money, and we were able to fix it. It just continuously got better—to a point where it was easier to raise money after because it was so clear what it needed. We would go to bars in L.A. and just bring strangers to watch the movie out of our closet that I was living in. They wouldn’t go in the closet, but basically, we could feel what parts of the movie sucked and which didn’t. It [helped] us be honest that this needs to be a tight 90 [minutes].</p>
<p></p>
<p><strong>Is figuring out how to make the inside of a car visually interesting something that had to develop over time? There are only so many ways one can shoot such a compact space, and it was remarkable how much I didn’t feel like I was just sitting in this car getting bored out of my mind.</strong></p>
<p>Well, I appreciate that you said it worked for you. In the first half of the film, there are like three times as many cuts. Whether it was for resource or scope reasons, the genre half of the movie in the front is shot like a typical thriller with set pieces. Then, the visual language changes as the characters emotionally change. Eventually, once they’re just locked into the road, we’re not even in handheld anymore. We just have the camera locked in the car when they’re making the memes and stuff. I was trying to change the visual rhythm enough to keep it interesting for long enough. I tried to shoot each chapter with a different approach.</p>

<p><strong>How did you approach phones and technology in the film? So often, they just function like a black hole for visual ingenuity or narrative movement in film, but you capture something really unique about how they can provide conduits for conversation through things like those memes.</strong></p>
<p>What’s crazy is that I never even considered the idea that that’s an absurd thing. I’ve had dreams where I’m on my phone, which is pretty pathetic! Phones are such a part of reality at this point that, of course, they would be in there and be used as a topic of conversation.</p>
<p><strong>Watching the film a second time, I was struck by the way you use both montage to elide passage of time and extended scenes where we feel it more acutely. How did you come to understand the way you’d need to manipulate time?</strong></p>
<p>That was something that we had to learn through screening cuts of the movie a million times and being like, “Okay, it drags here, but wait, we need the film to drag so that you can feel stuck in the liminal-ness, but also, I don’t want to be boring.” I hope I found the healthy balance!</p>

<p><strong>I saw <em>It Ends</em> last year at the “Scary Movies” series at Film at Lincoln Center, which I think set me up to interpret this first as a genre film. There are horror elements in the zombie-like people that swarm the car in the first 15 minutes, but those largely fade into the background as the drive continues. How did you think about incorporating this supernatural element into the story?</strong></p>
<p>I get a lot of questions about the people. There’s absolutely a higher power on the road, and the characters are absolutely reckoning with something supernatural. They could never learn what it is, but once they run into another car, they realize, “Oh, those people in the woods are just like us. What makes us different than them?” You can start to ease off the physical threats and become more psychological, which is where the film ends up going.</p>

<p><strong>I don’t think of <em>It Ends</em> as a film that we’re meant to solve as an audience&#8230;</strong></p>
<p>Thank you! It’s definitely not. I get it a lot, which is weird because I don’t see anyone asking who made the <em>Backrooms</em> and why. I do want to say that I find it ironic that a lot of people end up in the same emotional place as James [Phinehas Yoon], which is that they don’t get an answer for this. I would love if people who felt very fucked up by that decided to pull their own meaning from the movie. What James goes through is that there’s no answer to this, so what do I do? The answer is what part of the movie actually resonated with you—unless you don’t like the characters, in which case, the movie’s fucked regardless.</p>
<p><strong>But obviously there are rules and design to this time loop, so what ground rules for the mechanics and practicalities did you consider?</strong></p>
<p>One of my favorite parts of the movie is [when the characters realize] that reality is off, and it’s just them starting to recognize the different elements of basic physics and reality being broken. I feel like I’m still looking for that in real life, like&#8230;is this the moment where I stare at a lamp too long and drop out of my life into a dream?</p>

<p><strong>You’ve mentioned Richard Linklater as an inspiration for this film’s “hangout horror,” and even suggested that maybe you’d want to pick up with these characters again in a few years like the <em>Before</em> series. What would that look like?</strong></p>
<p>Well, it would be different because the <em>Before</em> films are obviously romances. I just think it’d be really cool, since <em>It Ends</em> is about the transition into adulthood, and then you check back in when the characters are 40. Then, it’s like, “How do we feel about transitioning into dealing with real mortality?” I would change the rules, but I would want to see how our generation has aged into 40. Unless we’re scavenging for water and in some sort of revolutionary war. But if movies still exist, I assume that society does [well] enough for me to see how Gen Z is doing.</p>

<p><strong><em>It Ends</em> doesn’t have a real setting per se, but it does feel inspired by your home state of Florida. How did the environment of your upbringing inspire your work?</strong></p>
<p>I see Florida as the Australia of the United States. I knew I was gonna get shit because the movie does look like a college brochure [with] an Asian [lead and an ethnically diverse supporting cast], but that was what Florida felt like when I grew up. I was James, and these were the racial demographics. It was a very specific melting pot, and I was grateful for that experience.</p>

<p><strong>I understand you’ve just wrapped your sophomore feature. While a lot of the learning process on <em>It Ends</em> sounds like it involved a trial by fire and learning what mistakes not to repeat, were there elements of the magic from your debut you were consciously trying to recapture? I know you have one cast member in both…</strong></p>
<p>Mitch [Cole, who plays Tyler in <em>It Ends</em>], fuck yeah! Speaking of Florida, he literally was an HVAC repairman. That’s not a joke in the movie. This guy was tiling roofs and fixing AC units. It’s awesome, and now, he’s gonna be in front of a microphone!</p>
<p><strong>This isn’t to cast aspersions on the rest of your cast, but he is a very specific type of person from the South who I don’t see on screen very often.</strong></p>
<p>Definitely. I think I learned every single possible filmmaking lesson you can learn on <em>It Ends</em>, and I brought all of that with me to <em>4 x 4</em>. But I didn’t have to wrap the grip truck on <em>4 x 4</em>. It was a much physically lighter experience, but mentally, I was in it a lot more. The stakes felt a lot higher, so I had to get sleeping medication.</p>
