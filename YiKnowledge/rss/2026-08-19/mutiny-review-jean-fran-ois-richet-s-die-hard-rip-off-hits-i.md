<p><img alt="Mutiny" class="attachment-thumbnail-large size-thumbnail-large wp-post-image" height="480" src="https://www.slantmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/mutiny-720x480.jpg" width="720" /></p><p>With 2023’s <em>Plane</em> and, now, <em>Mutiny</em>, Jean-François Richet has positioned himself as a go-to director for sturdy but mostly unremarkable high-concept action films set largely in a single confined location, and with a stone-faced hero at their center. Both films deliver enough lean, mean action to almost distract from the fact that they’re two in a long line of <a href="/film/appreciation-die-hard/"><em>Die Hard</em></a> rip-offs with little of that classic’s wit, verve, or charm.</p>
<p>Given its brevity, <em>Mutiny</em> can nearly be forgiven for its disinterest in everything from logic to world-building. Indeed, its sole purpose is to get Jason Statham’s Cole Reed on a cargo ship so he can get his revenge on those who murdered his billionaire boss, clear his name in connection with the man’s death, and rescue the few dozen sex-trafficked women and children he discovers on board. At that, the film succeeds, serving up some solidly choreographed tight-quarters fight scenes with enough tactility to somewhat compensate for the weightlessness of the script.</p>

<p>Whatever fun the film offers, though, is frequently dampened by the pedestrian dialogue and a slew of interchangeable, vaguely European baddies. Cole’s eventual sidekick—the ship’s third mate, Angie (Annabelle Wallis)—occasionally gets to join in on the ass-kicking, but she’s too often presented as the damsel in distress to register as his equal. Of course, as with most Statham joints, he’s unquestionably the anointed hero, wronged or not.</p>

<p><em>Mutiny</em> is effective when it leans fully into the cat-and-mouse chase between Cole and the ship’s evil captain (Roland Møller), who sends a seemingly endless line of henchmen after him. Richet and cinematographer Brendan Galvin make clever use of the ship’s cramped spaces and narrow corridors, making each successive fight hit that much harder.</p>

<p>In <em>Mutiny</em>’s final act, things really veer into absurdity with an extended fight between a man wielding a giant axe and another armed with a small knife, and not much later a harpoon enters the mix for one particularly memorable kill. It’s clear the filmmakers know how to be cheeky and over-the-top with a painfully familiar premise. It’s just a shame that we didn’t get any of that delightfully madcap energy until the final 20 minutes of the film.</p>
