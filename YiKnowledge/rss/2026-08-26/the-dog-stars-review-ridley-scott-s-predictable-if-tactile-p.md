<p><img alt="The Dog Stars" class="attachment-thumbnail-large size-thumbnail-large wp-post-image" height="480" src="https://www.slantmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/dogstars-720x480.jpg" width="720" /></p><p>One imagines that Ridley Scott, who collaborated with Cormac McCarthy on 2013’s <a href="/film/the-counselor/"><em>The Counselor</em></a>, would have liked to adapt <a href="/film/the-road/"><em>The Road</em></a> for the screen. With <em>The Dog Stars</em>, Scott has at least gotten ahold of the airport-bookstore equivalent of McCarthy’s seminal, Pulitzer-winning post-apocalyptic novel, and the result, if not quite a return to form for a director for whom big-budget filmmaking has come to seem like a wellness routine, is at least a return to baseline competency after the gilded train wreck that was <a href="/film/gladiator-ii-review-paul-mescal-pedro-pascal-ridley-scott/"><em>Gladiator II</em></a>. It also allows Sir Ridley to check off another subtype of speculative fiction filmmaking from his bucket list.</p>
<p>Adapted by Mark L. Smith from Peter Heller’s 2012 novel, the film takes place several years after the majority of the human race’s extinction in an influenza pandemic. Doe-eyed flyboy Hig (Jacob Elordi) buzzes about the majestic hills and overgrown ruins of Colorado in his vintage light aircraft with his trusty hound Jasper. When he isn’t sleeping under the stars, Hig is scavenging supplies from abandoned stores, trading goods with Mennonites, and (reluctantly) pumping the occasional rival scavenger with inferior personal hygiene habits full of lead.</p>

<p>At an airfield, Hig maintains a home base with Bangley (Josh Brolin), a grizzled military type who was clearly ready for the post-apocalyptic lifestyle long before it found him. The irascible, paranoid Bangley wants for nothing, living off the grid and sniping trespassers on sight, while Hig is tormented by visions of his wife (Sai Bennett)—barefoot, pregnant, and dead—pushing him to either rebuild civilization or fly his plane into a mountainside. Choosing the former, Hig embarks on a journey to Grand Junction Regional Airport, the source of a mysterious radio transmission, making contact along the way with Pops (Guy Pearce) and his lonely single daughter, Cima (Margaret Qualley)—and rapacious cannibal tribes.</p>

<p><em>The Dog Stars</em> has hardly an original idea or image to set it apart from its neo-western brethren: <em>The Road</em>, <a href="/tv/the-walking-dead-season-one/"><em>The Walking Dead</em></a>, <a href="/tv/the-last-of-us-season-two-review-pedro-pascal-bella-ramsey/"><em>The Last of Us</em></a>, and any number of other color-desaturated stories about soulful, self-sufficient American tough guys wandering the nation’s post-civilization wilds, grappling with the mighty burden of responsibility to family and society. But it does have the ultra-crisp digital clarity and workmanlike efficiency of late-period Scott to help that death-drive-garnished comfort food go down smooth.</p>

<p>Scott, rarely one for sentiment even at his most inspired, can hardly be bothered to invest himself in the thin melodrama of Hig’s struggles with intimacy that undergird the script. To this end, we get some drippy stares from Elordi and lilting Southwestern instrumentals from Harry Gregson-Williams before the film seems to call it a day on that front. Nor is Scott willing to hold any shot long enough to establish a grounded sense of time or space, or even just to show off the film’s gorgeous location settings and lavishly desolate production design. </p>
<p>Apart from the sets, Scott’s hand is most evident in the particulars of craft and process glimpsed around the story’s peripheries, from Bangley smelting bullets from spent rifle cartridges to Hig and Cima building a woodland airstrip with nothing but farming equipment and a mule. Scott’s flair for tactile world-building is most excitingly felt in the tense, elaborate, and bloody battle sequences with hostile survivors, one of them intercutting hectic nighttime wide shots with claustrophobic first-person glimpses through a pair of night-vision goggles.</p>


<p>The battles, though, in their aestheticization of hooting, tattooed, primitive hordes falling in waves before rifle-savvy white warriors, disturbingly recall the imagery of early westerns, bringing unusually close to the surface the reactionary fantasies embedded in these post-American visions of rugged men on the new frontier. “We’re living the American fuckin’ dream!” Bangley exclaims at one point, only semi-sarcastically, about his resource-hoarding lifestyle with no government, no vaccines, no neighbors, and loaded guns ready for any stranger in his midst. </p>
<p>Bangley isn’t one for religion, but real-life Bangleys may be delighted to know that the only semblances of “civilization” in the film take the form of Christian communes, their members’ level of moral fortitude aesthetically correlated with their adherence to traditional gender roles. <em>The Dog Stars</em>, clearly made with post-Covid America on the brain, pits Hig’s yearning for connection against Bangley’s bitter lust for isolation but largely elides serious contemplation of its well-worn themes in favor of base genre thrills and perfunctory romance.</p>
