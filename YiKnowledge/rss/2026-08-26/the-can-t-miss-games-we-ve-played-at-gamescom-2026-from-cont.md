<h2>Mega Man: Dual Override</h2><p><em>By Kyle Hilliard</em></p><p>The first thing I learned playing what is ostensibly Mega Man 12 is that the game is called "Override" and not "Overdrive." A crucial distinction. The other thing I learned is that the game feels great and is optimistically a worthwhile follow-up to 2018's Mega Man 11. I played through three stages leading up to a boss fight with Twinkle Girl. The biggest shift from Mega Man 11 is the Dual Override mode which activates when your health is low and gives you some extra fire power after a flashy (but skippable) cutscene. It feels a bit like a last chance, but doesn't radically give you the edge in the fight. Your health is still low, after all. But emotionally, it feels like a final chance at success in a challenging game, so I liked its implementation. Look out for a larger hands-on preview with more details in the coming days.</p><p class="toc-anchor">Control Resonant</p>





  
          
                  
              
              
                  <img alt="Game Informer" height="450" src="https://gameinformer.com/sites/default/files/styles/body_default/public/2026/06/04/05862361/control-resonant-sgf-2026-6.jpg.webp" width="800" />

  


  
  
  


<h2>Control Resonant</h2><p><em>By Wesley LeBlanc</em></p><p>Let me say right off the bat: I am stoked for Control Resonant as a Remedy-verse fan, but I still have not played the game. Unfortunately, that hasn't changed at Gamescom 2026, because my preview of a new section of Control Resonant was hands-off. This didn't inspire confidence in me, especially as Control Resonant marks a big departure for the studio with its melee action combat and RPG elements. However, what I watched the Remedy Entertainment employee play was another exciting look at the game.&nbsp;</p><p>My colleague and senior associate editor <a href="https://gameinformer.com/preview/2026/06/05/hands-on-with-remedys-surreal-take-on-stylish-action">Marcus Stewart did go hands-on with it at SGF</a> and came away with positive thoughts about actually playing the game, so I do still have hope, but alas. Okay, that's enough about that – here's what I actually saw.&nbsp;</p><p>Dylan Faden, the brother of Control protagonist Jesse, is out of the FBC's containment alongside all of the Hiss the Oldest House tried to keep at bay. There have been plenty of looks at the Hiss' role in Control Resonant, but for this hands-off preview, Remedy showed off a Manhattan park area covered in a blue mold, complete with various mold enemies, too. Dylan is tasked with finding a Resonant here – the big bosses of the game.&nbsp;</p><p>Curiously, Remedy says you will fight the same first and last boss no matter how you play the game, but that all the Resonants you fight in between can be tackled in any order you choose. In this preview, we're after a moldy Resonant boss, but first, we have to eat some mold to learn more about it.&nbsp;</p><p>If you didn't already know, the mold in Control Resonant is "delicious," according to Remedy, and hard to resist. As such, Dylan passes all kinds of bodies who look dead but are actually paralyzed in a euphoric state (because they've willingly eaten the mold). En route to the mold Dylan needs to eat, we pass an FBC Field Content Research facility before shifting gravity so we can climb up a vertical wall. &nbsp;</p><p>Up ahead, there's a group of mold enemies. The standard mob enemies are easy to take out, but they're great for farming Dominance, an effect activated by Dylan's combat kit that increases damage after killing an enemy. After garnering some Dominance, we tackle the massive garlic bulb-looking enemy skulking about, placing mold mines in the area. This enemy is about the size of an elephant and shoots mold mines and other projectiles out of its "trunk," for lack of a better term.&nbsp;</p><p>Here, Remedy explains that the equipped Dual Blades have a critical chance buff, and that the drill transformation for his transforming Aberrant weapon does some serious Falter damage. Every enemy has a yellow Falter bar below their red health bar – Falter is essentially a Stagger meter. When you fill it up, you can perform an execution by pressing L3 (on PlayStation 5).&nbsp;</p><p>After taking this enemy out and clearing out the others, we make our way toward the mold Dylan needs to eat. On the way, we see a traffic barrier behaving weirdly, rotating in a glitchy fashion; Remedy says this indicates a nearby world event, but we don't have time to complete it.</p><p>At this point, I ask Remedy if there are real-world locations from Manhattan in Control Resonant. They say no, and that instead of actual named locations, the places within Control Resonant are meant to feel inspired by the real Manhattan, but with a "twisted" Remedy spin on it. We continue forward, fight some additional enemies, and I see Dylan's shoulder button abilities on display; R2 deals fire damage by way of Ignite, while L2 uses the Barrage ability, which pummels enemies with rocks to greatly increase Falter damage. R1 is Push, and it's basically a Jedi force push.&nbsp;</p><p>I also learn here that there is no parry and no block – there's just a dodge (and you can perfect dodge to slow down time and deal extra damage). Remedy wants Control Resonant players to be aggressive; that's why you can only heal by defeating enemies, as they drop blue orbs that add health to your HP bar.&nbsp;</p><p>A few minutes later and we finally reach the mold for Dylan to eat. He eats it, and we get some strange dialogue choices to select. Remedy says dialogue choices won't change the game's ending – there's only one ending – but that some side quests can have different endings depending on your choices. Plus, your dialogue choices will change how NPCs perceive you. It's not a Mass Effect-like system or anything like that, but Remedy says you will notice NPC perception in their attitude toward you.&nbsp;</p><p>After eating this mold, Dylan has an idea of where the Resonant he's after is hiding. We find it underground; it's called the Collective, and it's a giant pile of mold shaped into various frowning faces. During this boss fight, Dylan can't do any damage to the Collective. Instead, you have to attack the Collective's tentacles attached to the walls in the arena. To do that, you need to gravity shift so that you can walk on the walls and hit the tentacles. All the while, the Collective is firing moldy projectiles at you and summoning enemies to slow you down. After damaging all of the tentacles, weak spots open up, and you finally do some actual damage to the Collective.&nbsp;</p><p>I don't get to see the fight's conclusion, but I'm excited to do so myself in the final game. It's not the wildest boss, but seeing Remedy's spin on the "attack weak points to defeat the boss" formula is neat. I expect a lot of weird and strange when I play a Remedy game and so far, Control Resonant seems like it's going to deliver all that and more.&nbsp;</p><p>Control Resonant launches digitally on PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, and PC on September 24. The physical version is planned for October, and a Mac version will launch later.&nbsp;</p><p class="toc-anchor">Nekome: Nazi Hunter</p>

<h2>Nekome: Nazi Hunter</h2><p><em>By Wesley LeBlanc</em></p><p>Earlier this year, I discovered a trailer for Nekome: Nazi Hunter, which is how I learned it's <a href="https://gameinformer.com/2026/02/04/nekome-nazi-hunter-is-a-grindhouse-inspired-action-game-where-you-brutally-kill-a-lot-of">a Grindhouse-inspired action game where you brutally kill a lot of Nazis</a>. Immediately, I was in. And now, after playing it for roughly 30 minutes, I'm even more in.&nbsp;</p><p>Nekome: Nazi Hunter is absolutely, deliciously, disrespectful to the Nazi's of the 1940s Nazi regime in Germany during World War II. It begins with protagonist Vano Nastasu dressed as a butler within a palace of sorts. Inside, a group of Nazis is seated around a table covered in delicious food. As the Nazis eat, a leader figure praises the group for opening up three more death camps, gloating about their awful acts of violence while eating chicken, vegetables, and more. Vano wheels in a silver platter, removing its cover to reveal a decapitated Nazi head with a knife through its skull.&nbsp;</p><p>He then uses a knife engraved with "Nekome," which is Yiddish for vengeance, according to the game, to completely slice off the leader's arm. He then guts his belly before slitting the Nazi's throat. At this point, I take over on the sticks, pressing X repeatedly to deliver all manner of cuts, slashes, and more to the other Nazis in the room – they stand no chance, and I quickly paint the ballroom red.&nbsp;</p>





  
          
                  
              
              
                  <img alt="Game Informer" height="450" src="https://gameinformer.com/sites/default/files/styles/body_default/public/2026/02/04/0068cfd7/nekome_promo.jpg.webp" width="800" />

  


  
  
  


<p>You can parry with Y and break their blocks, but ultimately, combat is simple, more focused on delivering bloodshed than intricacy. What I play is in a pre-beta stage, and it's evident – it's a bit janky, but not enough to distract me from the delight I take part in as I rip apart Nazis galore.&nbsp;</p><p>From here, we flash back to Vano as a child to learn that his entire family is ripped from him at the hands of "Hitler's Hyenas," which is seemingly led by the Nazi leader Vano kills at the preview's start. I'm glad he gets his revenge, because that leader shoots Vano's father point-blank, then his mother, then forces Vano and his brother to fight over a knife to kill each other, reminiscent of the pool stick scene in <em>The Dark Knight</em>. Vano refuses to kill his brother and stabs himself instead. The aforementioned Nazi leader shoots Vano in the back and doesn't check to make sure he's dead.&nbsp;</p><p>I flash forward again to see Vano in the future, somewhere in New York City. He's speaking with Harold, a butcher-mentor with a heavy NY accent. He explains to Vano the importance of training before exacting revenge, but Nazis soon storm the room and take Harold out, adding more fuel to Vano's vengeance-filled journey. Searching for Harold's killer, I stalk through a NYC alley to learn there's an American-German Bund group in support of Nazis in 1940s America. I utilize the game's stealth kills here and like the action combat, it's simple (but effective). Turns out, killing Nazis just doesn't get old, at least in this 30-minute preview.&nbsp;</p><p>I hope to continue my Nazi-killing delight in the full game when it launches sometime early next year.&nbsp;</p><p class="toc-anchor">Crimson Moon</p>

<h2>Crimson Moon</h2><p><em>By Wesley LeBlanc</em></p><p>Crimson Moon is developer/publisher ProbablyMonsters' take on the Soulslike, but instead of aiming for a massive, grandiose FromSoftware-style game, it's aiming for a smaller experience; it's more focused on replayability, especially with a co-op buddy. That's why the game is only $19.99. That said, the 30 minutes I played reflect the game's more scaled-down approach and smaller price of entry.&nbsp;</p><p>The game's combat feels reminiscent of other Soulslikes, with light and heavy attacks, blocking, parrying, and dodging. You also have special abilities you can tap into to do heavy damage, and an ultimate-like transformation that turns your Crimson Moon acolyte knight into an angelic being with a huge halberd that hits hard. Despite these similar trappings, the combat looks and feels like something more akin to Kingdom of Amalur: Reckoning. The "heavy metal" fantasy visual design reflects that too, with a touch of classic World of Warcraft fantasy. This art design is what I walked away most impressed with – it's a nice change of pace from the typical dark and brooding gothic feel of the Soulslike.&nbsp;</p><p>That said, the game's main appeal is the co-op element, and unfortunately, I didn't get to experience any of that. But I do know the co-op party sessions begin from the game's primary hub, and that loot is <em>not </em>shared. If your buddy takes a sword from a chest, it's theirs. ProbablyMonsters teases that online co-op is in the works and that the team will share more in the future. Despite the emphasis on co-op, it feels like a game built around runs in isolated linear missions, so it should work fine solo.&nbsp;</p><p>The game feels janky, but the final build may feel better (though it launches next week, so we'll see). Nonetheless, if you're looking to spend $20 on a Soulslike you can play with your friend, Crimson Moon might be one to keep an eye on.&nbsp;</p><p class="toc-anchor">&nbsp;Kingdom Hearts Collection I~III on Nintendo Switch 2</p>

<h3>Kingdom Hearts Collection I~III on Nintendo Switch 2</h3><p><em>By Wesley LeBlanc</em></p><p>Have you ever played Kingdom Hearts 1, 2, or 3, or any of the various spin-off-but-still-inexplicably-very-important-to-the-story games? <h3>Cosmo Tales</h3><p><em>By Wesley LeBlanc</em></p><p>Cosmo Tales comes from Czech-based Bohemia Interactive, and after playing roughly 20 minutes or so of it, it's a game I'm greatly looking forward to. It launches in Early Access next week, on September 3, before an anticipated Q1/Q2-ish 1.0 launch. The pitch is simple: it's a retrofuturistic 1980s space-age-inspired shooter where you fly around bright and colorful arenas, shooting robots with lasers, rockets, and more.&nbsp;</p><p>It's a roguelite, too, so there are pickups to earn after completing each arena bout, like an electrifying Tesla coil, flame-imbued weapons, and more, and you earn XP at the end of each run to further upgrade your various ships. Each ship is based on a car, with many based on the Czech vehicle manufacturer Skoda. The combat and movement are simple, but that's by design because Bohemia Interactive intends for the game to be children-friendly; the team told me it's aiming for it to be your 10-year-old's first roguelite, and I can definitely see what they're getting at.&nbsp;</p><p>But don't let that deter you – it's a light, breezy, but, crucially, fun time, and it's all backed by a groovy score. It will launch on PC via Early Access next week, but the team hopes to bring it to PlayStation 5 and Nintendo Switch 2 for the 1.0 launch next year.&nbsp;</p><p class="toc-anchor">&nbsp;When Sirens Fall Silent</p>

Florence, Italy-based LKA is aiming to do the same with When Sirens Fall Silent, this time putting you in control of a rookie cop on the outskirts of Rome, investigating mysterious and disturbing murders.&nbsp;</p><p>As the name implies, there is likely something related to Sirens behind these murders, but that's all I can say for now. Its movement and platforming aren't doing anything too different from the aforementioned Little Nightmares formula, but its unique setting spices up what might otherwise be a standard adventure game.&nbsp;</p><p>Plus, your companion is a cute and chunky lil zebra, and it's sure to be the game's fan-favorite character. <h3>Murals</h3><p><em>By Wesley LeBlanc</em></p><p>If you know me, you know I adore Dlala Studios' Disney Illusion Island – <a href="https://gameinformer.com/review/disney-illusion-island/joy-in-simplicity">you can read my review here to learn more about why</a>. Murals is a new action game from Dlala that sees the studio spinning up its own take on classic fairy tales, not unlike what Disney has done with the works of Hans Christian Andersen and more. I'm told there are more fairy tales to play through, but my hands-off demo during Gamescom pulled back the curtain on Dlala's Little Mermaid.&nbsp;</p><p>The humor is very British, the art is warm, inviting, painterly, and beautiful, and the score, made by returning composer David Housden, sounds grand and amazing (and this demo still featured Housden's MIDI tracks, so it's not even the final orchestral arrangements). Unlike Hades, which typically gives you free rein in the various arenas, Murals uses some intentional blocking and environmental hazards to keep consecutive arena bouts feeling different. I don't know if it feels as good as it looks to play, but Dlala has shown prowess in creating characters that feel great to control, so I have high expectations.&nbsp;</p><p>There's a lot more going on in Murals, from charming voice performances to some neat Boon-like pickups that change your combat kit (à la Hades) to different weapons to use and some unique spins on classic fairy tales I look forward to learning more about. <h3>Hellraiser: Revival</h3><p><em>By Wesley LeBlanc</em></p><p>If you told me an upcoming Hellraiser video game adaptation was a first-person shooter, I'd scoff at the idea – it's quite low on my list of game styles I'd imagine a Hellraiser game to be created in. But, after 30 minutes of hands-on time with its latest build, I can't wait for more. It's decrepit, with mangled bodies strewn about what appears to be a luxury apartment home to a deviant cult, and fresh blood and gore painting the walls of its hallways.&nbsp;</p><p>Combat is pretty straightforward; I used a pistol and shotgun (both with limited bullets) to take down leather-strapped-and-gagged enemies as I searched for a way to get through this apartment's various doors. In a fun survival-horror twist, LeMarchand's Box, or the Lament Configuration cube, allows you to grab scissors, knives, and even fire from candles and projectile-throw them at enemies.&nbsp;</p><p>Halfway through the demo, I was brought to the Labyrinth to solve a simple platform-moving puzzle before I landed in a house. &nbsp;In this house, which is clearly a nod to Hideo Kojima's P.T. demo, I move through hallways over and over again, searching for differences and clues to the way forward. It's clear the protagonist is beaten up about Sunny, his girlfriend, falling victim to the torturous cenobites, and he's on a frantic search to learn more about them.&nbsp;</p><p>Hellraiser: Revival launches October 8, and I'm looking forward to playing more of this horror adventure (which will run 10 to 12 hours, according to the developers, making it a perfect game for the Halloween season).&nbsp;</p><p class="toc-anchor">&nbsp;Showa American Story</p>

<h3>Showa American Story</h3><p><em>By Wesley LeBlanc</em></p><p>Based on rumors the game's director remembers hearing in China about Japan's 1980s economic prowess, Showa American Story is an action RPG set in an America that Japan purchased and colonized. As such, Hollywood is now Neo Yokohama, and Chigusa Choko is on a road trip searching for her sister.&nbsp;</p><p>I fight various "Warriors" as I run up roads leading to the mountain home to the Neo Yokohama sign. Combat is most reminiscent of RGG's Yakuza/Like a Dragon series, with a comedic intensity to each hit. In my 30-minute demo, I use a massive BioShock-like drill, a large (and sharp) American flag, a katana, a machine gun, and a shotgun to lay waste to enemies in Tarantino-esque fashion. That checks out, as developer Nekcom Entertainment tells me director Quentin Tarantino's Kill Bill movies are a massive inspiration for the game.&nbsp;</p><p>The combat is brutal, bloody, graphic, and hilarious – it never got old executing enemies by impaling them with an American flag. The world and characters are also hilariously raunchy. I stumble upon a guy who did not pay a sex escort the money she's owed, so the Warriors, a gang of sorts that resides here, smash his "balls" into a tree over and over. After I defeat the goons, Choko can choose to ask how the tree is doing or how the man's "balls" are doing.&nbsp;</p><p>There are plenty more laugh-out-loud moments I experience in this short demo, and you can read more about them in a separate preview I anticipate writing later.&nbsp;</p><p class="toc-anchor">&nbsp;Stranger Than Heaven</p>

<h3>Stranger Than Heaven</h3><p><em>By Wesley LeBlanc</em></p><p>I got to play through three battles, each more difficult than the last, in Yakuza/Like a Dragon developer RGG's upcoming century-spanning action-brawler game, Stranger Than Heaven. I didn't spot Tupac, nor did I get to talk to the team about his inclusion (<a href="https://gameinformer.com/sgf-2026/2026/06/11/rgg-studio-head-talks-tupacs-controversial-stranger-than-heaven-inclusion">though we asked RGG about it back in June</a>), but I did spot plenty of street goons to beat the ever-living crap out of with my fists, a large metal pipe, a knife, and a machete.&nbsp;</p><p>I continue to be impressed with RGG's efforts to do something new with the action-brawler space it has cultivated across multiple Yakuza games. You control the main character's right fist and right leg with the RB/RT shoulder buttons, and his left side with the LB/LT shoulder buttons. You can punch, kick, slam, and more, but crucially, you must also block using the correct side of your body as well. Having to watch an opponent's movements to recognize they're swinging left or right and must be blocked appropriately by that side of my body is thrilling; it adds a rhythm/fighting game vibe to the street brawling RGG is known for, and it rules.&nbsp;</p><p>It's difficult and demanding, but landing multiple blocks in a row to defeat a katana-wielding enemy in the streets of 1943 Osaka was a blast.
