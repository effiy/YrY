<p><img alt="Paranormal Activity" class="attachment-thumbnail-large size-thumbnail-large wp-post-image" height="480" src="https://www.slantmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/paranormalactivity-720x480.jpg" width="720" /></p><p>Now scaring audiences at the August Wilson Theatre, <em>Paranormal Activity</em> is the sort of play you’ll likely to watch with one eyebrow raised and the hairs on your neck standing on end. Though playwright Levi Holloway’s script falls far short of its aspirations of psychological complexity, it’s as terrifying a play as has haunted Broadway in a long while.</p>
<p>For the most part, <em>Paranormal Activity</em> only borrows the broadest outline from the <a href="/film/paranormal-activity/">2007 film</a> that spawned a seven-part franchise when a couple’s home is haunted by a seemingly demonic presence. Sorry, scratch that: As the script hammers home, places aren’t haunted, <em>people</em> are. Occasional specific scares from the film find their way onto the stage (like bedding that moves on its own), but director Felix Barrett, best known for the immersive <a href="/theater/sleep-no-more-haunts-chelsea-warehouse/"><em>Sleep No More</em></a>, hasn’t found a specific theatrical substitute for the film’s defining found-footage aesthetic.</p>

<p>Instead, watching <em>Paranormal Activity</em> on stage, with the entire cluttered house visible at all times, can sometimes feel like a giant, chilling game of <em>Where’s Waldo?</em>, with the next scare feeling as if it could come from anywhere. Every time a character goes to open the refrigerator, pull aside the shower curtain, even lift the toilet seat, there’s a tense anticipation that something could be lying in wait. Every piece of Fly Davis’s jack-in-the-box set is a red herring until it’s not.</p>

<p>Without the cinematic guidance of a camera, <em>Paranormal Activity</em> presents an extra cause for anxiety. Always it feels like you could be looking in the wrong place. But even if it’s possible to miss a few of the smaller visual surprises, the play’s soundscape, designed by Gareth Fry, is the real terrorizer. In a production that sometimes features action in multiple rooms at once, the eye can wander, but the ear can’t. Doorbells have never been as startling as they are here, and for the most part, Anna Watson’s lighting design effectively directs, and misdirects, our attention, as the second floor falls gradually falls into darkness as the play goes on.</p>

<p>James (Travis A. Knight) and Lou (Cher Álvarez) can’t quit going upstairs in their new London home, which seems like an increasingly stupid choice once they realize there’s wickedness afoot in the nursery. But they have more problems to deal with than an angry spirit. Lou’s plagued by ongoing mental health challenges that may be depression stemming from a childhood trauma or may be something more sinister. (Holloway wastes too much time on destigmatizing mental illness when the underlying diagnosable issue at play here may just be a demon.) And James, a mama’s boy with secrets of his own (Shannon Cochran is an especially compelling presence as his über-religious mother), struggles to offer his wife much meaningful sympathy.</p>
<p>To Holloway’s credit, as in the subtler but more bewildering <em>Grey House</em>, the playwright makes an earnest attempt to ground ghostly presence in psychological realism. But since there’s no question what genre we’re in from the beginning, the marital bickering, delivered credibly by Knight and Álvarez, is mainly background noise as the audience braces for the next fright.</p>


<p>Holloway is at his best in punctuating these conversations with deliciously well-timed gotchas. The spirit has a nasty habit of possessing the bodies of other characters, stirring up some shocks that are more lingering than the sudden loud noises. Maybe that’s your wife you’re talking to, or maybe it’s a vengeful omniscient ghost who’s taken on her form. </p>
<p>Throughout the play, Lou keeps complaining about an ever-present dread, and that’s the element that Holloway and Barrett get just right in passing along to the audience. Places aren’t haunted, people are, and, however little you care about James and Lou’s marriage, <em>Paranormal Activity</em>’s abiding creepiness will probably follow you home.</p>
<p><em><a href="https://paranormalbroadway.com/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Paranormal Activity</a> is now running at the August Wilson Theatre.</em></p>
