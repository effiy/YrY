<img src="https://img.pastemagazine.com/wp-content/avuploads/2026/08/26135510/Untitled-design.jpg" /><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">On August 27, 2006, at the 58th Primetime Emmy Awards, Fox&#8217;s </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">24</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> was named Outstanding Drama Series. It was a milestone, first-time win for the Kiefer Sutherland-led thriller; each of its four previous seasons had been nominated in the category, to no avail. With local affiliates itching to throw to the evening news—and the clock ticking on host Conan O’Brien’s “</span><a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=w0FHYgacUzg"><span style="font-weight: 400;">TV legend Bob Newhart will suffocate if the show goes over three hours</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">” bit—</span><a href="https://www.instagram.com/reel/DOmS2YaEsYf/?hl=en"><span style="font-weight: 400;">the acceptance speech</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> was, as co-creator Joel Surnow promised, quick. Executive producer Howard Gordon, coming off his first year as showrunner, wrapped up the remarks with an earnest expression of thanks to </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">24</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">’s cast, crew, and viewers. “We all love doing the show and we’re grateful to everybody who watches the show for giving us that chance.”</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">But the ceremony also proved to be an altogether different kind of milestone: In the 20 years since, no other network show has won what is arguably the Emmys&#8217; top prize. Not once. In 20 years. And the trend shows no sign of ending anytime soon.</span></p><!-- inlinecontent_1 --> </p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">To claim the award in 2006, </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">24</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> had to best NBC&#8217;s </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The West Wing</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> (the Television Academy’s pick for Outstanding Drama Series from 2000 to 2003), HBO&#8217;s </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Sopranos</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> (the 2004 winner), ABC&#8217;s </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Grey&#8217;s Anatomy</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, and Fox&#8217;s </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">House</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">. Two decades on, Gordon tells </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The A.V. Club</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> that heading into that ceremony, there was no expectation to finally emerge victorious, though &#8220;that&#8217;s more a function of protecting yourself.”</span></p><!-- admarker --> 
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;We were super proud of the season, so we felt on the one hand, we had as good a chance as we ever did,&#8221; he says. &#8220;On the other hand, it was the fifth year of a show that quite possibly had seen its best days, or for which the novelty had worn off.&#8221;</span></p><!-- inlinecontent_2 --> </p>
<p><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">24</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8216;s triumphant &#8220;Day 5&#8221; opens with Sutherland&#8217;s Jack Bauer—whose death had been faked in the previous finale—toiling at a Mojave, CA oil refinery under an alias. A series of tragic events brings the former Counter Terrorist Unit boss out of his 18 months of hiding, and pulls him into the hunt for a deadly nerve gas stolen by Russian terrorists. It was the second straight </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">24 </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">season to air all two dozen weekly episodes in a row, uninterrupted—an ambitious yet fitting scheduling gambit for the real-time thriller. It also quickly established itself as </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">24</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8216;s most-watched season ever, opening with north of 17 million viewers and proceeding to average 14 million weekly.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Gordon and company might not have expected that season five would snap </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">24</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8216;s losing streak in the best drama race, but from an Emmy observer’s perspective, it came as little surprise. The well-reviewed season also netted nods for lead actor Sutherland, supporting players Gregory Itzin and Jean Smart, and director Jon Cassar.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">According to Gordon, the season’s “secret weapon” was that one-two punch of Itzin—reprising his &#8220;unique and Nixonian take&#8221; on slithery President Charles Logan—and Smart, whose First Lady Martha Logan purposely evoked Martha Mitchell, the famously outspoken wife of Nixon’s Attorney General, John M. Mitchell.</span></p><!-- admarker --> 
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;The casting gods were smiling on us,&#8221; Gordon says. &#8220;Their chemistry together was extraordinary.&#8221;</span></p><!-- RevContent --> <div id="revcontent-hidden"> <!-- revisit -->
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">It was also a noisy, attention-grabbing season that killed off multiple fan favorites within the first 15 minutes, starting with former president David Palmer, played by original cast member Dennis Haysbert.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;I remember feeling very vividly, every single year, like there&#8217;s no way we could do this again,&#8221; Gordon says. &#8220;Between seasons four and five, I felt it the most, that we had to take a really big swing, and there&#8217;s no bigger swing than the assassination of David Palmer as a starting point—just because of our fondness for the character.&#8221;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Couple that with a tragic death among the series’ core CTU operatives—Jack, Chloe (Mary Lynn Rajskub), Tony (Carlos Bernard), and Michelle (Reiko Aylesworth)— and you had what Gordon calls a &#8220;big reset.&#8221;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">In addition to </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">24</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> finally laying claim to Outstanding Drama, Sutherland and Cassar both won their first Emmys that evening. Gordon says &#8220;a </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">big</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> party&#8221; followed, with the celebratory </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">24</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> team descending upon Spago at one point, and staying out &#8220;for a good part of the night.&#8221;</span></p><!-- admarker --> 
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">But was that celebration the last of its kind? Is part of </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">24</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8216;s legacy to be the </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">final</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> network show ever awarded the Emmy for Outstanding Drama Series? The clock </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">does</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> seem to be running out on Fox and its broadcast rivals. To wit:</span></p><!-- admarker --> 
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">To find an Outstanding Drama Series race in which a </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">majority</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> of nominees aired on network TV, you&#8217;d have to go back 19 years, to when </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Grey&#8217;s</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">House</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, ABC&#8217;s </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Boston Legal</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, and NBC&#8217;s </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Heroes</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> got whacked by </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Sopranos</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">It has been 16 years since broadcast TV fielded </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">multiple</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> nominees in the category; in 2010, ABC&#8217;s </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Lost</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> and CBS&#8217; </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Good Wife</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> were pitted against four cable dramas, ultimately losing to AMC&#8217;s </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Mad Men</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">A network drama hasn’t even mustered a </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">nomination</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> in the category since 2021, when NBC&#8217;s </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">This Is Us</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> went up against five streaming shows plus two cable contenders; Netflix&#8217;s </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Crown</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> wound up reigning.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Of the 40 shows nominated for Outstanding Drama over the past five years, 60% hail from streaming, with cable claiming dibs on the rest. This year, streaming accounts for 75% of nominees.</span></li>
</ul>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;I would not have predicted that there wouldn&#8217;t be another network show [to win since </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">24</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">],&#8221; Gordon says, but the &#8220;bigger budgets,&#8221; &#8220;commercial free-ness,&#8221; and oft-edgier content of cable and streaming fare put broadcasters at a disadvantage, he suggests. Meanwhile, the more voluminous output demanded of a network drama can prove to be a relentless &#8220;high-wire act.&#8221;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;It was like speeding down the highway and you&#8217;re laying down the asphalt as you go,&#8221; he says. &#8220;It forever had that feeling.&#8221;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">As for whether a network drama might ever again make the Emmys&#8217; short list, Gordon says with a shrug, &#8220;I don&#8217;t know.&#8221; In addition to the handicaps mentioned above, he cites a certain &#8220;stigma&#8221; (for lack of a better word) now associated with broadcast offerings.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Gordon goes on to clock that </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Pitt</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">—HBO Max&#8217;s acclaimed, real-time medical drama—not only looks and feels like a network TV show, &#8220;it in many ways resembles </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">24</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> in its pedal-to-the-metal-ness, its fearlessness and relentlessness.&#8221; As such, if </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Pitt</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> or a network-style, high-caliber show akin to it were to call, say, NBC home, &#8220;that&#8217;s a case&#8221; where the decades-long Outstanding Drama streak might be broken.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">But in the TV landscape as it currently exists? The one where even Gordon has moved on to streaming? The showrunner and Emmy-nominated EP for Netflix&#8217;s </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Beast In Me</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> can only marvel. &#8220;We&#8217;re living in interesting times!&#8221;</span></p><!-- inlinecontent_2 --> </p>
