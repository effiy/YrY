<p><img alt="Buddy" class="attachment-thumbnail-large size-thumbnail-large wp-post-image" height="480" src="https://www.slantmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/buddy-720x480.jpg" width="720" /></p><p>Buddy the Unicorn (Keegan-Michael Key) is the star of a <em>Barney &#038; Friends</em>-type show where he frolics around with a bunch of kids, teaching them valuable lessons about sharing and being yourself. But when Buddy starts to feel underappreciated, he charges horn-first into a bloody rampage that sends youngsters Freddy (Delaney Quinn), Oliver (Tristan Broders), and Hannah (Madison Skyy Polan) running for their lives.</p>
<p>The premise for Casper Kelly’s <em>Buddy</em> sounds a bit like a <em>Family Guy</em> cutaway gag, taking something cheerfully wholesome and adding an absurd, sinister twist. It’s easy to see how that could result in a one-joke movie that relies entirely on the initial shock value of a children’s character chasing kids around with an axe. But you can tell from the first glimpse of <em>Buddy</em>’s world that we’re dealing with something much more thoughtfully constructed.</p>

<p>Kelly and company have meticulously recreated the look of ’90s kids’ television in all its vibrant, plasticky, narrow aspect-ratioed glory. They find a devilish humor in the details, like how much of a hassle it is to carry out a murder spree in a world of anthropomorphized objects, since everything from the clock to the mailbox is a potential eyewitness.</p>

<p>The film takes place within the reality of the show itself rather than behind the scenes on a TV production. Freddy and her friends aren’t working actors but prisoners in a strange alternate realm where fixed smiles and saccharine song-and-dance numbers are the order of the day. The inexplicability of that concept is what gives <em>Buddy</em> its most disturbing charge, pushing the events of the film well into the terrain of uncanny horror, homing in on how unsettling the world of a kids’ show looks when viewed through adult eyes.</p>

<p>The film, written by Kelly and Jamie King, also finds a clever way to turn the repetitive structure of children’s TV into a plot mechanic. Each episode of Buddy’s show ends with the kids singing a non-copyright-infringing version of Barney’s “I Love You Song” to him, magically resetting much of the day’s events; that includes healing any injuries Buddy might have sustained and returning escapees to the playhouse. So, once the kids make their initial getaway, it’s a race to see whether they can escape Buddy’s world altogether before he forces one of them to sing.</p>
<p>The thing that saves <em>Buddy</em> from the tired, try-hard edginess of <em>Winnie the Pooh: Blood and Honey</em> and its ilk is that, somewhere beneath all that blood-caked orange fur, it actually has a heart. The kids aren’t just there to serve as apple-cheeked punchlines to dark jokes and victims of gleeful violence. The film genuinely seems to like them, and it wants us to like them too. Which is easy to do since the young cast is charming and there’s a sincere sweetness to their journey together, as they get out from under Buddy’s watchful eye and discover the joys of all the things you’re not allowed to do when you’re trapped in a TV-Y series. Simple things, like swearing, peeing outside, and talking about how much they hate that goddamn unicorn.</p>
