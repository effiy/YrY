<img src="https://img.pastemagazine.com/wp-content/avuploads/2026/08/26114611/MixCollage-26-Aug-2026-11-44-AM-4399.jpg" /><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Tim Curry has died. A veteran stage, screen, and voice actor much beloved for his iconic baritone purr and finely-honed sense of sardonic glee, Curry lent his talents to everything from children’s fare like </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Home Alone 2 </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">and </span><a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=K6089Z_NgPk"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Worst Witch</span></i></a><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">to hallmarks of cinematic sexual subversion—as with his very first film role, </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Rocky Horror Picture Show</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, in 1975. <a href="https://www.tmz.com/2026/08/26/tim-curry-dead/">TMZ confirmed</a> that Curry died last night in his home in Los Angeles; he was 80.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><br />
<span style="font-weight: 400;">Among the numerous images burnt into the public consciousness by 20th century film, few are as raucous, confident, or unrelenting as watching Curry’s Dr. Frank-N-Furter strut, prowl, and seduce his way through every other character in </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Rocky</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">’s sci-fi-inspired menagerie of weirdos, inviting them to do the Time Warp (and other, lewder acts). Curry originated the role in its various stage productions, having previously met writer Richard O’Brien when they were both working on </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Hair</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">. (The theater would remain one of the great loves of his long life; while </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Rocky</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">’s Broadway debut was famously disastrous, Curry eventually took home three Tony nominations across his long career, for </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Amadeus</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">My Favorite Year</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, and </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Spamalot</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">.) He brought that same high-camp energy to the screen for the cult movie adaptation of </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Rocky</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, vamping in garters and pearls, asking whatever happened to Fay Wray, and just generally giving the biggest performance imaginable and then some—a key hallmark of the Curry acting technique.</span></p><!-- admarker --> <!-- inlinecontent_1 --> </p><!-- RevContent --> <div id="revcontent-hidden"> <!-- revisit -->
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Among the greatest, most gleeful hams of his generation, Curry spent decades absolutely devouring scenery in major productions of all stripes, giving performances that would have been indelibly lessened in the hands of a man more burdened by concepts like “restraint.” It’s not that he wasn’t subtle or nuanced as a performer—anyone who’s watched his comic turns in films like </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Clue </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">knows how well the man could sell a punchline with little more than the raise of an eyebrow and a slight inflection of tone—it’s just that he seemed to be having so much god-damned </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">fun </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">going over the top, whether exulting in oily satisfaction as the villain Hexxus in </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">FernGully: The Last Rainforest</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, or threatening Tom Cruise from underneath mountains of prosthetics in Ridley Scott’s </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Legend</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">. Pennywise, Long John Silver, even simply the smarmy hotel manager from </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Home Alone 2</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">: These weren’t just movie villains. They were </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Tim Curry </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">villains, and that told audiences of an entire generation that they were in good (if also extremely evil) hands.</span></p>
<p></p><!-- inlinecontent_2 --> </p><!-- admarker --> 
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Said identification/deification among younger audiences was helped along immeasurably by Curry’s long career as a voice actor; even ignoring starring roles like baffled dad Nigel on Nickelodeon’s </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Wild Thornberry</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">’s, his resume is jam-packed with vocal performances—often attached to names like Maelstrom, Dr. Calamitous, or Skullmaster. (The last one, from the mid-’90s </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Mighty Max </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">cartoon, pitted him against the late Tony Jay, for a real showdown of deep-voiced British cartoon baddies.) The platonic Tim Curry voice role had much in common with his live-action acting: High-volume, high-energy, and with just enough of a wink to let you know that it was all in good fun. (Not that he didn’t make an occasional mis-step; his let’s-say-challenging performance as a Cajun mystery writer in the first of Sierra’s </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Gabriel Knight </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">mystery games remains an affectionately goofy touch point for adventurer game players of a certain age.)</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">In contrast to his public performances—which also encompassed multiple studio albums, Broadway musicals, and more—Curry was a famously private figure; although he shed some of his shyness in later years, his thoughtfulness and quiet in interviews still came through, even when he gamely popped on a Dracula cape to chat about </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Rocky Horror </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">with Sinbad.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Curry suffered a stroke in 2012, which severely curtailed his mobility, and functionally ended his live-action acting career. (He continued to voice act, including taking over the role of the Emperor for Cartoon Network’s </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Star Wars: Clone Wars</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">.) He leaves behind a legacy of beloved characters, alongside a dark chuckle and winking gleam that echo through the last 50 years of the history of television, film, and more.</span></p><!-- inlinecontent_2 --> </p>
