<p><img alt="Troy" class="attachment-thumbnail-large size-thumbnail-large wp-post-image" height="480" src="https://www.slantmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/troy4k-720x480.jpg" width="720" /></p><p><img alt="Troy" class="alignleft wp-image-50018619" src="/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/troy4k.jpg" width="315" />We may think of Helen of Troy from <em>The Iliad</em> as the face that launched a thousand ships, but it’s the chiseled visages and sculpted physiques of male matinee idols that matter in Wolfgang Petersen’s <em>Troy</em>. As Paris, Orlando Bloom’s delicate, teenybopper attractiveness helps accentuate the Trojan prince’s slightness and egotism, while a conspicuous spectacle is made of a bearded Eric Bana’s heavage as Paris’s noble warrior brother Hector.</p>
<p>And at the epicenter of this collection of drool-worthy studs is Brad Pitt, whose bronzed, ripped body, long golden tresses, and photogenic brooding give his demigod killer Achilles a striking <em>Vanity Fair</em>-ish stylishness. With such a bevy of buff bods, Petersen’s film—a stately, serviceable, but largely unremarkable historical epic about the legendary fight for the heart of a woman and the conquest of a nation—naturally subscribes to a no-shirt rule for ancient Greece’s most prominent heroes. This isn’t the era of antiquity so much as an age of rock-hard abs.</p>

<p>Petersen’s film is largely faithful to the basic elements of <em>The Iliad</em>: Paris steals Helen (Diane Kruger) away from Spartan prince Menelaus (Brendan Gleeson), who in turn convinces his conquest-mad brother Agamemnon (Brian Cox) to wage war against Troy with the help of his peerless soldier Achilles and, ultimately, a trusty wooden horse. Yet for a more than 3,000-year-old legend dealing with the big themes of love, death, honor, sacrifice, and the gods (reverently pronounced “gawds” by Spartans and Trojans alike), <em>Troy</em>’s by-the-books spears-and-sandals spectacle (streamlined for the screen by David Benioff) has a decidedly modern feel.</p>

<p>During a rowdy opening feast between the cordial Trojans and Spartans, Hector and Menelaus toast “to the Gods!” by pouring wine on the ground. Hector and Paris’s return home to Troy with Helen in tow is celebrated, while Achilles’s showdown with Hector—not only the best of the film’s many accomplished action sequences, but a clash that outdoes anything in Ridley Scott’s <a href="/film/oscar-best-picture-winners-ranked/"><em>Gladiator</em></a>—has a WWE-meets-the-NFL adrenaline rush. Achilles, in fact, even has a pro-wrestling-style patented finishing move in which he leaps up and fatally stabs his opponent once in the shoulder. By the time Trojan King Priam (played with magisterial weariness by Peter O’Toole) first lays his eyes on the stunning Helen, one half-expects him to call her “juicy.”</p>

<p>Not to say that <em>Troy</em> is just bodacious boy toys grappling with each other on the sandy Trojan shore. Petersen’s film strives for the timeless and the monumental, and thus his cast is filled out with an assortment of distinguished thespians. Cox and Gleeson, spitting and screaming to their hearts’ delight, maniacally devour the scenery as Agamemnon and Menelaus, while O’Toole and Julie Christie (as Achilles’s mother Thetis)—no strangers to David Lean-esque cinematic pageantry—add some dignified regality to the bloody, combat-heavy events. And Petersen, aided by beautifully sunburnt cinematography by Roger Pratt and authentic production design by Nigel Phelps, skillfully balances his cornucopia of colossal CGI effects (which include a recreation of the 1,000-ship fleet and Troy’s towering architecture) with quieter moments involving Achilles, Hector, and Paris’s struggle with doubt, fury, and guilt.</p>
<p>What the director cannot accomplish, though, is instilling his story’s turbulent passions and desires with more than a solemn superficiality. Achilles, who loathes his king Agamemnon, fights not for country or honor but for immortality. Yet his inner discord over the worthiness of such a goal—heightened by his romance with feisty, fearless Spartan slave girl Briseis (Rose Byrne), and also his realization of the countless lives he’s ended—is, like too much of the film’s essential drama, handled with affected grandiosity. Petersen is at home orchestrating the immense war scenes, but when it comes to intimate personal relationships, his direction becomes stilted and pretentious, such as his penchant for awkwardly switching to slow-motion at the end of scenes, relying on thunderous war drums for the battles and Enya-ish music for the tragic events, and including unintentionally funny bug-eyed reaction shots of Helen, Priam, and Hector’s wife Andromache (Saffron Burrows) during Hector’s last stand.</p>


<p>“Ignore the politics,” Odysseus (Sean Bean) tells the jaded Achilles, though it will surely be tough for some to avoid concocting parallels between Agamemnon’s crusade and the war in Iraq, or Achilles’s defilement of the slain Hector with recent images of prisoners being despicably mistreated by U.S. soldiers. In the case of <em>Troy</em>, though, such comparisons are largely facile, as Petersen’s film is mainly interested in the immense cost war takes on its victors and losers, as well as the greed and irresponsibility that governs so many youthful hearts.</p>
<p>Bruised and battered by the skillful Menelaus in a duel he believes may save Troy from war, Paris—a reckless playboy more interested in his own emotional and sexual appetites than the fate of his countrymen—crawls to his brother’s feet, desperately seeking refuge from impending death at the hands of his Spartan adversary. Hector, exhibiting courageous loyalty, saves his spineless sibling. But in this one pitiful act of cowardice, Bloom’s Paris sums up the foolishness of the Trojan War, and provides <em>Troy</em> with a magnificently arresting glimpse of human cravenness that trumps the film’s otherwise overwrought classical posturing.</p>

<h3>Image/Sound</h3>
<p>It’s difficult not to see this release as a cynical attempt to capitalize on the success of Christopher Nolan’s <a href="/film/the-odyssey-review-christopher-nolan-matt-damon/"><em>The Odyssey</em></a>. <em>Troy</em> first appeared on Blu-ray in the format’s nascent days, and any flaws in that presentation could be attributed in part to era-specific limitations. Arrow’s release comes with brand-new 4K remasters of the theatrical and director’s cuts of Wolfgang Petersen’s epic, and the transfers are remarkably film-like. The colors are incredible, notably the more true-to-life cooler tones, and skin tones are natural while still boasting a sheen that reminds you that we’re in the realm of fantasy. On the audio front, both the original lossless DTS-HD MA 5.1 surround audio and stereo audio will give your sound system a workout, but the former is especially resonant, with the crystal-clear dialogue well mixed with the persistent sound effects and James Horner’s ostentatious score spaciously presented throughout.</p>

<h3>Extras</h3>
<p>Most of the extras here have been ported over from the 2004 Warner DVD release. Among the featurettes is “In the Thick of Battle,” which focuses on the training the film’s Mexican and Bulgarian extras and stars had to go through, and “From Ruins to Reality,” which is about the recreation of the ancient world but mostly lingers in the mind for the suggestion that David Benioff, hot off of writing Spike Lee’s incendiary <a href="/film/25th-hour/"><em>25th Hour</em></a>, took the project because of his similarity to a model-faced Greek warrior. The highlight, though, is the “Gallery of the Gods” 3D-animated guide, which makes learning about the gods fun and campy. Rounding things out is an archival picture-in-picture commentary, an introduction by Petersen, an Easter egg, the film’s trailer, and image galleries. An accompanying booklet features new writing on the film by Priscilla Page and Laurie Maguire and an excerpt from the original production notes.</p>
<h3>Overall</h3>
<p>Arrow Video memorably burnishes Wolfgang Petersen’s epic from 2004 for our UHD times, though the extras are disappointingly second-hand.</p>
