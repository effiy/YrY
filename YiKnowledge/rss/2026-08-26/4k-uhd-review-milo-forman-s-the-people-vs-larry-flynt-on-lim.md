<p><img alt="The People vs. Larry Flynt" class="attachment-thumbnail-large size-thumbnail-large wp-post-image" height="480" src="https://www.slantmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/peoplevslarryflynt4k-720x480.jpg" width="720" /></p><p><img alt="The People vs. Larry Flynt" class="alignleft wp-image-50018619" src="/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/peoplevslarryflynt4k.jpg" width="315" />Miloš Forman’s <em>The People vs. Larry Flynt</em> is mostly remembered for the hysterical controversies it caused (the film sank in <em>Variety</em>’s hypothetical “stix” like many an Oscar contender). That’s not so much a reflection on how heated the editorial battle lines were drawn between Jerry Falwell, Gloria Steinem, the filmmakers, and the real-life Flynt, but rather a somewhat damning suggestion toward the lukewarm film itself.</p>
<p><em>The People vs. Larry Flynt</em> was lucky to be blessed with just about the coolest cast. Harrelson stepped into the role of the sleazemonger Flynt with a sense of matinee impishness, Edward Norton pulled another hat trick after <em>Primal Fear</em>, and Courtney Love’s balls-out interpretation of Althea was enough to net an award from the New York Film Critics Circle. Unfortunately, the film they do their best to bring to life ends up as conventional as the Frank Capra movies that it’s obviously modeled after.</p>

<p><a href="/dvd/ed-wood/"><em>Ed Wood</em></a>’s screenwriters, Scott Alexander and Larry Karaszewski, known now for their quirky biopics (they would later pen Forman’s <em>Man on the Moon</em> and Paul Schrader’s <a href="/film/auto-focus/"><em>Auto Focus</em></a>), have a distinct knack for hammering real, messy human lives into an ultra-rigid dramatic arc. Here, the film ends with the landmark <em>Hustler Magazine v. Falwell</em> Supreme Court decision in Flynt’s (and the First Amendment’s) favor. They do it so well to Larry Flynt that it’s hard to notice the ignored points of debate or contention whizzing by unexplored.</p>

<p>In an effort to be fair to both sides, Flynt (Harrelson) is depicted as something of an “aw, shucks”-ter while Reverend Jerry Falwell (Richard Paul), ostensibly the film’s foe, is barely presented at all; he’s just a benign sack of eye-rolling incorruptibility. While it’s understandable that one’s natural impulse when walking through a minefield is to step lightly, Alexander and Karaszewski—unmistakably the <em>auteurs</em> behind the film, as Forman’s brand of smooth naturalism just about approaches nonchalance—unfortunately left the door open for a firestorm of criticism from the far left. They’ve since made a point of expressing their surprise that it was the left that attacked the film, rather than the extreme right. Why, one wonders?</p>

<p>Though <em>The People vs. Larry Flynt</em> is, by the writers’ admission, “a love letter to the ACLU,” it’s also an absolute reading of the Bill of Rights. “[Conservatives] <em>get</em> that,” reason the filmmakers on the DVD commentary track. Unfortunately, what the writers don’t consider is that leftists also “get that,” but have perhaps moved on to expect something more (like the feminist critique of Flynt’s white-trash brand of pornography) from them. Whitewashing the truth is a forgivable sin in the world of biopics, but to demean ideologies by remaining “balanced” is cowardly.</p>
<h3>Image/Sound</h3>
<p>Arrow’s 4K presentation of the film abounds in lustrous nuances made possible by the Dolby Vision HDR dynamic range, with reds, whites, and blues especially (and appropriately) resonating beautifully. Skin tones are true, black levels are perfect, and the grain is well resolved. The DTS-HD Master Audio 5.1 is no more eventful than it has to be, but natural environments are presented with sonic precision. Also included is an LPCM 2.0 track.</p>


<h3>Extras</h3>
<p>This release offers up a stacked assortment of the old and new. On the archival front, we get two commentary tracks. The more informative and thoughtful of the two comes from screenwriters Scott Alexander and Larry Karaszewski, who admittedly often simply reiterate the film’s obvious messages but sometimes refer to their more anarchic sides (they bemoan the infamous deleted scene in which Flynt is approached on his plane by the spirit of Lenny Bruce, which would’ve been the high point). The second features the three stars (Courtney Love is by far the most colloquial), but unfortunately they seem to have been recorded separately, as there’s no discernible dialogue between them. The most interesting commentary tracks make one feel as though they’re eavesdropping on a private conversation behind the scenes. This one doesn’t.</p>
<p>Much more rewarding are the interviews with Forman and cinematographer Philippe Rousselot and the thoughtfully assembled half-hour featurettes (one on Larry Flynt, the other on the issue of free speech). In addition to brand-new interviews with Harrelson, Alexander and Larry Karaszewski, and Columbia Pictures’s ex-production president Lisa Henson and producer Janet Yang, we also get a few deleted scenes (with commentary) that don’t add up to a whole lot, a trailer, image galleries, and TV spots. The accompanying booklet features new writing on the film by Miloš Forman expert Andreas Halskov, an archival essay by Alexander and Karaszewski, and an archival Forman interview conducted by director Adam Davidson.</p>
<h3>Overall</h3>
<p>Surprisingly mundane, given the central figure, <em>The People vs. Larry Flynt</em> puts the “lesson” in “civics lesson,” but Arrow’s 4K glow-up is anything but.</p>
