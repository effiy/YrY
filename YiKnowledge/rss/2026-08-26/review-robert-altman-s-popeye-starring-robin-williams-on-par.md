<p><img alt="Popeye" class="attachment-thumbnail-large size-thumbnail-large wp-post-image" height="480" src="https://www.slantmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/popeye4k-720x480.jpg" width="720" /></p><p><img alt="Popeye" class="alignleft wp-image-50018619" src="/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/popeye4k.jpg" width="315" />Many have written off 1980’s <em>Popeye</em> as another charred remnant of Robert Altman’s spectacular burnout at the close of the 1970s—virtually indistinguishable from the equally disparaged <em>Health</em>, <em>A Wedding</em>, and <em>Quintet</em>. But <em>Popeye</em> is in need of a serious critical rediscovery, because virtually every one of Altman’s signature hallmarks—that teeming sense of community gathering habits, concern for social inequalities, and fondness for earnest, country-fried comic bits—are very much alive here.</p>
<p>Anyone new to Altman is likely to be put off by the film’s unique worldview. Known for building American communities from the bottom up, the director took a well-established slice of Americana (E.C. Segar’s <em>Popeye</em> comic strip) and seemingly refused to distance himself through irony or radical departures—like, say, having Popeye on the front lines in the Korean War.</p>

<p>Popeye (Robin Williams), Bluto (Paul L. Smith), and Olive Oyl (Shelley Duvall) trip and mumble their way through exaggerated love triangles just like they did in the comics and short films. But if you strip away the film’s loyalty to the  E.C. Segar source material, it’s not difficult to see that, in many ways, <em>Popeye</em> is Altman’s comic spin on <a href="/dvd/mccabe-and-mrs-miller-bd/"><em>McCabe &#038; Mrs. Miller</em></a>, even substituting that film’s whore house with a floating gambling house and brothel. Like Warren Beatty’s John McCabe, Williams’s Popeye has a habit of vocalizing his inner dialogues to no one in particular.</p>

<p>For a film often dismissed as kiddie fare, there are a surprising number of Altman concepts that are likely to fly right over the heads of younguns. The people of Sweethaven, where Popeye lands in search of his Pap (Ray Walston), are cheerfully oblivious their state of severe economic and social oppression. Bluto represents the strong arm of the law (the beanpole constable jumps out of windows whenever the man-mountain enters the room) and the noodly taxman represents its sticky fingers. Both work for a dictatorial menace known as the Commodore. “Next to Wimpy, I hate him best,” Mr. Geezil (Richard Libertini) privately bellows with a Topol-esque brogue. The hints of a far more menacing political situation undercut most of the jokes. Wimpy’s (Paul Dooley) immortal “I will gladly pay you Tuesday for a hamburger today” sounds like it’s coming from a man blissfully ignorant of his severe dependency on credit currency.</p>

<p>To kids, Wimpy is a hamburgler. To adults, he’s the recently laid-off neighbor. Also, most kids probably can’t grasp what Olive Oyl’s arpeggio-ridden ballad to Bluto’s “large” qualities is <em>really</em> about. Throughout <em>Popeye</em>, Altman directs the complex web of social interactions with a frame that’s both inclusive and prying. And the actors he collected help evoke an atmosphere that’s at once genial and guarded. Duvall couldn’t possibly have played Olive Oyl badly, and to watch Williams’s sweet interpretation of the hyper-violent original character here is to mourn what we lost when the actor (understandably, in retrospect) leaned into the saccharine feels in such films as <em>Patch Adams</em>, <em>Bicentennial Man</em>, and <em>Jakob the Liar</em>.</p>
<p>Altman’s location work ties all of this together. Sweethaven was built plank by plank into a Maltese hillside, a lived-in warren of crooked jetties and sagging clapboard that the camera is free to wander through rather than merely frame. (The set exists to this day as something of a tourist attraction.) A film this committed to the illusion of a functioning, ramshackle town deserves a format that can finally show its seams and its craft in equal measure. Nearly 50 years on, <em>Popeye</em> isn’t a curio to be indulged; it’s a communal, melancholic comedy hiding in plain sight inside a musical for kids, one that stands shoulder with Altman’s other great musicals.</p>


<h3>Image/Sound</h3>
<p><em>Popeye</em> already got a sweet makeover when it was remastered for Blu-ray. Giuseppe Rotunno’s location cinematography—all salt-bleached wood and hazy Adriatic light—finally has the Dolby Vision HDR dynamic range to register as intentional rather than murky. Film grain is stable and filmic throughout, and the increase in fine detail does real favors to the handmade texture of Wolf Kroeger’s production design. As for the audio, Robert Altman’s typically layered sound design and the film’s many songs are given a robust Dolby TrueHD mix that nicely separates all of the overlapping dialogue and deluge of various sound effects.</p>
<h3>Extras</h3>
<p>No new supplements have been produced for this 4K UHD Blu-ray release that weren’t already included on Paramount’s 2020 Blu-ray. “Return to Sweethaven: A Look Back with Robin and the Altmans” looks at some of the insanity of the film’s shoot in Malta, but while the stories about the efforts made to create the Sweethaven set and the studio’s responses to the production going long and over budget are interesting, 13 minutes is barely enough time to scratch the surface. Considering the perceived failure of the film and its effect on the next decade of Altman’s career, this subject matter begs for a more comprehensive treatment.</p>

<p>The other featurette on the disc, “The Popeye Company Players,” is even more perfunctory, running down the major actors in the film and providing brief behind-the-scenes anecdotes, such as Robin Williams learning to dance for the film and Altman’s insistence on Shelley Duvall for the part of Olive Oyl. The disc is rounded out with a very short slideshow of pictures from the film’s debut in 1980 and a feature that lets you watch only the songs from the film, both separately or all in a row, along with a theatrical trailer. Fans hoping for a fresh retrospective befitting the film’s critical reappraisal will have to keep waiting.</p>
<h3>Overall</h3>
<p>Robert Altman’s idiosyncratic, cult musical comedy gets a beautiful transfer for its 4K debut, but the continued lack of meaningful extra features is a lamentable omission.</p>
