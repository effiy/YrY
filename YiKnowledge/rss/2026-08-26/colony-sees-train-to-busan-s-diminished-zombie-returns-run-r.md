<img src="https://img.pastemagazine.com/wp-content/avuploads/2026/08/24103448/colony-header.jpg" /><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;Imperfect communication, the source of all tragedy.&#8221; This is the societal pratfall that </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Colony</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, from South Korean filmmaker Yeon Sang-ho, examines by way of zombies. Since helming </span><a href="https://www.avclub.com/the-best-movies-of-2016-that-we-didn-t-review-1798255152"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Train To Busan</span></i></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">, its animated prequel S</span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">eoul Station,</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> and live-action sequel </span><a href="https://www.avclub.com/train-to-busan-sequel-peninsula-is-all-zombie-spectacle-1844587243"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Peninsula</span></i></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">, Yeon has clearly developed cred in this specific horror subgenre. Yet </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Colony </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">feels comparatively hollow. Despite attempting to recreate the formula of </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Busan</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8216;s success—chilling monster designs, a (mostly) single-location conceit, and ragtag group of survivors—this specific concoction doesn&#8217;t end up as potent.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">This time, the chaos unfolds amid a biotechnology conference, where professors and business leaders converge to discuss advancements in the field. Kwon Se-jeong (Jun Ji-hyun, totally void of charisma) hopes to gain more than just the low-down on new research, as the recent loss of her professorship means that she needs a new gig—and quickly. She&#8217;s joined at the conference by her well-meaning ex-husband, Han Gyu-seong (Go Soo, also </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Busan</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8216;s lead character), who hopes he can help before he relocates abroad with his new wife, whose contact is lovingly saved in his phone as &#8220;my better half,&#8221; establishing that no lingering attraction exists between the exes. After hearing an eminent CEO&#8217;s presentation on &#8220;collective intelligence,&#8221; &#8220;yellow slime mold,&#8221; and &#8220;organic semiconductors,&#8221; Se-jeong is encouraged to introduce herself and, with any luck, secure a coveted gig in her field.&nbsp;</span></p><!-- admarker --> <!-- inlinecontent_1 --> </p><!-- RevContent --> <div id="revcontent-hidden"> <!-- revisit -->
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">But the conference&#8217;s findings quickly go from conceptual to contagious. Se-jeong never gets to schmooze with the CEO, because shortly after leaving the stage, he&#8217;s confronted by Seo Young-cheol (Koo Kyo-hwan), whose crazed eyes loudly broadcast an unstable psyche. Declaring that the presentation directly plagiarized his research, Young-cheol procures a syringe loaded with Midori-colored liquid and stabs the executive in the neck. Within minutes, he&#8217;s covered in blood and sticky white mucus—and is hell-bent on transmitting whatever he&#8217;s been injected with to as many other people as possible. The conference center and its adjoining shopping mall are immediately quarantined, leaving everyone inside left to fend off an ever-increasing number of slimy, quick-crawling infected. (Their twitchy, frenetic movements, choreographed by </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Busan </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">collaborator Jeon Young alongside a team of dancers, are a major highlight.)&nbsp;</span></p><!-- admarker --> 
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">As the title suggests—and as the CEO plainly elucidates in his presentation—the blueprint for these gnarly ghouls comes from the social structure of ants. Perhaps because they just sat through a finely detailed explanation of the origins of the bioweapon they&#8217;re dealing with, Se-jeong and Gyu-seong, soon joined by a motley crew of survivors, begin to outsmart the creatures&#8217; swarm intelligence, which they transmit through an inbred mycelial network. They use the infected&#8217;s poor eyesight to trick them with billboards and mannequins; they chemically mask their human pheromones, which the ant-like zombies can easily sniff out; and they use water to wash away thick layers of slime mold. But their initial inroads are thwarted by the lingering presence of Young-cheol, who is perfectly cognizant despite having also injected himself with the mold. Not only that, but the diseased monsters seem to avoid him entirely.&nbsp;</span></p><!-- inlinecontent_2 --> </p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">An overwhelming sense of confusion courses through </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Colony</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, and not only among the protagonists. The bioweaponry aspect is flimsy, with the constant scientific sidebars (including one scene that involves hundreds of contaminated monkeys on the loose in a laboratory) distracting from the film&#8217;s central thesis: the importance of human connection. Unlike with</span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Train To Busan</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, the dynamic between these individuals doesn&#8217;t meaningfully develop over the course of the film. This makes each kill almost trivial, certainly void of the emotional resonance found in that movie&#8217;s climax. The closest we get is a brother and his wheelchair-using sister in a scene that feels insultingly crafted to tug on heartstrings. Not only are viewers left scratching our heads about the science behind this outbreak (overt homages to </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Contagion</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> don&#8217;t help), but we&#8217;re left wondering exactly why we should care if any of these people live or die. Preprogrammed desires to see the human race endure don&#8217;t quite cut it.</span></p><!-- admarker --> 
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The convolution continues when it&#8217;s revealed that Se-jeong and Young-cheol share a connection in their past—meant to strengthen the &#8220;imperfect communication&#8221; theme, albeit ham-fistedly—as well as when the contagion breaks free from the building and spills onto bustling city streets. There&#8217;s too much going on here, and it&#8217;s surprising that Yeon didn&#8217;t keep this story intentionally pared-down in order to replicate what has worked so well for him in the past. This doesn&#8217;t even pertain strictly to </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Busan</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, but also his earlier films, particularly his debut </span><a href="https://www.theguardian.com/film/2013/jan/24/king-of-pigs-review"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The King Of Pigs</span></i></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">, a semi-autobiographical, intensely psychological animated film that helped put him on the map. In a marked departure from these projects, </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Colony</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> is utterly unsure of its own identity—an ironic fate for a film about the importance of clear messaging.</span></p>
