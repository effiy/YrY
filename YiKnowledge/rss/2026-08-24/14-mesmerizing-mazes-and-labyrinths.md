<h3> </h3>
<p>Since antiquity, mazes and labyrinths have been places of spiritual reflection and deep contemplation. The famous Egyptian Labyrinth at Hawara was built around 1860 BCE during the reign of <span class="yADgie">Pharaoh Amenemhat III, though 2D depictions of the classic seven-circuit design appear as early as 2000 BCE in clay tablets from the Mediterranean. Mazes, too, have origins in ancient Egypt, primarily serving to keep looters away from the tombs of the important and wealthy. </span></p>
<p>Over hundreds of centuries, these structures have persisted in our cultural consciousness, embraced across the globe by bands of Vikings in Scandinavia, aristocrats in 18th-century Spain, eccentric elites in 1970s England, and bar owners in modern-day Vietnam. Whether you seek a mental challenge or simply a quiet place to retreat from the world, these mazes and labyrinths around the world are well worth a visit.</p>
<h3 class="article-second-subheading-pre-rd">What Is the Difference Between a Maze and a Labyrinth?</h3>
<p>A labyrinth consists of one winding, curved passage that ultimately leads to a central point. While it may take a while to walk through, you will arrive at the center without getting lost or needing to backtrack. A maze, meanwhile, is designed as a puzzle, with multiple pathways and dead-ends that may require you to retrace your steps.</p>
<p>Note: At some of the places featured in this list, the terms labyrinth and maze have historically been used interchangeably.</p>

<h3 class="article-second-subheading-pre-rd"><strong><a href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/longleat-hedge-maze">Longleat Hedge Maze</a></strong> 📍 Warminster, England</h3>
<p>In 1975, this impressive maze was added to the grounds of the stately Longleat House, the home of the Marquesses of Bath since 1541. While it is not not the world’s largest hedge maze, it <em>is</em> the world’s longest, composed of more than 16,000 English yews and stretching across 1.48 acres. The entire Longleat property is full of equally unusual features, including the very first drive-through safari park, opened to the public in 1966 and home to over 500 animals.</p>
<p>Before his passing in 2020, the Seventh Marquess of Bath, Alexander Thynn, added to the great maze and commissioned three other smaller ones: the Lunar Labyrinth, the Sun Maze, King Arthur’s Maze, and the Love Labyrinth. Today, the house, gardens, mazes, and safari park are all open to the public—the perfect place for a full day of whimsical exploration.</p>
<p><br /><img alt="article-image" class="article-image with-structured-caption " src="https://assets.atlasobscura.com/article_images/109778/image.jpg" width="auto" /></p>
<h3 class="article-second-subheading-pre-rd"><strong><a href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/magowans-infinite-mirror-maze">Magowan’s Infinite Mirror Maze</a></strong> 📍 San Francisco, California</h3>
<p>In a nondescript building on San Francisco’s Pier 39, this psychedelic spot encourages visitors to “get lost in the infinite” in its maze of disorienting columns and mirrors. Black lights and flashing neon illuminate the mirrors sporadically while music pulses, making for a completely immersive, multi-sensory experience. Once you’ve managed to make it through the maze once, staff members have been known to recommend trying it out backwards for an added challenge.</p>

<h3 class="article-second-subheading-pre-rd"><strong> <a href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/tasmazia-and-the-village-of-lower-crackpot">Tasmazia and the Village of Lower Crackpot</a></strong> 📍 Promised Land, Australia</h3>
<p>At the foot of Tasmania’s picturesque Mount Roland, this self-proclaimed “largest maze complex in the world” is essentially a nesting doll of hedge mazes: one Great Maze with a number of smaller ones nestled inside, from a Yellow Brick Road Maze to a Hexagonal Maze and an Irish Maze.</p>
<p>After you’ve navigated the mazes, there are plenty of other quirky attractions to explore: The eponymous Village of Lower Crackpot, a quaint miniature village built to one-fifth scale; the Embassy Garden, a set of model buildings representing locations around the world; and the Lower Crackpot’s Correctional Centre, home to an assortment of old-school punishments such as faux stocks and a guillotine.</p>

<h3 class="article-second-subheading-pre-rd"><strong> <a href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/bara-imambara">Bara Imambara</a></strong> 📍 Lucknow, India</h3>
<p>The massive central hall (<em>imambara</em>) of this 18th-century building is considered the largest arched hall in the world built without any kind of beams for external support. The trick that keeps the ceiling from crashing down? The <em>bhulbhulaiya: </em>a labyrinthine network of interconnected passageways that was built directly above the hall’s chambers, effectively making the ceiling hollow. Loosely translated as “maze” in Hindi, the bhulbhulaiya’s hundreds of paths are notoriously difficult to navigate, but don’t worry—guides are available!</p>
<p><img alt="article-image" class="article-image with-structured-caption " src="https://assets.atlasobscura.com/article_images/109781/image.jpg" width="auto" /></p>
<h3 class="article-second-subheading-pre-rd"><strong> <a href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/knossos-crete">Knossos</a></strong> 📍 Heraklion, Greece</h3>
<p>In northern Crete lie the ruins of Knossos, a city that dates back to 7000 BCE and was the setting for several Greek myths. It is said that King Minos built his palace here and commissioned the craftsman Daedulus to build a huge, underground labyrinth to hold Pasiphea’s son, the minotaur.</p>
<p>After centuries of existing only in myth, restoration of Knossos began after its rediscovery in 1900. However, though the city draws thousands of visitors each year, it is not an especially accomplished restoration project: Most of the city’s famous frescoes are entirely modern, and it is quite plainly reinforced with concrete. While those expecting an accurate depiction of antiquity may be somewhat disappointed, the ruins are still worth exploring for the many epic tales they evoke.</p>
<figure class=" contains-caption "><img alt="article-image" class="article-image with-structured-caption " src="https://assets.atlasobscura.com/article_images/109782/image.jpg" width="auto" /></figure>
<h3 class="article-second-subheading-pre-rd"><strong> <a href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/labyrinten-vid-tibble-the-tibble-maze">Labyrinten vid Tibble (The Tibble Labyrinth)</a></strong> 📍 Hökåsen-Badelunda, Sweden</h3>
<p>This part of southwest Sweden is a treasure trove of Viking artifacts and relics, including this 2,000-year-old labyrinth just outside the city of Västerås. Situated on a high, wooded ridge, the site is comprised of around 3,000 stones spanning an area of 40x45 feet. It was likely used for the ancient Nordic spring rite in which a boy had to save a girl from the center. The rules dictated that he could not touch the stones while traveling through the labyrinth; if he did, he was forced to exit and was replaced by another young contender.</p>

<h3 class="article-second-subheading-pre-rd"><strong><a href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/bolshoi-zayatsky-island">Bolshoi Zayatsky Island</a></strong> 📍 Solovetskiy, Russia</h3>
<p>The Solovetsky Islands on Russia’s White Sea are tiny and isolated, with beautifully sparse landscapes and a population of only a couple hundred. Bolshoi Zayatsky Island is only one square mile in total, but what it lacks in size it makes up for in mystery. Its rocky surface is covered in dozens of labyrinths that date back to 30,000 BCE.</p>
<p>Archaeologists have not determined the exact use of these structures, some of which are made of stacked stones and others of which are built into the ground as earthen mounds. However, their purpose was most likely mystical: portals to the underworld, traps for evil spirits, or ceremonial altars.</p>
<figure class=" contains-caption "><img alt="article-image" class="article-image with-structured-caption " src="https://assets.atlasobscura.com/article_images/109784/image.jpg" width="auto" /></figure>
<h3 class="article-second-subheading-pre-rd"><strong> <a href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/100-roofs-cafe">100 Roofs Cafe</a></strong> 📍Đà Lạt, Vietnam</h3>
<p>From the outside this may look like a run-of-the-mill bar, but inside, it houses a a multistory maze complete with staircases to nowhere, underwater arthropods, and (if you find your way to the top) amazing views of Đà Lạt’s skyline. While the cafe serves up a variety of delicious, boozy beverages, it’s best to pace yourself so you can maneuver through the many dead ends, random tunnels, and sculpted women emerging from the walls.</p>
<figure class=" contains-caption "><img alt="article-image" class="article-image with-structured-caption " src="https://assets.atlasobscura.com/article_images/109785/image.jpg" width="auto" /></figure>
<h3 class="article-second-subheading-pre-rd"><strong><a href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/the-labirinto-della-masone">The Labirinto della Masone</a></strong> 📍 Fontanellato, Italy</h3>
<p>This cultural park is home to the world’s largest bamboo labyrinth, composed of 20 different species of bamboo ranging from dwarf to giant. Designed by Franco Maria Ricci, an Italian publisher, art collector, and bibliophile (with an enduring fondness for bamboo), the labyrinth takes the shape of an eight-pointed star in a nod to <a href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/palmanova-star-fortress-city">16th-century star-shaped fortress cities.</a> Its center is a square surrounded by arcades and large halls, and a pyramid-shaped chapel recalls the labyrinth as a symbol of faith.</p>
<figure class=" contains-caption "><img alt="article-image" class="article-image with-structured-caption " src="https://assets.atlasobscura.com/article_images/109786/image.jpg" width="auto" /></figure>
<h3 class="article-second-subheading-pre-rd"><strong> <a href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/peace-maze-northern-ireland">Peace Maze of Northern Ireland</a></strong> 📍 Castlewellan, Northern Ireland</h3>
<p>This hedge maze opened in 2001 to commemorate the peace and reconciliation efforts of Northern Ireland in the years following “The Troubles.” At the center of its two distinct halves (which must be crossed in order to escape the maze) lies the Peace Bell, which visitors ring to indicate they have solved the puzzle.</p>
<p>The maze is made up of over 6,000 yew trees, planted by people from all over Northern Ireland during December of 2000 and funded largely by the European Union Support Programme for Peace and Reconciliation in Northern Ireland. The trees are intentionally shorter than those in most hedge mazes, allowing for more open communication and interaction between visitors as they make their way through.</p>
<figure class=" contains-caption "><img alt="article-image" class="article-image with-structured-caption " src="https://assets.atlasobscura.com/article_images/109787/image.jpg" width="auto" /></figure>
<h3 class="article-second-subheading-pre-rd"><strong><a href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/cherry-point-farm-and-market-lavender-labyrinth">Cherry Point Farm and Market Lavender Labyrinth</a></strong> 📍 Shelby, Michigan</h3>
<p>In 2001, Barbara Bull, the fourth-generation owner of this Michigan farm, commissioned a beautiful, floral labyrinth with a kaleidoscopic herb garden at its epicenter. Visitors are invited to take a peaceful journey through the fragrant lavender, Black-eyed Susans, and hollyhocks, eventually reaching the more than 30 herb beds carefully arranged by principles of sacred geometry. Twelve circles, 52 arbor posts, and seven cross pieces represent the months and weeks of the year and the days of the week.</p>
<figure class=" contains-caption "><img alt="article-image" class="article-image with-structured-caption " src="https://assets.atlasobscura.com/article_images/109788/image.jpg" width="auto" /></figure>
<h3 class="article-second-subheading-pre-rd"><strong> <a href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/labyrint-drielandenpunt">Labyrint Drielandenpunt</a></strong> 📍 Vaals, Netherlands</h3>
<p>Translating to the “Three Country Labyrinth,” this maze of 17,000 hornbeam shrubs is located where the Netherlands, Belgium, and Germany all meet. In the middle of the maze, a high platform allows visitors to look off into any of the three countries (or at their fellow travelers strolling through the hornbeams below).</p>
<p>Surrounding the maze are a handful of other activities for visitors to enjoy, from a colorful playground to clay shooting and archery. You can even play smuggling games that recall the once-frequent passage of smugglers who jumped borders to save money.</p>
<figure class=" contains-caption "><img alt="article-image" class="article-image with-structured-caption " src="https://assets.atlasobscura.com/article_images/109789/image.jpg" width="auto" /></figure>
<h3 class="article-second-subheading-pre-rd"><strong> <a href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/stuart-landsborough-s-puzzling-world">Puzzling World</a></strong> 📍 Wanaka, New Zealand</h3>
<p>This mystifying funhouse started as a single-story wooden maze in 1973 and has since grown into a veritable shrine to tricks and illusions. It was the world’s first maze to include overbridges, linking different parts of the maze together to create a fully immersive, 3D experience.</p>
<p>After you’ve navigated the maze, make your way through the park’s many fun follies, from a group of buildings splayed like a hand of cards to the “Leaning Tower of Wanaka,” forever tilted at a dizzying 53 degrees. There are also five Illusion Rooms, including the Hologram Hall and Tilted House, plus the Sculptillusion Gallery, which showcases artworks that double as optical illusions.</p>

<h3 class="article-second-subheading-pre-rd"><strong> <a href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/labyrinth-park-of-horta">Labyrinth Park of Horta</a></strong> 📍 Barcelona, Spain</h3>
<p>At the center of Barcelona’s Parc del Laberint d’Hort, this hedge maze has dazazled locals and tourists alike since 1791, when it was built as part of a wealthy estate owned by the Desvalls family. The meticulously manicured walls create over 2,000 feet of twists and turns, and if you make it to the center, you’ll find a solitary statue of Eros, Greek God of Love. The maze is also rich with water features, including waterfalls, ponds, and even a canal.</p>
