<p><img alt="Idiots" class="attachment-thumbnail-large size-thumbnail-large wp-post-image" height="480" src="https://www.slantmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/idiots-720x480.jpg" width="720" /></p><p>As with a disappointing offspring, there are excuses one could make for <em>Idiots</em>. Maybe form is supposed to follow function here, and this tale of the intersecting lives of a group of clueless, irredeemable people on the margins is <em>supposed</em> to be low-effort in order to express everyone’s listlessness. If you’re hungry for a good American movie comedy, your instinct might be to give the film the benefit of the doubt and appreciate it as idle amusement. But that’s a low bar that Macon Blair’s third feature struggles to clear right out of the gate.</p>
<p>The film’s opening scene, which finds the dunderheaded Davis (O’Shea Jackson Jr.) being reprimanded by his pastor and boss (Killer Mike), should be a layup. The men are discussing the details of Davis’s transgression, which involved unsuspecting children witnessing “dong going in” during a movie he took them to on his watch. But there’s no frisson to the scene, which stretches on for much too long, making its slackness that much more apparent.</p>

<p>The dynamic between Davis and drug-addled slacker Mark (Dave Franco) is the engine of the film, and if Franco and Jackson never lock into a complementary comedic groove, maybe it’s because the actors are aware that their characters have no backstory and are motivated by little more than keeping jobs that neither of them seems to want. Davis is a devout Christian, while the reefer-mad Mark at one point says that organized faith is “inane,” but you otherwise wouldn’t know why these guys can’t seem to get on the same wavelength.</p>

<p>New hires at the shady Dorindo Services, Davis and Mark are tasked with transporting a rich kid, Sheridan (Mason Thames), to rehab, only to lose him early in their trip. A somewhat internet-famous sociopath, Sheridan orchestrates his escape by drugging Mark, resulting in a gross-out moment that the film doesn’t even pretend it can top. From there, as Davis and Mark try to find Sheridan, the film cycles the characters through a series of absurd encounters that feel mostly like an excuse for the actors to mug, often bizarrely, for the camera.</p>

<p>At one point, Nicolas Braun shows up as Pricka Bush Da Werewoof, a horrorcore rapper who feels like a riff on James Franco’s Alien from <a href="/film/spring-breakers/"><em>Spring Breakers</em></a>. Braun’s commitment to expressing his character’s lycan-loving ways, as when he sings “lickin’ up his blood like it’s a lil treat,” is admirable, but his scenes are emblematic of just how threadbare <em>Idiots</em> is. </p>
<p>Pricka Bush Da Werewoof and his backwoods cohort, Koko (Peter Dinklage), enter the film to the sound of a thrash-metal song reminiscent of a cue from Joel Potrykus’s <a href="/film/vulcanizadora-review-joel-potrykus-joshua-burge/"><em>Vulcanizadora</em></a>, which covered similar thematic ground but with significantly more concerted effort. Throughout <em>Idiots</em>, the characters repeat a maxim seemingly from a self-help book, as if trying to convince themselves of something: Everyone is doing the best they’re able to do at any given moment. By the finale, it registers as a self-aware apology for how the film turned out.</p>
