<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">MTIA 300 is the first of Meta’s family of in-house training and inference accelerators optimized for training ranking and recommendation models.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">We’re sharing how MTIA 300’s built-in NIC chiplets allow it to meet the communication needs associated with training recommendation models with superior performance over general-purpose GPUs.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">By co-designing MTIA’s communication library, HCCL, alongside the chip we’ve taken a fundamentally different approach to chip design and made communication a first-class citizen.</span></li>
</ul>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Deep learning models may deliver personalized content—from short videos to friend posts—to people on apps. As these models have grown in complexity, so has the importance of the compute that trains them, and the network that connects those accelerators.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Training recommendation models is a unique infrastructure challenge. Unlike large language models, which need enormous floating-point throughput, recommendation models are bottlenecked by a need for fast and efficient communication between the accelerators that train them. Their embedding tables can contain over 99% of the model&#8217;s parameters, requiring hybrid parallelism that generates frequent AllReduce, AllToAll, and AllGather collectives across hundreds of accelerators. On chips like GPUs these communication operations compete with training computation for the same resources, often leaving expensive hardware underutilized.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">We’ve addressed this challenge starting on the Meta Training and Inference Accelerator (MTIA), our family of homegrown AI chips, with</span><a href="https://aisystemcodesign.github.io/papers/MTIA300_ISCA2026.pdf"> <span style="font-weight: 400;">MTIA 300</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">, the first of the MTIA family optimized for training recommendation and ranking models. By co-designing MTIA 300 with</span><a href="https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2608.00358"> <span style="font-weight: 400;">HCCL</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">, a communication library co-designed with the hardware from scratch, we’ve made communication a first-class citizen in the chip&#8217;s design, not an afterthought handled by general-purpose compute cores.</span></p>
<div class="jetpack-video-wrapper"></div>
<h2><span style="font-weight: 400;">Integrating the Network Directly on the Chip</span></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">With MTIA 300, the network interface lives inside the chip package itself (see Figure 1). Two network chiplets, each containing six custom 800 Gbps RDMA NICs, provide 1.2 TB/s of total I/O bandwidth without ever crossing a PCIe bus. This eliminates the host-device-NIC bottleneck present in traditional GPU architectures, where the CPU must mediate between the accelerator and the network. (More details about the silicon design are available in our recent paper from the </span><a href="https://aisystemcodesign.github.io/papers/MTIA300_ISCA2026.pdf"><span style="font-weight: 400;">ISCA 26</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> conference.)</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Because we use the same 12 Ethernet-based NICs for scale-up communication (within a rack of 16 nodes, at up to 1 TB/s) and scale-out communication (across racks, at 200 GB/s), we can flexibly partition the NICs to adjust to changing needs. </span></p>
<figure class="wp-caption alignnone" id="attachment_24395" style="width: 1200px;"><img alt="" class="size-full wp-image-24395" height="896" src="https://engineering.fb.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Meta-MTIA-300-Figure-1.png" width="1200" /><figcaption class="wp-caption-text" id="caption-attachment-24395">Figure 1. The MTIA 300 chip architecture. Diagram of MTIA 300 chip.</figcaption></figure>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">As model requirements shift, we can reconfigure this split by reconfiguring the network rather than changing the hardware. To minimize per-transaction latency, we introduced express doorbells. The work request write itself serves as the doorbell, eliminating an additional memory read and saving ~800 ns per operation.</span></p>
<h2><span style="font-weight: 400;">Offloading Communication From the Compute Grid</span></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">On GPUs, libraries such as NCCL execute collective communication as GPU kernels that consume streaming multiprocessors—the same hardware needed for training computation. When collectives and training kernels run simultaneously, both slow down.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">MTIA 300 takes a different approach. Alongside its 12×6 grid of processing elements (PEs) for computation, the chip includes 16 dedicated message engines (MEs) that handle all communication independently. </span><span style="font-weight: 400;"><br />
</span><span style="font-weight: 400;"><br />
</span><span style="font-weight: 400;">Each ME contains: </span></p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">an RISC-V core for orchestrating w</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">an NIC interface that routes requests to the correct NIC</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">a near-memory compute (NMC) block that performs reductions at 128 bytes/cycle </span></li>
</ul>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Positioned at the chip edges next to HBM and cache, the NMCs collectively deliver more than 2.8 TBs of reduction throughput—more than double the I/O bandwidth—enabling line-rate execution of AllReduce and ReduceScatter collectives without touching the compute grid.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The result is near-perfect isolation. Running large GEMMs concurrently with collective operations introduces less than 0.5% degradation to compute throughput, as opposed to traditional GPUs that can see over 20% degradation because communication is handled by the same GPU resources.</span></p>
<h2><span style="font-weight: 400;">A Compiled-Communication Model</span></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Our communication library, HCCL, was co-designed with MTIA 300. Rather than driving communication from the host during execution, HCCL compiles each collective into a complete set of subgraphs—arrays of work-queue entries with explicit dependencies—dispatched to the MEs for fully autonomous execution. Once work reaches the device, the host is uninvolved. Figure 2 shows how the CPU is no longer involved after copying the instructions into HBM.</span></p>
<figure class="wp-caption alignnone" id="attachment_24396" style="width: 1664px;"><img alt="" class="size-full wp-image-24396" height="640" src="https://engineering.fb.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Meta-MTIA-300-Figure-2.png" width="1664" /><figcaption class="wp-caption-text" id="caption-attachment-24396">Figure 2. A comparison of traditional accelerator design with host-based network instructions with MTIA 300’s offloaded communication model.</figcaption></figure>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">This compiled model integrates naturally with PyTorch’s c10d and </span><a href="https://pytorch.org/blog/torchcomms/"><span style="font-weight: 400;">torchcomms</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> interfaces. Collectives traced through </span><span style="font-weight: 400;">torch.compile</span><span style="font-weight: 400;"> are compiled into a single graph alongside compute operators. HCCL selects topology-aware algorithms that exploit the asymmetric bandwidth between scale-up and scale-out, minimizing cross-rack traffic where bandwidth is constrained. For inference workloads, we developed additional paths: one-sided communication where PEs submit work directly through express doorbells, and device-triggered collectives where compute kernels signal hardware-offloaded communication on a parallel stream without breaking graph execution.</span></p>
<h2><span style="font-weight: 400;">Performance in Production</span></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">HCCL achieves up to 940 GB/s of communication bandwidth within a single rack. On a 150-billion-parameter production-recommendation model running across 40 accelerators, MTIA 300&#8217;s total communication time is 3.9 times faster than the equivalent GPU cluster.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">MTIA 300&#8217;s design enables further co-design strategies: Its 216 GB of HBM3E allows larger local batch sizes (reducing trainer count and communication overhead); its 1:1 CPU-to-accelerator ratio enables CPU offloading of numerically intensive optimizer operations; and its high network bandwidth lets us use higher-precision datatypes to maintain precision. </span></p>
<h2><span style="font-weight: 400;">Looking Ahead</span></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">While MTIA 300 was designed for training recommendation models, the architectural principles—integrated networking, offloaded collective execution, and system-level co-design of compute and communication—position it for a broader set of workloads. As AI inference evolves toward reasoning, agentic, and long-context use cases, the communication demands a shift: Messages become smaller, more frequent, and latency-sensitive, with tighter per-collective budgets. </span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">An architecture that treats the network as a first-class system constraint, optimizing not just bandwidth but also latency and message rate, is well suited to meet these emerging demands. The patterns established in MTIA 300 and HCCL are the foundation for </span><a href="https://ai.meta.com/blog/meta-mtia-scale-ai-chips-for-billions/"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Meta&#8217;s next-generation AI silicon</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">.</span></p>
