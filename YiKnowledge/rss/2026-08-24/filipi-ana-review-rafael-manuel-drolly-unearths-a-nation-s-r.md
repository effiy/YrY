<p><img alt="Filipiñana" class="attachment-thumbnail-large size-thumbnail-large wp-post-image" height="480" src="https://www.slantmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/filipinana-720x480.jpg" width="720" /></p><p>Across <em>Filipiñana</em>, writer-director Rafael Manuel stacks one simulacrum on top of another. The film’s setting is a metaphor for the Philippines in miniature: a golf course inside the urban core of the still-developing country—and one that’s persevering through a drought, no less. Yet the film never feels like the thematic equivalent of putting a hat on a hat because Manuel commits so thoroughly to artifice. It makes a compelling case that the only way to reflect a country’s unsavory reality is to analyze the refractions of its self-image.</p>
<p>Expanding upon his 2020 short of the same name, <em>Filipiñana</em> follows two young women whose class positions might seem to signal that their trajectories will collide. Seventeen-year-old “tee girl” Isabel (Jorrybell Agoto) tries to absorb her new professional surroundings as an <em>ilokana</em> from the rural north of the Philippines brought in to serve a big city golf club that makes most of its money catering to wealthy patrons and Chinese tourists. The visiting Clara (Carmen Castellanos), meanwhile, plays her way through the course with family members constantly imploring her to stop the Filipino brain drain and return from New York for good.</p>

<p>For all their obvious differences, Isabel and Clara are both outsiders. Manuel and cinematographer Xenia Patricia adopt the characters’ sense of displacement and estrangement as the default visual language of <em>Filipiñana</em>. Shot by shot, an unease mounts that sits in tension with the immaculate aesthetics. The perspective of every scene feels warped in some way, from the setting’s scale, which often only crystallizes when a person steps into the shot, to the uncanny eeriness of a symmetrical composition. Often, the most vital action is staged at the very edge of the boxy 4:3 frame, without apparent regard for its narrative importance. The highly subjective sound design complements this visual disorientation throughout.</p>

<p>Manuel’s knack for visual wit and whimsy within largely static frames rivals the precision of Nordic masters like Roy Andersson and Aki Kaurismäki. This sensibility helps highlight everything unnatural unfolding around Isabel and Clara, be it the installation of new “natural” features in the verdant landscape or the creepy choreography of labor meant to make guests feel pampered. Manuel also edited <em>Filipiñana</em>, and the way he prolongs the film’s humorous moments matches the pace of a day on the links—at once languorous and lively.</p>

<p>Manuel’s gaze is sociologically oriented and satirical. The film’s images, often shot in deep focus and from a great enough distance to take in both characters and their surroundings, recall the work of Frederick Wiseman. While highly crafted in the spirit of fiction, <em>Filipiñana</em> captures with arch acuity the ways that institutions impress themselves upon individuals.</p>
<p>Isabel’s journey lays bare the harsh inequalities faced by people like her in the Philippines. Her main task in <em>Filipiñana</em> consists of returning a golf club to Dr. Palanca (Teroy Guzman), the facility’s director who inspires a cult-like reverence from members and employees alike. This unexpectedly long voyage to drop off a MacGuffin lends some propulsion to the narrative through Isabel’s ability to experience different areas of the country club, yet the golf club also doubles as an emblem of the club’s misguided priorities.</p>


<p>Each new sanctum of power and privilege Isabel enters further disillusions her. Each glimpse into the private conversations of the country’s elite shows that these individuals lack any sense of perspective. Manuel’s visuals echo that attitude by distorting the prominence a person or structure takes in the frame relative to their actual size, though his skewed point of view possesses the self-awareness these characters lack. <em>Filipiñana</em> even coyly incorporates this off-kilter outlook into the stakes of the narrative, with overheard whispers cluing Isabel into the real drama underlying the film: a missing girl on the premises.</p>
<p>The full extent of this rot under the surface doesn’t emerge until <em>Filipiñana</em>’s final shot. And once the closing credits begin to roll, Manuel lays bare the unsavory truth and violent foundations of his chosen milieu. It’s an ending that serves as a walloping indictment of all those distracted by superficial concerns—including his own audience.</p>
