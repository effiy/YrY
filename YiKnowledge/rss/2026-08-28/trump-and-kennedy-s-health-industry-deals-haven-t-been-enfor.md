<p>In the thick of his competitive reelection race in Michigan, Republican Rep. <a href="https://barrett.house.gov/media/press-releases/barrett-welcomes-secretary-kennedy-mid-michigan-highlights-efforts-make">Tom Barrett</a> joined Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. at a sprawling 400-acre <a href="https://www.instagram.com/p/DZs-BqXiXJK/">apple orchard, farm, and winery</a>. They touted Trump administration efforts to improve the American diet, including the removal of some artificial dyes from processed foods.</p>



<p>&#8220;We had a great discussion about healthy options for all Americans and taking back control of our healthcare,&#8221; Barrett said in a June <a href="https://www.instagram.com/reel/DaEC_U0iRk6/">Instagram post</a>, after sampling the farm&#8217;s apple cider.</p>



<p>Like the focus on artificial dyes, however, many of the administration&#8217;s highest-profile health initiatives rely on voluntary agreements. The goals, such as lower drug prices and nutrition classes for doctors, have widespread appeal, cutting across party lines and economic divisions.</p>



<p>But the administration-industry deals lack the enforcement teeth of more traditional federal regulation. Their details are vague, and minimal oversight makes it hard to monitor progress. In some cases, the administration has claimed victories that have yet to materialize.</p>



<p>Republicans consider the dealmaking a winning strategy. It fits with the party&#8217;s anti-regulatory stance, they say, and enables the administration to quickly forge agreements President Donald Trump and his allies can tout as accomplishments. In the run-up to the midterm elections, some, like Barrett, hope to woo voters by trumpeting the Trump administration&#8217;s efforts to shape health policy.</p>



<p>The practice also raises questions. Though the deals are announced with great fanfare — often during televised events on stages, with live audiences — there&#8217;s little documentation or follow-through, creating doubts about whether the administration&#8217;s health agenda will lead to lasting change or unravel once the political attention fades.</p>



<p>The distinction could prove important to voters as Republicans defend their health records in November&#8217;s midterm elections.</p>



<p>&#8220;These deals are often not transparent, so there&#8217;s no way for the public to judge how meaningful they are,&#8221; said Larry Levitt, executive vice president for health policy at KFF, a health information nonprofit that includes KFF Health News.</p>



<p><strong>Dealing With Dyes</strong></p>



<p>The push to remove certain artificial dyes from food and drugs, for example, was a headline grabber. In April 2025, Kennedy <a href="https://www.hhs.gov/maha/secretary-kennedy-holds-press-conference-on-food-dyes/index.html">strode onto an HHS stage</a> to announce deals with food makers. He was flanked by young children and mothers holding placards reading &#8220;Make America Healthy Again.&#8221;</p>



<p>He and former FDA commissioner <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marty_Makary">Marty Makary</a> drew a standing ovation from an audience selected by Kennedy&#8217;s staff as they said companies had pledged to phase out all petroleum-based synthetic dyes from the nation&#8217;s food supply and medicines. They targeted nine synthetic dyes for removal.</p>



<p>Voters love the idea of stopping the use of such dyes. In a nationally representative March survey by Consumer Reports, <a href="https://article.images.consumerreports.org/image/upload/v1775830675/prod/content/dam/surveys/Consumer_Reports_AES_March_2026.pdf">72% of adults</a> said they were at least somewhat concerned about synthetic dyes, and two-thirds said companies should be required to phase them out.</p>



<p>A year after making the first announcement at HHS, <a href="https://rumble.com/v77r7yk-full-speech-fireside-chat-with-secretary-robert-f.-kennedy-jr.-at-cpac-2026.html?e9s=src_v1_s%2Csrc_v1_s_o&amp;sci=c4a1d947-a2e5-4ce0-8d73-237adc701d34">Kennedy declared victory</a> during a discussion at the Conservative Political Action Conference, an annual political event.</p>



<p>&#8220;We&#8217;ve gotten rid of the nine synthetic-based food dyes,&#8221; he said.</p>



<p>Not quite. At the initial HHS event, federal officials said companies would voluntarily stop using six specific synthetic dyes <a href="https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/hhs-fda-phase-out-petroleum-based-synthetic-dyes-nations-food-supply">by the end of this year</a>. (The administration has also revoked or proposed revoking authorization for two other synthetic food dyes.)</p>



<p>Later, the FDA on its website quietly <a href="https://www.fda.gov/food/color-additives-information-consumers/tracking-food-industry-pledges-remove-petroleum-based-food-dyes">changed the deadline</a> to the end of 2027. So, most are still in use.</p>



<p>In fact, the FDA posted a list of 27 companies it said had made voluntary pledges as of December 2025 to remove six synthetic dyes from products such as Doritos and Kellogg&#8217;s Froot Loops. More than a year and a half later, <a href="https://www.fda.gov/food/color-additives-information-consumers/tracking-food-industry-pledges-remove-petroleum-based-food-dyes">seven food makers </a>— fewer than 30% of those who bought in — had met their promised goals.</p>



<p>Many major food makers, such as the Coca-Cola Co. and Unilever, have made &#8220;<a href="https://advocacy.consumerreports.org/press_release/one-year-after-rfk-called-for-phase-out-of-harmful-dyes-some-major-food-companies-havent-made-commitments-no-drug-companies-have-pledged-to-act/?utm">no concrete commitments</a>&#8221; to remove the synthetic dyes, according to Consumer Reports. In addition, no pharmaceutical companies have publicly said they have plans to remove dyes from drugs.</p>



<p>&#8220;It&#8217;s just all talk,&#8221; said Leslie Dach, who chairs <a href="https://www.protectourcare.org/">Protect Our Care</a>, a healthcare advocacy group that supports the Affordable Care Act. &#8220;They just govern for a day of publicity, and then it&#8217;s over. None of it happens. Yet the people don&#8217;t know because they have busy lives, so they think, ‘Just look at all these initiatives.&#8217;&#8221;</p>



<p>In fact, the administration <a href="https://www.hhs.gov/press-room/fda-takes-new-approach-no-artificial-colors-claims.html">loosened labeling requirements</a>, allowing companies to say their products contain no artificial colors — as long as they don&#8217;t use petroleum-based dyes. Previously, food makers could not make that claim unless their products contained no added colors. Some food dyes made from natural ingredients can contain contaminants and may pose their own health risks, <a href="https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/6/1067/164756/Food-Coloring-Additives-and-Incidence-of-Type-2">such as diabetes</a>.</p>



<p>&#8220;The federal government hasn&#8217;t taken any regulatory action on food dyes, for the most part, since the beginning of this administration,&#8221; said <a href="https://www.ewg.org/news-insights/our-experts/melanie-benesh">Melanie Benesh</a>, vice president for government affairs at the Environmental Working Group, an advocacy group.</p>



<p>HHS said the voluntary approach has yielded significant action, including commitments to remove synthetic dyes from products sold in schools for the 2026–27 school year.</p>



<p>&#8220;HHS and the FDA are moving forward with clear timelines and concrete industry commitments, with major changes expected in foods served in schools during the coming school year and across full product portfolios by the end of 2027,&#8221; HHS spokesperson Emily Hilliard said in an email.</p>



<p>At the same CPAC convention event, Kennedy said &#8220;the MCAT testing companies are going to put nutrition on the MCAT for the first time, so the students will actually want to do it.&#8221; MCAT refers to the Medical College Admission Test, an exam required for admission to medical schools.</p>



<p>Again, not quite.</p>



<p>The Association of American Medical Colleges administers the MCAT. Spokesperson <a href="https://www.aamc.org/news/press-center">Stuart Heiser</a> said Kennedy misspoke and may have meant to refer to a test taken by students to be licensed as doctors.</p>



<p><strong>An Insurance Deal Falls Short of Promises</strong></p>



<p>Kennedy again took to the HHS stage in June 2025, this time with Centers for Medicare &amp; Medicaid Services Administrator Mehmet Oz, to make what was billed as a game-changing announcement. <a href="https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/press-releases/hhs-secretary-kennedy-cms-administrator-oz-secure-industry-pledge-fix-broken-prior-authorization">Major insurers, they said, had agreed </a>to reduce the volume of healthcare services subject to prior authorization, a practice widely used by the insurance industry that often requires patients or their medical teams to seek preapproval before undergoing treatment.</p>



<p>The administration said 80% of insurers pledged changes to preauthorization requirements for 80% of diseases and injuries <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nCyqTVmBw8Y">by January 2026</a>. The administration also promised &#8220;<a href="https://www.rev.com/transcripts/oz-and-kennedy-make-insurance-announcement">public dashboards</a>&#8221; to track progress.</p>



<p>&#8220;It will happen very quickly,&#8221; Oz said at the event. &#8220;Necessary care will be delivered when it&#8217;s needed, in the right way.&#8221;</p>



<p>As of July, months past that January target date, health plans had reduced prior authorization for medical services <a href="https://kffhealthnews.org/insurance/prior-authorization-insurance-denials-reform-pledge-year-later/">by about 11%</a>, according to AHIP, the insurer trade group. But no public dashboards have debuted to track the deal, and some insurers that signed the pledge last summer told KFF Health News this year that they will not implement all the promised reforms as outlined by AHIP.</p>



<p>Hilliard did not respond to questions about the pace of progress.</p>



<p>The American Medical Association, in a 2025 web-based survey, asked 1,000 practicing doctors whether they believed the voluntary pledges would make a meaningful difference. <a href="https://www.ama-assn.org/practice-management/prior-authorization/ama-prior-authorization-physician-survey">Only 1 in 3</a> said they believed they would.</p>



<p>Insurers made a <a href="https://www.healthcaredive.com/news/health-insurers-pledge-prior-authorization-reform-trump-hhs/751309/">similar promise in 2018</a>, during the previous Trump administration. The next year, more than 80% of doctors said the number of prior authorization requests for drugs and medical services had been increasing, based on <a href="https://allianceforpatientaccess.org/resources/2019-ama-prior-authorization-physician-survey/">another AMA survey</a>.</p>



<p>Meanwhile, the administration is testing an artificial intelligence-powered <a href="https://www.kiplinger.com/retirement/medicare/prior-authorization-coming-to-traditional-medicare">prior authorization system</a> for Medicare, the federal health program for people 65 and older or with disabilities. In six states, Medicare beneficiaries must get preapproval for a few treatments that CMS considers to have little clinical benefit and to be susceptible to fraud or waste, including skin substitutes and knee arthroscopy for arthritis. The program began in January, the same deadline insurers had set for curtailing preauthorization delays.</p>



<p><strong>Deals and Deregulation</strong></p>



<p>The healthcare industry&#8217;s voluntary agreements appeal to voters who feel government regulation drives up costs and places unnecessary burdens on businesses, some supporters say.</p>



<p>&#8220;Secretary Kennedy is the antithesis of a public health industry that uses coercion over communication — and has demonstrated this by taking the time and effort to push voluntary initiatives over the typical approach of governmental mandates,&#8221; said <a href="https://davidmansdoerfer.com/">David Mansdoerfer</a>, a political consultant who was a political appointee at HHS in Trump&#8217;s first term.</p>



<p>But voluntary agreements with the health industry can prove ineffective. Former President Jimmy Carter in 1977 proposed a legislative plan to curb rising hospital costs. Hospitals fought back, and Congress rejected the proposal, instead favoring a <a href="https://www.commonwealthfund.org/sites/default/files/documents/___media_files_publications_blog_davis_stremikis_blog_dec_2009_1221.pdf">voluntary approach</a> desired by the industry. It ultimately failed once public attention faded.</p>



<p>One upside: Deals are fast. <a href="https://regulationroundup.com/explainer/what-is-a-notice-and-comment-rulemaking/">Enacting a federal regulation</a> can take two to three years. And some health analysts say the tempo of the agreements advanced by Kennedy and Trump may help take voters&#8217; attention off the Trump administration&#8217;s inability so far to produce a <a href="https://kffhealthnews.org/courts/back-to-the-future-trumps-history-of-promising-a-health-plan-that-never-comes/">long-promised health plan</a>.</p>



<p>Instead, Republicans can point to the array of accords reached with industry, including the <a href="https://www.whitehouse.gov/research/2026/05/savings-from-most-favored-nation-drug-pricing-policy/">administration&#8217;s voluntary arrangement</a> with drugmakers <a href="https://x.com/KushDesai47/status/2087564084220711168">to cut prices</a> so they&#8217;re in line with lower amounts charged in peer countries. The White House calls it the &#8220;most-favored-nation&#8221; prescription drug pricing policy.</p>



<p>Seventeen companies, including Pfizer and AstraZeneca, <a href="https://www.fiercepharma.com/pharma/touting-529b-savings-over-10-years-white-house-looks-expand-mfn-deals-pharma">announced agreements</a> with the administration to lower prices for Medicaid enrollees and cash-paying consumers using <a href="http://trumprx.gov">TrumpRx</a>, a narrow, government-run consumer platform.</p>



<p>Many details remain unknown, but the lower prices apply only to new drugs and existing drugs available through Medicaid. And prices at TrumpRx aren&#8217;t as low as out-of-pocket prices for most consumers with insurance. But the voluntary deals appeal to an industry that has railed against mandatory approaches drugmakers deride as harmful price controls.</p>



<p>&#8220;Each company makes its own decisions about how it prices medicines, and our industry is committed to working with the Trump administration to ensure Americans have access to affordable medicines,&#8221; said Chanse Jones, a spokesperson for PhRMA, a pharmaceutical industry trade group.</p>



<p>Policies that lead to reductions in drug prices typically worry investors because profits also can drop. But rather than seeing their stock prices fall after the agreements were announced, the drugmakers saw largely <a href="https://www.analysisgroup.com/news-and-events/news/market-responses-to-voluntary-most-favored-nation-deals-examined-by-analysis-group-consultants/">positive market reactions</a>.</p>



<p>Analysts say that&#8217;s partly because the deals are narrow in scope, largely exist only in principle, and don&#8217;t apply to existing drugs used by the more than 200 million Americans with commercial or private health insurance.</p>



<p>The Trump administration, however, is <a href="https://www.whitehouse.gov/releases/2026/08/president-trump-delivers-largest-prescription-drug-price-drop-in-over-60-years/">claiming success</a>.</p>



<p>&#8220;The most-favored-nation agreements on drug prices that we just did are delivering the largest drug price cuts in history,&#8221; Trump said in June at a <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-L5b2RNIH9I">Mack Trucks plant</a> in Pennsylvania. &#8220;That alone should win us the midterms.&#8221;</p>
