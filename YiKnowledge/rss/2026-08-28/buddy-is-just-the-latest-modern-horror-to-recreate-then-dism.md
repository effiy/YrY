<img src="https://img.pastemagazine.com/wp-content/avuploads/2026/08/28111516/buddy-child-head.jpg" /><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The believability of recent horror movies that&#8217;ve mined the past for fright lies partially in their filmmakers&#8217; ability to make the image itself look like a vintage artifact. Many who grew up in the &#8217;80s, &#8217;90s, and early aughts watched our favorite movies before the crisp fidelity of DVDs and Blu-rays took over; when TV screens were curved, not flat. Films like </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Buddy</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, </span><a href="https://www.avclub.com/backrooms-review"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Backrooms</span></i></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">, and </span><a href="https://www.avclub.com/teenage-sex-and-death-at-camp-miasma-review"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Teenage Sex And Death At Camp Miasma</span></i></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">conjure up that aesthetic—on set and on screen—to both create that warm, &#8220;remember when&#8221; feeling and disrupt that illusion.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Casper Kelly&#8217;s </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Buddy</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> relies on looking exactly like a <a href="https://www.avclub.com/buddy-review">vaguely remembered children&#8217;s show</a>. The sets are bright and colorful, almost like an overgrown playground. Objects like the trash can, couch, mailbox, and backpack all talk and feature friendly faces that are then marred and disfigured when the horror is eventually unleashed. The music and dance moves are decidedly old-school for today&#8217;s audience, about 30 years removed from when big, costumed characters hosted kids&#8217; shows. It&#8217;s all disarmingly retro, which makes it all the more absurd when this cheerful place for sing-alongs and hugs turns into a mass grave.&nbsp;</span></p><!-- inlinecontent_1 --> </p><!-- RevContent --> <div id="revcontent-hidden"> <!-- revisit --><!-- admarker --> 
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">To put a bow on the many different shows </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Buddy</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> references—like </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Barney &amp; Friends</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> and </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Pee-wee&#8217;s Playhouse</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">—Kelly filters it through an old TV aesthetic. An upbeat opening number features the kids in the show (back when they were worry-free), scenes shift through transition wipes few editors today would use, and end credits scroll over the closing song, breaking the film up into &#8220;episodes.&#8221; The meta format isn&#8217;t just a part of the movie&#8217;s aesthetics, it&#8217;s a key plot device: Buddy, the disturbed unicorn that hosts the show, needs the end credits to return him to his original state after he&#8217;s damaged by the kids trying to escape. </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Buddy </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">also uses the retro TV look to delineate different spaces. Once the kids are on the other side of Buddy&#8217;s fence, that aesthetic is gone, and they embark on a new journey through a dark, digitally crisp forest.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Likewise, Kane Parsons&#8217; </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Backrooms </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">switches between what looks like film and video formats. Set in the 1990s, </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Backrooms </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">sees characters film a commercial with a camcorder, playing with the gear the way many people used them for no-budget, homegrown ads for local businesses. It&#8217;s a silly moment but, when it comes time to prove the existence of this eerie architectural hell that seemingly doesn&#8217;t end, Clark (Chiwetel Ejiofor) uses that same camera, enlisting his employee and her boyfriend to film the Backrooms. They create a grainy movie-within-the-movie, exploring the space with a hauntingly lo-fi view, channeling movies from the time like </span><a href="https://www.pastemagazine.com/article/autumn-classics-the-blair-witch-project"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Blair Witch Project</span></i></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">—as well as newer films like Kyle Edward Ball&#8217;s </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Skinamarink</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, which turned digital footage into grainy horror—before jolting the audience back to contemporary aesthetics.&nbsp;</span></p><!-- inlinecontent_2 --> </p><!-- admarker --> 
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The </span><a href="https://www.avclub.com/teenage-sex-and-death-at-camp-miasma-movie-within-the-movie-meta"><span style="font-weight: 400;">movie-within-a-movie</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> format also appears in Jane Schoenbrun&#8217;s latest two features, </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">I Saw The TV Glow </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">and </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Camp Miasma</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, playing with the format and visual conventions of &#8217;90s TV shows and &#8217;80s slashers with a twist. For </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">I Saw The TV Glow</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, Schoenbrun created </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Pink Opaque</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, a quasi-</span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Buffy</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> tribute that looks appropriately aged, with characters in retro clothes and sinister villains—all filmed to look like it&#8217;s on tape, with TV bars across the screen to make the sequence look older. Two lonely children bond over their love for </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Pink Opaque</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, the glow from the television lighting up their faces as if restoring life to them through their new obsession. Years later, when Owen (Justice Smith) revisits </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Pink Opaque</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, it</span> <span style="font-weight: 400;">doesn&#8217;t look as cool or scary. It&#8217;s cheesy and goofy, a tangible reflection of how fans grow out of the media they love. The show has lost its glow for Owen, and as he ages, life has drained from him in turn.&nbsp;&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Schoenbrun continues playing with this idea in their latest film, where they created an entire horror movie franchise. The image quality of these fictional older films are made to look aged, with the dark grainy look of something cheaply filmed in the &#8217;80s. As the world of the horror franchise bleeds into its version of reality, the movie begins to feature matte paintings and other practical effects to create classic Hollywood movie magic. Soon, present and past aesthetics begin to combine, with modern camera movements and cartoonish blood splatters following in Little Death&#8217;s wake. The boundaries between reality and the screen are deliriously blurred, like when supporting characters in the town&#8217;s gas station lose their color and clarity as if they had stepped out of a faded TV screen. The most ambitious and complex use of this technique of this recent crop, Schoenbrun’s film recreates an older visual format as both a reference and meta conceit, using recognizable aesthetic details to detach their story from reality.&nbsp;</span></p><!-- admarker --> 
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">For filmmakers, recreating the past look of old recordings and broadcast-quality TV shows is a part of the story they&#8217;re telling, whether that involves an immersive, bizzaro kids’ program or the limits of consumer video technology making a liminal space all the scarier. In a digital age, returning to these older aesthetics, not just in set design but in cinematography, offers an immediate, tactile visual difference from what audiences are used to, which therefore makes these films appear more real than those fully committed to crisp, easily manipulated digital images. Fully returning to that sense of retro terror plays on the audience’s collective memory of that time, which means that any variation or distortion feels even more jarring. The image quality isn&#8217;t as sharp, the colors aren&#8217;t as clear, and childhood memories are no longer sacred.&nbsp;</span></p><!-- inlinecontent_2 --> </p>
