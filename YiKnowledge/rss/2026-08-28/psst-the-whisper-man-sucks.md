<img src="https://img.pastemagazine.com/wp-content/avuploads/2026/08/27172700/whisper-man-header.jpg" /><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">You could think up a less threatening name for a serial child killer than The Whisper Man, but few others would so aptly capture the experience of watching this barely-there thriller. You can hear it alright, but that only makes its sometimes amusing lack of substance more legible. Produced by the Russo brothers for Netflix—which has become a far more threatening phrase than anything uttered by this film&#8217;s sociopaths—</span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Whisper Man </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">is grayer than </span><a href="https://www.pastemagazine.com/movies/netflix/the-gray-man-review"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Gray Man</span></i></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> and more obvious than </span><a href="https://www.avclub.com/the-snowmans-main-character-is-named-harry-hole-and-1819671356"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Snowman</span></i></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">, who gives Mister Police all the clues.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Here, all the clues are given to the audience, while the police mostly seem like they&#8217;re recovering from a collective head injury. Under the command of </span><a href="https://www.avclub.com/the-rule-of-jenny-pen-review"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Rule Of Jenny Pen</span></i></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> director James Ashcroft, a trio of interconnected characters search for a murderer who&#8217;s started snatching kids in the same fashion as a prior killer, caught decades ago. There&#8217;s Amanda (Michelle Monaghan), the detective assigned to the current case; widowed author Tom (Adam Scott), whose son Jake (Acston Luca Porto) gets snatched as soon as they move back Tom&#8217;s New Jersey hometown; and retired detective Pete (Robert De Niro), the initial Whisper Man investigator and Tom&#8217;s estranged dad, called back up for one more job—and this time, it&#8217;s personal.</span></p><!-- inlinecontent_1 --> </p><!-- admarker --> 
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The writerly web linking everyone and everything is overt, with utilitarian dialogue that whacks backstory and murder victims with the same bluntness. You won&#8217;t ever be confused, not about the culprit (who lures lonely kids outside by whispering to them through their windows and/or mail slots) and not about whether Tom&#8217;s wife is dead. But since the film condenses Alex North&#8217;s novel into 105 pre-credits minutes, there&#8217;s just not a lot of time to set up a multigenerational cat-and-mouse chase that frequently detours into strained paternal relationships.&nbsp;</span></p><!-- RevContent --> <div id="revcontent-hidden"> <!-- revisit -->
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Attempting to do so anyway, where the narrative numbers are nearly visible under the paint in every scene, traps a capable cast in a contrived story. There&#8217;s not much room to build suspense when you&#8217;ve also got to fit in Jake&#8217;s imaginary friend (it doesn&#8217;t matter), a clear red herring suspect, and a silly little Whisper Man rhyme, akin to those that summon demons like </span><a href="https://www.avclub.com/no-you-seriously-shouldn-t-think-about-the-bye-bye-man-1798189999"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Bye Bye Man</span></i></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> in schlocky horror movies. And that&#8217;s not even mentioning what might be the first-ever case of Chekhov&#8217;s leaking roof. Actually there&#8217;s a lot of heavy lifting done by the ceilings in this film, as, in a representative example of </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Whisper Man</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8216;s depth of insight, an oft-plastered-over crack stands in for the fractured relationship between Pete and Tom.</span></p><!-- inlinecontent_2 --> </p><!-- admarker --> 
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Talking about ceilings is only a degree or so off from describing </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Whisper Man</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> as watching paint dry, which would give the false impression that anything in the film had color. Its bleak color palette is as generic and predictable as the plot, and as frustrating as the protagonists&#8217; actions. One can&#8217;t expect a film character to act perfectly rationally, but Tom, wandering about in his desaturated world, seems to simply forget about some major breaches of home security and an </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">uncovered secret room</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> in his new basement. Cynically, it can feel like he&#8217;s designed to be yelled at from the comfort of the living room couch. More realistically, it seems like there&#8217;s a lot of movie missing around him.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">What&#8217;s left is a couple run-ins with creeps, including the incarcerated Whisper Man himself (Michael Keaton, squirrely as ever) and a cheeseball murderabilia collector caricatured by Hamish Linklater. Every once in a while, Scott will say &#8220;The Whisper Man&#8221; out loud and we&#8217;ll all have a good laugh. One scene, where he shouts it over and over again, is more fun than the rest of the movie combined. Though Monaghan is in serviceable cop procedural mode and De Niro isn&#8217;t as worn-out as he could be, it&#8217;s really just Owen Teague, who does a passable Anthony Perkins bit, who comes out looking ok. The rest—from the half-hearted investigation to the truly sad action climax (Who can be expected to pretend that 83-year-old Robert De Niro is fistfighting anyone?)—is like trying to watch a more competent drama over a bad internet connection, everything chugging along in a pixelated 144p.</span></p>
<p><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Whisper</span></i> <i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Man</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> never finds even the B-movie thrills of a similarly close-to-home child-abduction drama like </span><a href="https://www.avclub.com/prisoners-1798177987"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Prisoners</span></i></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">, instead flipping through its airport novel plot and discarding it as such without much thought. More disheartening, it never allows its capable cast any room to work within the intensity that comes with the territory, reducing all their emotions to a dull murmur.</span></p><!-- admarker -->
