<p>Horror fans have no shortage of movies to choose from, but the films that stay with you aren&#8217;t always the ones dominating the biggest streaming lists. Some of the genre&#8217;s most unsettling experiences are hidden in cult classics, international horror movies, psychological thrillers, and overlooked films that never received the attention they deserved. That&#8217;s what this list is about.</p>



<p>These movies cover a wide range of horror, from supernatural nightmares and haunted house stories to home invasion brutality, folk horror, psychological terror, and movies that are difficult to classify at all. Some are genuinely obscure. Others, such as <em>Suspiria</em> and <em>Silent Hill</em>, have passionate fan bases but remain surprisingly easy for casual moviegoers to overlook.</p>



<p>The goal isn&#8217;t to claim that these are objectively the ten scariest movies ever made. Horror is too subjective for that. Instead, these are ten films that can still unsettle, disturb or genuinely frighten viewers, and that deserve to be discovered by anyone who thinks they&#8217;ve already seen everything the genre has to offer. If you&#8217;re looking for something beyond the usual horror recommendations, start here.</p>



<figure class="wp-block-image size-large"><img alt="Session 9" class="wp-image-813902" height="538" src="https://www.joblo.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/session-9-1024x538.jpg" width="1024" /></figure>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-1-session-9">#1 &#8211; SESSION 9</h2>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Release Year:</strong> 2001<br /><strong>Director:</strong> Brad Anderson<br /><strong>Country:</strong> United States<br /><strong>Horror:</strong> Psychological Horror / Supernatural Horror<br /><strong>Why It’s Scary:</strong> The abandoned Danvers State Hospital creates an atmosphere of isolation and dread, while unsettling recordings and the characters’ deteriorating mental states make it increasingly difficult to distinguish psychological breakdown from something genuinely supernatural.<br /><strong>Who Should Watch It:</strong> Fans of slow-burn psychological horror, haunted locations, ambiguous supernatural stories, and movies like <em>The Shining</em> and <em>The Blackcoat’s Daughter</em>.</li>
</ul>



<p><a href="https://www.joblo.com/tag/brad-anderson">Brad Anderson</a>, the director behind <em>The Machinist</em> and <em>Transsiberian</em>, has worked in horror on both the big screen and the small. But none of his films echoes the psychological power of <em>Session 9</em>. Set inside the abandoned Danvers State Hospital, the movie follows an asbestos removal crew whose seemingly routine job gradually becomes something far more unsettling.</p>



<p>What makes <em>Session 9</em> so effective is that virtually nothing happens on screen to make you think there are ghosts or supernatural powers at play. Instead, Anderson builds an atmosphere of isolation and unease, allowing the decaying hospital, strange recordings, and increasingly unstable characters to get inside your head. You may not be able to point to exactly what is frightening you, but you will still leave this movie feeling haunted and uneasy.</p>



<p>It is a great movie that remains criminally underseen and will definitely appeal to fans of any type of horror, particularly those who appreciate psychological dread over constant jump scares or gore. <em>Session 9</em> is the kind of horror movie that lingers in your mind after the credits roll. This is a movie everyone should see.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-2-suspiria">#2 &#8211; SUSPIRIA</h2>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Release Year:</strong> 1977<br /><strong>Director:</strong> Dario Argento<br /><strong>Country:</strong> Italy<br /><strong>Horror:</strong> Supernatural Horror / Giallo<br /><strong>Why It’s Scary:</strong> Argento transforms a German dance academy into a nightmare of vivid colors, sinister architecture, graphic violence, eerie music, and supernatural menace. The film&#8217;s dreamlike logic makes even seemingly ordinary moments feel dangerous.<br /><strong>Who Should Watch It:</strong> Fans of atmospheric supernatural horror, Italian horror, occult movies, stylish filmmaking, and classics like <em>The Exorcist</em> and <em>The Wicker Man</em>.</li>
</ul>



<p>I am a big proponent and fan of 1970s Italian horror films from directors like Lucio Fulci and Dario Argento. These movies are unlike anything else being made around the world, and their stunningly violent effects remain impressive to this day. But even among the many great Italian horror films of the era, <em>Suspiria</em> stands apart.</p>



<p>Dario Argento&#8217;s 1977 masterpiece follows an American ballet student who arrives at a prestigious dance academy in Germany and quickly discovers that something is terribly wrong with the school. Argento turns the academy into a nightmare world of vivid colors, unsettling architecture, strange sounds, and sudden bursts of graphic violence. The result is a movie that feels less like a conventional horror story and more like a beautifully constructed nightmare.</p>



<p><em>Suspiria</em> consistently ranks among the scariest movies of all time, and yet I still meet tons of people who have never seen it. Even if you&#8217;ve somehow managed to avoid it for nearly five decades, this is the perfect time to fix that. Do yourself a favor and check out the works of <a href="https://www.joblo.com/tag/dario-argento">Dario Argento</a> as soon as you can. <em>Suspiria</em> is the obvious place to start, but his filmography offers plenty more for anyone who falls under the spell of his particular brand of horror. Just trust me when I say that <em>Suspiria</em> is the one you absolutely need to see.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-3-the-town-that-dreaded-sundown">#3 &#8211; THE TOWN THAT DREADED SUNDOWN</h2>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Release Year:</strong> 2014<br /><strong>Director:</strong> Alfonso Gomez-Rejon<br /><strong>Country:</strong> United States<br /><strong>Horror:</strong> Slasher / Meta-Horror / True-Crime Horror<br /><strong>Why It’s Scary:</strong> The film blurs the line between reality and fiction by treating the 1976 <em>The Town That Dreaded Sundown</em> as a movie that exists within its own story. The result is a disturbing slasher that turns a decades-old true crime legend into a self-aware modern nightmare.<br /><strong>Who Should Watch It:</strong> Fans of slashers, true-crime-inspired horror, meta-horror, and movies that play with the boundary between fiction and reality.</li>
</ul>



<p>The original 1976 version of <em>The Town That Dreaded Sundown</em> is a cult classic in its own right, inspired by a series of murders that terrorized Texarkana in the 1940s. But the 2014 film is something much stranger. It is a sequel, but it&#8217;s also a remake. Well, sort of.</p>



<p>The 2014 <em>The Town That Dreaded Sundown</em> treats the original movie as if it actually exists within the film&#8217;s world, with the 1976 movie serving as the starting point for a new series of killings. In other words, the filmmakers essentially made a meta-sequel that revisits the original story while simultaneously reimagining it. Trying to explain exactly how it works makes it sound much more complicated than it actually is.</p>



<p>And that&#8217;s part of the fun. The film takes the legend of the Phantom Killer and gives it a modern slasher treatment while playing around with the audience&#8217;s expectations about remakes, sequels, and true-crime movies. Never mind my explanation. Just watch this movie.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-4-inside">#4 &#8211; INSIDE</h2>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Release Year:</strong> 2007<br /><strong>Directors:</strong> Alexandre Bustillo &amp; Julien Maury<br /><strong>Country:</strong> France<br /><strong>Horror:</strong> Home-Invasion Horror / Body Horror<br /><strong>Why It’s Scary:</strong> <em>Inside</em> combines the claustrophobia of a home invasion thriller with exceptionally graphic violence and the terrifying vulnerability of its pregnant protagonist. Its relentless pacing leaves almost no room for relief once the attack begins.<br /><strong>Who Should Watch It:</strong> Fans of extreme horror, French New Extremity cinema, home invasion movies, and intensely graphic films. <strong>Not recommended for viewers who are sensitive to pregnancy-related violence or extreme gore.</strong></li>
</ul>



<p>Some movies have earned the disclaimer of being hard to watch, and <em>Inside</em> is definitely one of them. This brutal French horror movie follows a pregnant woman who is alone in her home when another woman arrives with a terrifying obsession: she wants the woman&#8217;s unborn child.</p>



<p>The premise alone is disturbing, but <em>Inside</em> wastes very little time turning that setup into an exceptionally intense home-invasion nightmare. The film is relentless, claustrophobic, and extremely gory, making it one of those horror movies where the warning that it is &#8220;hard to watch&#8221; is absolutely justified. The cover art in some countries features a large pair of scissors, and I will not say more than that.</p>



<p>Originally released in 2007, <em>Inside</em> is one of the standout examples of the wave of extreme French horror that emerged during the 2000s. It isn&#8217;t simply trying to shock you with graphic violence, either. Beneath all the blood is a remarkably effective survival thriller that keeps escalating until there is almost no room left to breathe. Disturbing and gory, this is also a phenomenal horror flick. Just be prepared for a movie that earns every bit of its reputation for being difficult to watch.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-5-the-house-of-the-devil">#5 &#8211; THE HOUSE OF THE DEVIL</h2>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Release Year:</strong> 2009<br /><strong>Director:</strong> Ti West<br /><strong>Country:</strong> United States<br /><strong>Horror:</strong> Occult Horror / Satanic Horror / Slow-Burn Horror<br /><strong>Why It’s Scary:</strong> Ti West deliberately recreates the look and pacing of late-1970s and early-1980s horror, allowing tension to build through isolation, silence, strange behavior, and mounting suspicion. The movie understands that anticipation can be more frightening than seeing the threat itself.<br /><strong>Who Should Watch It:</strong> Fans of <em>Rosemary’s Baby</em>, <em>The Exorcist</em>, satanic horror, retro filmmaking, and patient slow-burn movies that reward viewers who let the tension build.</li>
</ul>



<p>Those in the know have long recognized the name of director Ti West, but these days, he&#8217;s hardly a secret within the horror community. Since the original publication of this list, West has gone from a cult filmmaker to one of the most recognizable names in modern horror, thanks in large part to films like <em>The Sacrament</em>, <em>X</em>, <em>Pearl</em>, and <em>MaXXXine</em>. Before all of that, however, West had already delivered one of his most effective horror movies with <em>The House of the Devil</em>.</p>



<p>Released in 2009, the film is a loving throwback to the exploitation and satanic horror movies of the 1970s and early 1980s. West deliberately recreates the look, pacing, and atmosphere of that era, building tension slowly rather than relying on a constant stream of jump scares. The result is a movie that feels like a forgotten VHS-era horror classic that somehow slipped through a time portal.</p>



<p>The film also brings cult favorites like Tom Noonan and Mary Woronov into a story that echoes everything from <em>Rosemary&#8217;s Baby</em> to <em>The Exorcist</em>. West&#8217;s patience is a major part of the movie&#8217;s effectiveness: the longer the story goes without fully revealing what is happening, the more uncomfortable you become waiting for the other shoe to drop. <em>The House of the Devil</em> remains one of the best examples of West&#8217;s ability to take familiar horror ideas and make them feel fresh. If you haven&#8217;t seen it yet, this is one of those overlooked genre gems that deserves to be moved much higher on your watchlist.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-6-deathdream">#6 &#8211; DEATHDREAM</h2>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Release Year:</strong> 1974<br /><strong>Director:</strong> Bob Clark<br /><strong>Country:</strong> United States / Canada<br /><strong>Horror:</strong> Zombie Horror / Psychological Horror / Body Horror<br /><strong>Why It’s Scary:</strong> Bob Clark turns the return of a supposedly dead soldier into a bleak story about war, trauma, family denial, and physical decay. The horror becomes increasingly disturbing as the protagonist&#8217;s return home proves far more terrifying than his death.<br /><strong>Who Should Watch It:</strong> Fans of vintage zombie movies, psychological horror, socially conscious horror, Bob Clark, and films that use supernatural horror to explore real-world trauma.</li>
</ul>



<p>Bob Clark may be forever remembered for his beloved holiday classic <em>A Christmas Story</em>, but the late filmmaker also made some truly memorable horror films. From <em>Black Christmas</em> to <em>Children Shouldn&#8217;t Play with Dead Things</em>, Clark repeatedly found ways to make movies that were genuinely scary while bringing something distinctive to familiar genre ideas.</p>



<p><em>Deathdream</em> is by far his least remembered horror movie, which is a shame because it deserves to be watched. Released in 1974, the film takes the familiar idea of a soldier returning home from war and turns it into a bleak, disturbing nightmare. Andy Brooks is presumed dead in Vietnam, only to unexpectedly return home to his family. But something about him has changed, and the longer he remains there, the more disturbing his presence becomes.</p>



<p>What makes <em>Deathdream</em> particularly memorable is the way it blends zombie and vampire lore into something that feels entirely its own. Clark uses the supernatural story to create an unsettling portrait of death, family denial, and the lingering effects of war, while Tom Savini&#8217;s early special effects work adds some genuinely gruesome imagery. <em>Deathdream</em> may not have the name recognition of <em>Black Christmas</em>, but it is a fascinating and terrifying piece of Bob Clark&#8217;s filmography. If you&#8217;re a fan of Clark&#8217;s better-known work and haven&#8217;t seen this one, you&#8217;re missing one of his most interesting horror movies.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-7-the-orphanage">#7 &#8211; THE ORPHANAGE</h2>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Release Year:</strong> 2007<br /><strong>Director:</strong> J.A. Bayona<br /><strong>Country:</strong> Spain<br /><strong>Horror:</strong> Supernatural Horror / Haunted-House Horror / Psychological Horror<br /><strong>Why It’s Scary:</strong> The film combines traditional ghost story imagery with an intensely emotional story about motherhood, childhood and grief. Its quiet atmosphere and increasingly disturbing revelations make the supernatural elements feel tragic as well as frightening.<br /><strong>Who Should Watch It:</strong> Fans of atmospheric ghost stories, emotional supernatural horror, haunted houses, Guillermo del Toro&#8217;s films, and movies like <em>The Devil’s Backbone</em> and <em>The Others</em>.</li>
</ul>



<p>Produced by Guillermo del Toro and directed by J.A. Bayona, <em>The Orphanage</em> is like <em>Pan&#8217;s Labyrinth</em> crossed with <em>The Devil&#8217;s Backbone</em>, with a little <em>Crimson Peak</em> thrown in for good measure. Those comparisons should tell you almost everything you need to know about the kind of haunting experience you&#8217;re in for, but this 2007 Spanish-language horror movie has a distinctive identity all its own.</p>



<p>The story follows Laura, a woman who returns to the former orphanage where she grew up with her husband and their adopted son, Simón. What begins as an attempt to turn the building into a home for disabled children gradually becomes something much darker when Simón begins talking about an imaginary friend named Tomás. From there, the film slowly transforms the old orphanage into a deeply unsettling haunted house.</p>



<p>A slow burn of a film, <em>The Orphanage</em> has some truly unsettling imagery that will creep you out long after the credits roll. Bayona is remarkably patient about revealing what is really happening, allowing the building itself, the strange children&#8217;s games, and the growing sense of grief to do much of the work. The result is a ghost story that can be frightening one moment and heartbreaking the next. And yes, watch out for Tomás. You won&#8217;t forget him.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-8-pontypool">#8 &#8211; PONTYPOOL</h2>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Release Year:</strong> 2008<br /><strong>Director:</strong> Bruce McDonald<br /><strong>Country:</strong> Canada<br /><strong>Horror:</strong> Psychological Horror / Zombie Horror / Pandemic Horror<br /><strong>Why It’s Scary:</strong> <em>Pontypool</em> creates terror from an extraordinary idea: what if a virus could be transmitted through language? By confining most of the story to a radio station, the film forces viewers to experience the outbreak through fragmented reports, sounds and the characters&#8217; increasingly desperate attempts to understand what is happening.<br /><strong>Who Should Watch It:</strong> Fans of unconventional zombie movies, contained horror, psychological thrillers, high-concept horror, and films that find terrifying possibilities in ordinary things.</li>
</ul>



<p>Those wacky Canadians sure do love their horror! A lot of the best genre work comes out of the Great White North, including this wonderfully strange 2008 movie. Starring Stephen McHattie as radio DJ Grant Mazzy, <em>Pontypool</em> follows a mysterious plague as it spreads through the small Ontario town of the same name. The catch? The infection is transmitted through the English language. Sounds nuts, and it is. But that&#8217;s exactly what makes <em>Pontypool</em> so effective. </p>



<p>Rather than turning the outbreak into a conventional zombie movie filled with hordes of infected people, director Bruce McDonald keeps most of the action inside a radio station. Grant and his coworkers learn about the rapidly unfolding crisis through phone calls, reports from the streets, and increasingly disturbing information coming through their microphones. That limited perspective makes the movie surprisingly claustrophobic. You rarely see the full extent of the outbreak, so your imagination has to fill in the gaps. The idea that something as ordinary and unavoidable as language could become dangerous gives <em>Pontypool</em> an especially unsettling quality, while McHattie&#8217;s performance as the increasingly bewildered DJ gives the movie plenty of personality.</p>



<p>A unique movie and definitely one worth checking out. If you think you&#8217;ve seen every possible variation on the zombie or pandemic movie, <em>Pontypool</em> still has a very different story to tell.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-9-kill-list">#9 &#8211; KILL LIST</h2>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Release Year:</strong> 2011<br /><strong>Director:</strong> Ben Wheatley<br /><strong>Country:</strong> United Kingdom<br /><strong>Horror:</strong> Folk Horror / Psychological Horror / Crime Horror<br /><strong>Why It’s Scary:</strong> <em>Kill List</em> begins like a gritty crime thriller before gradually revealing something much darker underneath. Its increasingly disturbing atmosphere, fractured family dynamics, brutal violence and shocking descent into folk horror make the movie feel as though it is constantly moving toward an inevitable nightmare.<br /><strong>Who Should Watch It:</strong> Fans of folk horror, cult movies, crime thrillers, psychological horror, and films like <em>The Wicker Man</em>, <em>A Field in England</em>, and <em>The Invitation</em>.</li>
</ul>



<p>Ben Wheatley was slowly making a name for himself in the States when <em>Kill List</em> arrived, but in England he was already known for his wildly varied output. This 2011 film is one of the best examples of just how unpredictable Wheatley could be. It starts in one place and keeps moving somewhere darker, stranger, and more disturbing.</p>



<p><em>Kill List</em> plays like a cross between <em>The Wicker Man</em> and <em>In Bruges</em>, which sounds like an odd combination until you actually see the movie. The story follows Jay, a former soldier turned hitman who takes on a new assignment after struggling with problems in his marriage and career. What initially looks like a fairly straightforward contract-killing thriller gradually reveals something much more sinister lurking underneath.</p>



<p>If movies about cults mess with you, this movie will rocket to the top of your list. Wheatley keeps the audience off balance by mixing crime, domestic drama, brutal violence, psychological horror, and increasingly disturbing folk horror elements. The less you know about where the story is going, the better, so this is one where going in relatively blind is highly recommended. <em>Kill List</em> is scary as hell and will stick with you for a long time. It is the kind of horror movie that becomes more disturbing the more you think about it after the credits roll.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-10-silent-hill">#10 &#8211; SILENT HILL</h2>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Release Year:</strong> 2006<br /><strong>Director:</strong> Christophe Gans<br /><strong>Country:</strong> Canada / France / United States<br /><strong>Horror:</strong> Supernatural Horror / Survival Horror / Video Game Horror<br /><strong>Why It’s Scary:</strong> Christophe Gans brings the oppressive atmosphere of the <em>Silent Hill</em> games to the screen through fog-shrouded streets, grotesque creatures, disturbing religious imagery, Pyramid Head, and the terrifying transition into the rust-covered alternate version of the town.<br /><strong>Who Should Watch It:</strong> Fans of video game adaptations, supernatural horror, creature features, disturbing visual imagery, and atmospheric movies like <em>The Mist</em> and <em>Jacob’s Ladder</em>.</li>
</ul>



<p>Director Christophe Gans fully delivered on the creepy factor with <em>Silent Hill</em>, his 2006 adaptation of the PlayStation video game series. Replete with Pyramid Head, faceless nurses, grotesque creatures, and a bunch of disturbing visuals, the movie succeeds at something that many video-game adaptations struggle with: it captures the atmosphere of its source material.</p>



<p>Rather than simply borrowing the name and characters from the games, Gans embraces the oppressive mood of <em>Silent Hill</em>. The seemingly abandoned town is perpetually covered in fog, the sirens signal a terrifying transformation, and the characters are repeatedly confronted with nightmarish creatures and disturbing imagery. When the world shifts into its darker, rust-covered alternate form, the movie becomes a full-blown descent into nightmare territory.</p>



<p><em>Silent Hill</em> is one of the best video-game adaptations and still doesn&#8217;t get the respect it deserves. It isn&#8217;t a perfect movie, and some of its mythology can become convoluted, but its visual design, atmosphere, creature work, and commitment to the disturbing aesthetic of the games remain impressive. The follow-ups are pretty awful, but don&#8217;t let that discourage you from checking out the original. You will want to sleep with the light on after watching this flick.</p>
