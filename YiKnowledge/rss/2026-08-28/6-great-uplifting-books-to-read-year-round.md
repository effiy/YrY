<p>To refer to specific books as “uplifting” is, of course, subjective. What might be uplifting and/or comforting to you might not be the case for me. Indeed, while researching this very list, I had the thought that perhaps I’m too cynical a person for the assignment. But as I dug deeper, I began to realize that there are plenty of books that I’ve read that I have found uplifting or heartwarming–they might fly under the radar of what’s typically considered uplifting. I was also surprised to find a few titles I haven’t read that might actually melt my cold, bitter heart should I choose to pick them up (stay tuned).</p>



<p>All of which to say, the world can be a negative and overwhelming place. Who are we without the things we turn to for comfort? Be it a book, a movie, a song, or a TV show, they help us escape our hectic planet for just a moment. </p>



<p>In the case of books, there is no shortage of titles to choose from when it comes to comfort reads. As far as this list is concerned, I chose books that would typically fall under the label “uplifting, even if they might be absent from other lists in the same category. <p>One could argue Elizabeth von Arnim was considerably ahead of her time. She wrote under multiple pen names or published anonymously, allegedly to avoid upstaging her aristocratic husbands. <em>The Enchanted April </em>is considered her most lighthearted and euphoric work, inspired by a month-long vacation the author spent in the Italian Riviera. </p>



<p>The novel follows four women in the 1920s who are dissatisfied with their everyday lives and meet each other through a London newspaper ad on a February afternoon, and their escape begins. Their escape? A medieval Italian castle in Portofino offering plenty of much-needed solitude. What these women don’t expect to find is a route back to themselves and their true natures, rediscovering their sense of joy in the process. If only the same transformation could be worked on their husbands. </p>



<p>A bestseller in both the United Kingdom and the United States, <em>The Enchanted April </em>has twice been adapted for film and as a Broadway play.</p>

<p>The publisher describes this book as “for people who don’t know who they are without the books they love,” which to me is just the definition of an uplifting read. </p>



<p>Libby Weeks is the high-earning author of <em>The Falling Children</em>, a bestselling fantasy series. Only the world doesn’t actually know her as Libby; they know her as F.T. Goldhero, a pseudonym made up to protect her privacy. The world is also impatient to know how the beloved series will end. </p>



<p>When the last manuscript is already months overdue, Libby is diagnosed with early-onset Alzheimer’s disease. The author’s symptoms advance quickly, as does her already-crippling anxiety. After an incident in which she leaves her dog at the dog park and almost reveals her true identity to the journalist who returns him home, Libby realizes she’ll need help to bring her incredibly famous book series to a close. </p>



<p>That doesn’t seem like much of a problem once she turns to Peanut Bixton, a precarious 11-year-old who eats, sleeps, and breathes <em>The Falling Children </em>books, which provide her a sense of stability and validation in a world committed to misunderstanding her.
