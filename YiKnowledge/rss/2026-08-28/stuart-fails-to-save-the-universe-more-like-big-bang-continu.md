<img src="https://img.pastemagazine.com/wp-content/avuploads/2026/08/27171917/kunal-nayyar.jpg" /><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The far-fetched, genre-shifting premise of </span><a href="https://www.avclub.com/hbo-max-renews-stuart-fails-to-save-the-universe-for-season-2"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Stuart Fails To Save The Universe </span></i></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">attempts to give four of </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Big Bang Theory</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">’s side characters richer lives and storylines than those they had on the original series. The sci-fi comedy props up the introverted underdog Stuart Bloom (Kevin Sussman), who, tasked with stopping a multiversal doomsday, brings Denise (Lauren Lapkus), Bert (Brian Posehn), and Barry Kripke (John Ross Bowie) along for the ride. As the quartet hops between dimensions, series co-creators Chuck Lorre, Bill Prady, and Zak Penn essentially say, &#8220;See, these people are more than their pop-culture obsessions, poor fashion senses, and caricatured way of speaking!&#8221; Their roles expanded from B-plots and guest appearances so they can be more fully realized. And yet, </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Stuart </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">fails</span> <span style="font-weight: 400;">to extend the same courtesy to a character who played a vastly more prominent role on </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Big Bang Theory</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Big Bang </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">star Kunal Nayyar briefly pops up as astrophysicist Raj Koothrappali in </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Stuart</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">’s sixth and latest episode, ranting and raving about collapsing galaxies on a Pasadena street corner. No one takes him seriously, though—not even Stuart and Denise, who’ve spent the entire series seeing realities blink out of existence. The couple hears, but doesn&#8217;t heed</span><span style="font-weight: 400;">, his warnings, dismissing him just like the show&#8217;s powers-that-be. Nayyar appears in two </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Stuart</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> episodes (so far), and they each, in their own way, remind us </span><a href="https://www.avclub.com/the-big-bang-theory-the-russian-rocket-reaction-1798170029"><span style="font-weight: 400;">just how poorly <i>The Big Bang Theory </i>always treated his character</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">. </span><span style="font-weight: 400;">The spin-off</span> <span style="font-weight: 400;">continues the tradition of making Raj—the franchise&#8217;s only protagonist of color—the butt of the joke.&nbsp;</span></p><!-- admarker --> <!-- inlinecontent_1 --> </p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">For 12 seasons of </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Big Bang Theory</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, Nayyar&#8217;s ace comedic timing was put in service of countless harmful, racist jokes, and Raj remained a relatively irksome, one-dimensional hodgepodge of broad South Asian clichés: an exaggerated accent, multiple subplots involving arranged marriage, social awkwardness, his friends exoticizing India as a land of snake-charmers—you name it. The guy couldn&#8217;t even talk to women if he wasn&#8217;t drunk for half of the show&#8217;s run. And in the end, his most genuine connections were with his pet dog, a </span><a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hAu3uVstPFA"><span style="font-weight: 400;">phone app,</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> and, of course, Howard Wolowitz (Simon Helberg). I particularly loathe the season-five punchline Amy (Mayim Bialik) uses to pacify Penny (Kaley Cuoco) following what she thinks was a drunken hookup with Raj: Amy likens the coupling to Russian ruler Catherine The Great allegedly participating in &#8220;interspecies hanky-panky&#8221; with her horse. Amy, in her heightened, socially-awkward-sitcom-character way, is trying to assuage Penny&#8217;s guilt over sleeping with a friend who&#8217;s also a friend of her ex Leonard (Johnny Galecki). But anyone unfamiliar with the character&#8217;s dating history (or the show&#8217;s similarly retrograde treatment of Penny&#8217;s active sex life) would be forgiven for thinking </span><span style="font-weight: 400;">that </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Big Bang Theory </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">is equating “shagging a little Indian boy” with bestiality.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">To make matters worse, </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Big Bang</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8216;s series finale brings everyone’s story to a satisfying conclusion—everyone’s, that is, except Raj’s. Howard and Leonard have fulfilling families and careers, while a happily married Sheldon (Jim Parsons) earns a coveted Nobel Prize alongside his wife, Amy. As for Raj, always portrayed as the true romantic-at-heart among the show’s men? He ends up alone, working two jobs at Caltech and the Griffith Observatory, and his biggest accomplishment involves sitting next to Sarah Michelle Gellar at the Nobel ceremony.&nbsp;</span></p><!-- admarker --> <!-- inlinecontent_2 --> </p><!-- RevContent --> <div id="revcontent-hidden"> <!-- revisit -->
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">So color me surprised when Raj was the first main character to pop up in </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Stuart Saves The Universe</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">. Was the spin-off about to rewrite a glaring error from its parent series? Was Raj about to have a storyline not tied to his ethnicity in some way? Would he finally be allowed to have more than </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">a single defining trait</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">?&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Well, no. Even in an infinite number of alternate realities, Raj is mostly a one-note loner who either gets killed or disrespected. At least the series premiere’s version of Raj gets to tell Stuart that he got married before his world was destroyed and Kripke took over and imprisoned him. Still, it&#8217;s nothing more than a throwaway line without any additional context.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">It&#8217;s disappointing because, as previously mentioned, </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Stuart </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">grants </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Big Bang</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8216;s oft-forgotten supporting players an actual voice, personality, and story. The HBO Max series gives them well-earned nuance: Stuart and Denise&#8217;s romance occupies a chunk of the season, as does Kripke&#8217;s redemption arc, and Bert&#8217;s journey of self-discovery and confidence (like when the gang land in a universe where the soft-spoken geologist is a sex-magnet magician).&nbsp;</span></p><!-- admarker --> 
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Meanwhile, other returning </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Big Bang </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">leads are treated to similarly enticing destinies. In episode two, Penny drops in as the leader of a resilience group trying to overthrow an AI-led government; Bernadette (Melissa Rauch) is a cool but evil demon in episode three; and Amy is a considerate psychiatrist in the fourth outing, consoling Stuart when he tries to explain his multiversal predicament. Heck, even Sheldon’s arch-nemesis Wil Wheaton gets a fun arc in which he helps Stuart and the gang destroy the fiendish, extra-tall Bernadette as she chomps on the city. Compared to all of them, Raj gets—as he always has—bupkis.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">When </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Big Bang Theory </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">debuted in 2007, American TV was struggling to accurately reflect multi-dimensional and non-stereotypical South Asian characters (with rare exceptions in </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">ER </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">and </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Office</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">). A multi-camera CBS sitcom with a British-born lead of Indian descent felt fairly inventive—as inventive as a multi-camera CBS sitcom poking fun at general nerdiness at a time before superheroes and video games had fully infiltrated the mainstream. Over time, </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Big Bang </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">lost its appeal because the humor—along with Raj&#8217;s character—felt dated. As it turns out, almost 20 years later, he remains a disappointingly lazy sitcom punchline.&nbsp;</span></p>
