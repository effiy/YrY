<p><img alt="" class="attachment-thumbnail-large size-thumbnail-large wp-post-image" height="480" src="https://www.slantmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/safe4k-720x480.jpg" width="720" /></p><p><img alt="Safe" class="alignleft wp-image-50018619" src="/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/safe4k.jpg" width="315" />Pay attention to Fulvia (Martha Velez) in Todd Haynes’s <em>Safe</em> as she casts a confused, sideways glance at Carol (Julianne Moore), who reacts to seeing a wrongly delivered couch as if her child’s battered body were strewn across the floor. Haynes isolates Fulvia in a brief shot as Carol “telephonos” a San Fernando Valley furniture joint, lending silent but privileged access to Fulvia’s feelings of alienation from Carol’s sense of entitlement. Yet Haynes’s subtle reversal here is that Carol is the alien, not Fulvia, who actually lords over the domestic duties of the home, tending to Carol’s stepson, Rory (Chauncey Leopardi), and satiating Carol’s “milkaholic” desires on demand. Note the way Carol calls for Fulvia once she enters the home, as if a child wailing for her mother in the middle of the night.</p>
<p><em>Safe</em> is prescient because of its double vision, both toward the AIDS crisis of the previous decade and the impending technological freak-outs of the next. Perhaps that’s why, just after the absent fruition of Y2K’s doom-and-gloom rhetoric, <em>The Village Voice</em> named <em>Safe</em> the best film of the 1990s; it’s perhaps the ’90s American film that’s most attuned to the paralyzing ramifications of ubiquitous technologies and the fragmented networking of information. Here’s a film that, like Carol, is no longer certain of any kind of physical durability, either of bodies or information.</p>

<p>We still need to talk about Carol, whose illness the film leaves unresolved and, in turn, asks to be considered by viewers through an address of her undergirding social conditions. Though the story is set in 1987, Haynes utilizes the time period more for its oddities and cultural anachronisms, most notably an emergent fascination with New Age dieting, exercise routines, and pseudo-religious gurus claiming to possess phenomenological visions of “essence” where “normal” citizens merely feel a conspicuous detachment. </p>

<p>But the film’s primary concern is the era’s insistence upon silence, as Carol’s early visit with Linda (Susan Norman), whose brother has just died from what is heavily hinted to be AIDS. (The silence is in keeping with Ronald Reagan’s official policy during the 1980s that he would not speak the word publicly.) Silence defines domestic situations, not just through Fulvia’s tight-lipped distance from Carol’s behavior, but also in Carol and Linda’s refusal to verbally make sense of reality through unrepressed discussion.</p>

<p>Such is also the case with Carol’s husband, Greg (Xander Berkeley), who fulfills his patriarchal duties whether by suiting up for work, briefcase in hand to provide the household income, or routinely going missionary for the umpteenth time in their painfully passionless marriage (Carol’s post-coital peck on the cheek is one of the saddest kisses in the history of cinema). Greg can only speak through passive-aggressive shit-talking, insisting to Carol that “nobody has a headache every night of the fucking week!” But Carol <em>does</em>, and no matter anyone’s insistence to the contrary, she <em>is</em> ill, medically verifiable or not. </p>
<p>Haynes is clever about the silences formally, often placing Carol at the edge of the frame or refusing to treat her with any privilege over the numerous luxury items littering her home. As such, his eye is seemingly objective even though he’s rigorously calculating her eventual migration to Wrenwood for “treatment” and, most unforgettably, setting up Carol’s final declaration of “love” while staring at herself in the mirror.</p>


<p>Before that, though, Haynes fulfills one of the primary qualifications B. Ruby Rich has explained for New Queer Cinema by unmapping easy expectations of positive queer representation. After all, when Peter (Peter Friedman), Wrenwood’s co-founder and head counselor, is introduced as having AIDS, the presumption, as Rich explains it, is that since Haynes is a gay filmmaker, any character having AIDS will be portrayed in a positive light. That’s nearly the opposite case here, where Peter’s having AIDS is part of Haynes’s deconstruction of easy leftist outs for expressing empathy. Peter is a proto-Svengali of the impending Internet age, disseminating information from behind an unseen avatar of hollow identity explanations (“What you see is a reflection of what is within”), in an effort to attain “followers,” if you will.</p>
<p>But what’s consistently challenging about <em>Safe</em> is its refusal to ever quite lay down its hand. Maybe Peter <em>does</em> believe what he says and <em>is</em> genuinely interested in healing. But in Haynes’s cinema, good intentions don’t get you very far, since ultimately, all of his characters, regardless of masks or—in a mute Wrenwood client named Lester (Rio Hackford)—a full-body getup, are beholden to and trapped within bodies that are rapidly breaking down. The final irony, of course, is that we’re all on the downward slope, so that self-love and seclusion from societal realities ultimately conceals nothing but humanity’s one shared attribute: mortality.</p>

<h3>Image/Sound</h3>
<p>It’s a true testament to the power of Todd Haynes’s vision that you could be excused for wondering, early on, whether the quicksilver pristineness of Criterion’s 4K upgrade of <em>Safe</em> will set off involuntary seizures. Thankfully, it won’t spill over into physical symptoms, but suffice it to say that there’s an almost eerie surfeit of visual information to process from the UHD transfer here. From the visuals’ ruthlessly oppressive color timing to the sound mix’s at times almost oppressive sense of closeness in the atmosphere, the presentation here underlines all the points Haynes makes in the film itself. That’s a compliment, lest there be any doubt.</p>

<h3>Extras</h3>
<p>It’s hard to believe that it’s been 25 years since Haynes, Julianne Moore and producer Christine Vachon recorded their commentary track for the film, and the time elapsed opens up a whole new layer to their revelation that <em>Safe</em> was at least partially intended to be a comment on the AIDS epidemic, albeit one that didn’t explicitly center the disease itself in Carol’s storyline, so as not to prematurely date the film. Now that we live in a post-PrEP world, there’s every reason to think that many will come to the film not even keying into the film’s metaphorical implications. That, of course, is doubly ironic, since, as noted in the commentary, the gay community split down the middle, with some perceiving <em>Safe</em>’s more universal, metaphorical stance as a cop-out. </p>

<p>Ported over from the original Criterion Blu-ray release from a dozen or so years ago is a video conversation with Haynes and Moore, a separate interview with Vachon, and Haynes’s 1978 short film “The Suicide.” The accompanying booklet contains an essay on the film by Dennis Lim, who positions <em>Safe</em> alongside the work of Chantal Akerman, in the context of post-’80s self-improvement coltishness and ultimately as a hauntingly open-ended depiction of human nature.</p>
<h3>Overall</h3>
<p>It’s not so much that time has been kind to <em>Safe</em> as it is that <em>Safe</em> remains endearingly and mercilessly unkind to modernity itself. </p>
