<p><img alt="" class="attachment-full size-full wp-post-image" height="1270" src="https://www.nme.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/NMEAR_NME-READING-2026_AndyFord_Geese-60373.jpg" width="2000" /></p><p>“Don’t drink the tent water,” mumbles <a href="https://www.nme.com/artists/geese">Geese</a> frontman <a href="https://www.nme.com/artists/cameron-winter">Cameron Winter</a>. “It’s not good for you.” Rain is cascading down the sides of the Gallery tent amid a truly biblical downpour at Reading Festival. At least the 24-year-old has dressed for the weather, donning a black raincoat in lieu of the white vest that became his onstage uniform earlier this year.</p>
<p>In certain lights, the jacket&#8217;s high neck looks like a priest’s dog collar, which is fitting given the dizzying number of religious references threaded through the Brooklyn four-piece’s game-changing 2025 album<a href="https://www.nme.com/reviews/album/geese-getting-killed-review-3892821"> ‘Getting Killed’</a>, as images of crucifixes, serpents and apocalyptic horsemen tumble out in a fabulously unhinged stream-of-consciousness.</p>
<p>If Winter were ranting on the street corner about <em>“kneeling on the turnpike”</em> with an<em> “angel down my throat”, </em>you’d probably give him a wide berth. As it is, he’s drawn a devoted congregation to the tent, like a travelling preacher who’s rolled into town with intoxicating tales of eternal damnation. And Geese sure know how to get the people going, preceding the set with a heart-pumping video montage of sporting triumphs that also includes the Mitchell and Webb sketch in which the former bellows at you to ‘WATCH THE FOOTBALL!”</p>
<p>The band open their sermon with the brooding plod of ‘Husbands’, which Winter mucks about with a little, mumbling unintelligibly before the song unfurls into its expansive, impossibly beautiful coda and abruptly ends. He’s in a mischievous mood, deadpanning the surreal, horror movie lyrics of Trinidad (“<em>My daughters are dead / My wife’s in the shed / My husband’s burning lead / The rest are force-fed or else baked into bread</em>”) as if each line ends in a question mark. It’s like he’s hearing them for the first time and is trying to work out what the hell he’s singing about.</p>
<p>Elsewhere, he delivers an untranscribable war-cry midway through a scything ‘100 Horses’ and pantomimes that he’s about to say something before theatrically abandoning the thought and launching into ‘2122’’s<em> “Get out, get under!”</em> chant instead. Guitarist Emily Green similarly ad-libs a gnarly riff before the explosive <em>“THERE’S A BOMB IN MY CAR!” </em>breakdown of ‘Trinidad’.</p>

<p>This isn’t quite as incendiary a show as <a href="https://www.nme.com/reviews/live/geese-london-hammersmith-eventim-apollo-gig-review-photos-setlist-3936717">Geese’s last UK date at the Eventim Apollo</a> back in March, though it has its own punch-drunk charm, with the band sounding more settled into the material. After all, as the kids going nuts in the circle pit prove, they’re preaching to the converted.</p>
