<img src="https://img.pastemagazine.com/wp-content/avuploads/2026/08/28142848/MixCollage-28-Aug-2026-01-26-PM-1062.jpg" /><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">TikTok has taken to showing me clips from shows I haven&#8217;t watched in a decade. </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Grey&#8217;s Anatomy</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">. </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">ER</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">. </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Sopranos</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">. An uncommonly large number of scenes from </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Once Upon A Time</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> and </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Glee</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">. Without fail, I spy one type of comment attached to these clips. Doesn&#8217;t matter if it&#8217;s Sue Sylvester ruthlessly mocking a teen or a militant Bo Peep attacking Prince Charming and Anna from </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Frozen</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">. The comments are the same—so similar they could be mistaken for an army of bots: “This was made before AI.” I&#8217;ve grown to love that phrase. It gives credibility to some of the truly dumbest (and entertaining) shows humanity has produced. No large language model came up with the plotlines of </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Glee</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">. That was 100-percent human absurdity.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Increasingly, people are craving the absurd and original. Hollywood is on track to have its best year since 2019 and the films that have surprised analysts and delighted audiences aren&#8217;t the big tentpole franchises that Hollywood churns out with rare regard for craft or quality. This year it&#8217;s horror produced by the YouTube generation and an epic made by one of Hollywood&#8217;s only auteurs and Ryan Gosling doing science with a sentient rock. Slop can now be made with a few keystrokes, but audiences seem hungry for grade-A human nonsense.</span></p><!-- admarker --> <!-- inlinecontent_1 --> </p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">What&#8217;s been surprising is that it isn&#8217;t the old people returning to theaters in droves—it&#8217;s the young people nearly written off by Hollywood. The longer you consider why people in their 20s, 30s, and 40s are showing up, though, the more it makes sense. The Gen Z-ers and millennials bringing Hollywood back from the brink are probably the savviest audiences to ever live. Millennials grew up with the internet, while Gen Z is the first true generation of digital natives.&nbsp;</span></p><!-- RevContent --> <div id="revcontent-hidden"> <!-- revisit --><!-- admarker --> 
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Gen Z-ers have spent their entire online lives being inundated with targeted ads. The pressure they face every time they pop online rivals operations the CIA was doing forty years ago. Meta just settled for (an egregious) </span><a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/08/26/technology/meta-settlement-social-media-addiction-lawsuit.html"><span style="font-weight: 400;">$17 billion</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> because it was running psyops on the kids, but the psyops weren&#8217;t limited to kids and teens. If you&#8217;ve used the internet, you&#8217;ve absolutely been an unwitting participant in some of the largest behavioral science studies ever performed, and you&#8217;ve also been subject to what big companies have learned from all these studies.</span><a href="https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2021/05/help-bring-dark-patterns-light"> <span style="font-weight: 400;">Dark patterns</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">,</span><a href="https://bogost.com/blog/gamification_is_bullshit/"> <span style="font-weight: 400;">gamification</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">,</span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crippleware"> <span style="font-weight: 400;">crippleware</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">, and</span><a href="https://doctorow.medium.com/https-pluralistic-net-2024-04-04-teach-me-how-to-shruggie-kagi-caaa88c221f2"> <span style="font-weight: 400;">a whole slew of other legal cons</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> that didn&#8217;t even exist a couple of decades ago. The online marketers and money-makers are savvy, and what they&#8217;ve become savviest about is slop.</span></p><!-- inlinecontent_2 --> </p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Now a lot of people like slop. Slop has been an engine of the internet economy since the first web ads loaded in a browser and we&#8217;ve all clicked on a slide show or two because some herb wanted more clickable content to monetize. Slop also used to be man-made, so there was a limit on how much of it you saw because people have to sleep, eat, and come up with new ideas. Over the last three years the slop has reached a crescendo, thanks to large language models and other AI tools. It is now everywhere. The other night I heard the TikTok robot voice and looked up to the TV to see a supplements commercial entirely generated by AI. After that it was an AI woman telling me about the power of yoga, followed by an AI man telling me to get a gun license now. You cannot use your phone or sit through a handful of commercials without being assaulted by it.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Humans are clever, though. Like large language models, humans are really good at seeing patterns. With exposure, most humans can spot a camouflaged predator in the forest, and the same skill applies to slop. The sheer volume of slop we&#8217;ve been fed these last few years has also made us all</span><a href="https://www.techspot.com/news/113580-people-have-clicked-like-ai-slop-button-linkedin.html"> <span style="font-weight: 400;">very attuned to seeing it</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">. We know (even if we don&#8217;t mind) when something is just being made because someone somewhere will get paid. But occasionally people do reject the slop, and increasingly reject it at the theater.</span></p><!-- admarker --> 
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">That makes sense too—the movie theater isn&#8217;t as cheap as opening up TikTok. It has always been an activity you willfully engage in and spend money on. Unless substances are involved, most people don&#8217;t just find themselves sitting in a movie theater like they might sitting on a couch. So when they choose to spend their time in the theater, it&#8217;s to see something real. They get enough slop from Instagram, why get it from a multimillion dollar movie about a superhero?</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">That&#8217;s part of why both </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Supergirl</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> and </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Masters Of The Universe</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> failed. They weren&#8217;t AI slop, by any means, and the latter was actually a delightful film! Yet audiences saw both as cash grabs. Their marketing seemed centered largely around “this is an IP people have historically loved, so join us at the trough!” That might work for children&#8217;s films where parents have always loved to chuck a child in the trough, but older audiences are feeding at the trough daily on their phones and TVs. At the theater they want something a little more refined (or at least featuring Zendaya). It doesn’t need to pre-date AI, it just needs to not be slop.</span></p><!-- inlinecontent_2 --> </p>
