<img src="https://img.pastemagazine.com/wp-content/avuploads/2026/08/27115753/tim-curry-musketeers-header.jpg" /><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">As an actor, Tim Curry was a personification of impish delights and devilry. His deep voice and debonair persona gave him a refined air while his big eyes hinted at something darker. He was suave, not just in his line delivery, but in his movement. He could slink around the set with confidence, as if seducing his audience through the camera. He could also skulk in the shadows, scheming against the heroes with a deranged glee and a maniacal laugh. In both extremes, he was an <a href="https://www.avclub.com/tim-curry-best-roles-rocky-horror-picture-show-muppet-treasure-island-clue">unmistakable presence</a> in a crowded room full of other recognizable faces. His expressions were so sharp, you could almost hear when he raised an eyebrow or curled the sides of his lips into a Cheshire Cat grin. For many millennials, Tim Curry was their Vincent Price, a stand-in for and guide to the spooky side of life. Curry leaves behind a wicked legacy of onscreen villains, outsiders, charmers, and reprobates.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Those too young for </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Rocky Horror Picture Show </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">midnight screenings may have first encountered Curry as the cheeky, witty butler Wadsworth from 1985&#8217;s </span><a href="https://www.avclub.com/the-most-satisfying-answer-to-clue-s-farcical-whodunit-1844312477"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Clue</span></i></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">. He delights in staying ahead of his captive audience, and delivers his lines at a hyperactive pace, leaving listeners an extra beat to catch up to what he just explained, subtly one-upping the well-heeled set with his own games. That same year,</span> <span style="font-weight: 400;">Ridley Scott&#8217;s </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Legend</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> featured Curry as its Lord Of Darkness, a swole, horned Satanic figure hellbent on plunging the world into—you guessed it—darkness. Curry is so committed to his performance that he makes the viewer cower in his character&#8217;s wake. He stands imposingly tall, sporting a black cloak, vivid red skin, and a ripped chest fit for Venice Beach. His already sonorous voice is slowed and given a booming quality, so that Curry fills each of his scenes with the Darkness&#8217; chilly and strangely Adonis-like presence—all of which contributed to his character&#8217;s cruelty and seduction of the innocent princess.&nbsp;</span></p><!-- admarker --> <!-- inlinecontent_1 --> </p><!-- RevContent --> <div id="revcontent-hidden"> <!-- revisit -->
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Like Curry, Price established his remarkable range before reaching a new crowd later in his career. Price played romantic leads and character parts in the 1930s and &#8217;40s before finding his stride in the horror genre in the 1950s, standing out in movies like </span><a href="https://www.pastemagazine.com/movies/horror-movies/best-horror-movie-of-1953-house-of-wax-vincent-price"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">House Of Wax</span></i></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">, </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Fly</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, and </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Tingler</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> with a commanding presence and outsized performances that thrilled audiences almost as much as the schlock that Price needed to convince them were scary. In the &#8217;60s, Price’s horror bona fides expanded into Roger Corman&#8217;s adaptations of Edgar Allen Poe adaptations, where he used his elocution and refined performances to elevate the films’ modest budgets.</span></p><!-- admarker --> 
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">While Gen Xers were lucky to discover Curry’s early film performances in theaters, many millennials found his early work channel-surfing or rifling through Blockbuster shelves. Something about his face on the poster or the strange, larger-than-life nature of his performances caught their attention. He could be a live-action cartoon, wildly expressive and boisterous in ways other actors never dare to ham it up for the camera, but he could also possess a threatening yet sultry aura. So many of his roles portrayed men both enchanting and dangerous, maybe even comforting, even if we couldn&#8217;t see him. In the late 1990s and 2000s, his resonant voice could be heard in numerous animated movies and TV shows, including </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Fern Gully: The Last Rainforest,</span></i> <i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Wild Thornberrys</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Mighty Ducks</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, and </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Gargoyles</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">. Even if younger fans didn&#8217;t know they were familiar with Tim Curry, they likely knew his voice. Similarly, Price also ventured into television, where he frequented game shows and series like </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Batman, The Brady Bunch,</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> and </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Muppet Show </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">that played with his horror-based persona. And like Curry, Price lent his expressive voice to audience-expanding projects like </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Great Mouse Detective</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> and the “Thriller” video.</span></p><!-- inlinecontent_2 --> </p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Curry similarly showed a talent for bringing skeevy energy to family-friendly fare. Curry often filled the role of rogues kids had to learn to watch out for. In </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Annie</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, he played Rooster Hannigan, the con-artist brother of Miss Hannigan, the cruel woman who runs the orphanage the main character is trying desperately to leave. Rooster isn&#8217;t much better, as he tries to pass himself off as Annie&#8217;s father for reward money. Curry gives the character a slick, smooth-talking façade before revealing a desperate, threatening edge, especially when it becomes apparent he will stop at nothing for financial gains. Notably, both Prince and Curry performed the villainous role of the slimy, power-hungry Cardinal Richelieu in adaptations of </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Three Musketeers, </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">with equally threatening and repulsive flair. Curry’s a lot friendlier when playing the pirate-in-disguise Long John Silver in </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Muppet Treasure Island</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, but he is no more subtle in relishing when an evil plan comes together. This culminated with him directly scaring the bejesus out of kids as the shape-shifting monster who often appears as the creepy clown Pennywise in the TV adaptation of Stephen King&#8217;s </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">It</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">.&nbsp;</span></p><!-- admarker --> 
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Part of why so many of these roles are terrifying is because Curry&#8217;s characters always seem to have the upper hand, and they relish in the power of knowing more or having more strength than their targets. It gives his performances a smarmy air, but that unwavering confidence can be both appealing and vampiric. At every turn, Curry embraced doing too much, pushing his expressions just a little further, and letting his freak flag fly. He was no matinee idol, but his devil-may-care personality outshone many of the pretty faces around him. Like Price, Curry defined a generation of ghouls, kooks, and crooks, with a hypnotic presence as commanding as that big voice of his—inviting us to keep watching to see if he indeed could be trusted. No matter how much the man thrilled, teased, and terrified generations of moviegoers, we loved him for it. </span></p><!-- inlinecontent_2 --> </p>
