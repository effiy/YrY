<p>We may earn a commission from links on this page.</p><p>It&rsquo;s been more than 15 years since <em>Lost</em> went off the air, ending a legendary run on broadcast television. Though the show could be frustratingly slow to reveal its secrets, it remains one of the best TV shows of the modern era&mdash;and one of the most influential, with a host of other series playing off a similar &ldquo;mystery box&rdquo; premise and shuffled timeline. </p><p>Fans of the show have never stopped looking for a viewing experience that matches it in terms of surprise and heartfelt, emotionally rich storytelling (if that describes you, you&rsquo;ve probably already watched <a href="https://lifehacker.com/entertainment/shows-like-lost" target="_blank">all of these streamalikes offering similar vibes</a>), so allow me to suggest the one movie that fits the bill perfectly: <em>Jacob&rsquo;s Ladder</em>, a 1990 psychological thriller starring Tim Robbins.</p><h2 id="why-jacobs-ladder-has-lost-vibes">Why <em>Jacob's Ladder</em> has <em>Lost</em> vibes</h2><div class="mx-auto mt-8 w-full max-w-4xl aspect-video">
    <div id="video-container-01M15N5FNVAKPCR9M61HK94WCP"></div>
</div>


<p>On the surface, <em>Jacob&rsquo;s Ladder</em> might not seem like an immediate match for the heady sci-fi-tinged, character-based storytelling of <em>Lost</em>&mdash;but once you dig deeper, the narrative and tonal parallels become clear. In the film, Jacob Singer (Robbins) is a struggling Vietnam Veteran living in Brooklyn in 1975, four years after a disturbing incident with his unit that ended with Jacob being stabbed by a bayonet. Haunted by his dead son and his wartime experiences, Jacob&rsquo;s descends into a living nightmare, filled with hallucinations of monstrous figures. But something most mysterious is happening, and while the ending is a clear antecedent to <em>Lost</em>&rsquo;s infamous finale, there are many visual echoes as well.</p><p><em>Jacob&rsquo;s Ladder</em> is arguably a horror movie, and is certainly more frightening and disturbing than <em>Lost</em>, which was more philosophical and even optimistic in its approach. But the way Jacob&rsquo;s reality is plagued by a mounting sense of mystery and dread will remind you a lot of the early seasons of <em>Lost</em>, when the Others and the Hatch were ominous mirages hiding in the shadows of that mysterious island. And the film's ultimate payoff&mdash;and its message that sometimes, the devils we perceive to be attacking us are actually angels trying to save us&mdash;will resonate with anyone who was all-in on Jack Shephard&rsquo;s inner struggles (and <em>Lost</em>&rsquo;s overarching themes of atonement and redemption).</p><p>Finally, there&rsquo;s the non-linear way <em>Jacob&rsquo;s Ladder</em> deals with time, which echoes the flashbacks, flash-forwards, and flash-sideways moments in <em>Lost</em>. As Jacob&rsquo;s life dissolves into an increasingly hellish chaos, his experience of time becomes shuffled and blurred, with the past (and alternate futures) intruding on the present in disorienting ways. Just like in the show, these flashes of Jacob&rsquo;s life slowly paint a picture of his true present, and his ultimate fate.
