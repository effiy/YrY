<p>It&#8217;s hard to imagine a more impactful presence in the millennial mind, particularly the outsider, queer, or cinephile, than that of <a href="https://www.rogerebert.com/cast-and-crew/tim-curry">Tim Curry</a>, the veteran actor of stage and screen who passed this Wednesday at the age of 80. Tony Award nominee, voice actor extraordinaire, honorary Muppet—all of these descriptors fit Curry&#8217;s decades of work, a body of acting best characterized by his resonant purr of a voice, deep, piercing eyes, and a flamboyance from which it was impossible to look away. </p>



<p>Curry was born in 1946 in Cheshire, the son of a school secretary and a Royal Navy Captain; the latter&#8217;s revolving door of assignments sent him everywhere from Hong Kong to a variety of British seaside towns before they settled down in Plymouth. It was mere weeks after that that Curry&#8217;s father suffered a stroke and later died from pneumonia complications in 1958. Curry was 11.&nbsp;</p>



<p>He literally burst onto the scene in fishnets, high heels, and thickly-lacquered lipstick as the libidinous mad scientist Dr. Frank-N-Furter in 1975&#8217;s &#8220;The Rocky Horror Picture Show,&#8221; a role he originated on the London stage. (He met the show&#8217;s playwright, and his co-star as Riff Raff, Richard O&#8217;Brien, when they did <em>Hair </em>in London.) A &#8220;sweet transvestite from transsexual Transylvania,&#8221; Curry&#8217;s Frank proudly flaunted all pretenses of good taste, and in the case of poor Brad Majors and Janet Weiss, the pearl-clutching norms of heteronormativity.&nbsp;</p>



<p>Roger Ebert <a href="https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/the-rocky-horror-picture-show-1976">said of Curry&#8217;s performance</a>, &#8220;He’s the best thing in the movie, maybe because he seems to be having the most fun.&#8221; He vamped, strutted, and sneered his way through that picture, urging his fellow freaks and queerdos to &#8220;don&#8217;t dream it, be it.&#8221; Like Terence Stamp in &#8220;Teorema,&#8221; he seduced virtually every other character in the picture, and it was as liberating as it was chaotic. And Curry, with every high kick and bugged-out eye, embodied the queer freedom that a generation of young queer folks would cling to. (I had the CD of the film&#8217;s soundtrack in my car as a teen; little did I realize why those notes would stick with me so much.)&nbsp;&nbsp;</p>

<p>It was the kind of role that could ruin or pigeonhole a young actor like Curry and, well, despite a modest reception upon release, &#8220;Rocky Horror&#8221; would quickly become a midnight movie favorite, one that&#8217;s still screened in movie theaters to enthusiastic crowds (and audience participation) to this day. From there, he&#8217;d bounce into Hollywood with a variety of snarling villain roles perfectly suited to his particular brand of bombast: Rooster in 1982&#8217;s &#8220;Annie&#8221; (that mustache), the Satan-like Darkness in Ridley Scott&#8217;s gauzy 1985 fantasy &#8220;Legend&#8221; (those horns), a duplicitous preacher in 1988&#8217;s underseen &#8220;Pass the Ammo.&#8221;&nbsp;</p>



<p>On &#8220;Legend,&#8221; Roger Ebert <a href="https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/legend-1986">would say of Curry</a> that he &#8220;makes an effective Darkness; I liked his style. But, once again, the makeup people have done such a good job with his blazing eyes and gigantic horns that, in a way, almost any actor could be lurking inside there (only Curry’s sensuous lips give him away).&#8221;</p>



<p>But his next iconic role would be as the breathlessly expositing butler Wadsworth in 1985&#8217;s &#8220;Clue,&#8221; in which he chewed through scenery as if it were a steaming bowl of shark&#8217;s fin soup. &#8220;Clue&#8221; is murder mystery farce at its finest, and alongside his talented co-stars (including Eileen Brennan, Madeline Kahn, Christopher Lloyd) he met every gag, line, and frenzied dash from room to room with the same brio and clipped upper-crust accent we&#8217;d come to expect. Even when he went small, it felt big; when he arched his eyebrow, the ceiling always rose a couple inches.&nbsp;</p>

<p>Same goes for Pennywise in 1990&#8217;s &#8220;IT,&#8221; playing the demonic clown from Stephen King&#8217;s imagination with equal playfulness and menace. His villainy was always a bit impish, no matter the role; sure, he was a baddie, but he <em>loved</em> it, and that&#8217;s what made him so magnetic to watch. It&#8217;s what made him so cuddly even when he transgressed in such unscrupulous ways—it&#8217;s funny, considering the gender-bending hedonism of his breakout role, that he&#8217;d find himself snarling giddily through so many family films, from &#8220;Home Alone 2&#8221; to Long John Silver in &#8220;Muppet Treasure Island.&#8221; (As the cape-wearing Grand Wizard in the 1986 TV movie &#8220;The Worst Witch,&#8221; he threatened us with such Halloween horrors as a dog turning into a cat.) With every oily, smarmy retort, Curry let his characters relish in the delight of villainy, and we enjoyed every second of it.</p>

<p>In addition to his live-action roles, his reedy, musical voice made him perfect for voice acting. Here, too, he chiefly played villains in everything from &#8220;FernGully&#8221; to the Power Rangers knockoff &#8220;Superhuman Samurai Syber-Squad&#8221;—no role was beneath him. (He did, of course, play the occasional virtuous character, as his long run as patriarch Nigel on Nickelodeon&#8217;s &#8220;The Wild Thornberries proves.) He&#8217;d even dabble in computer games, most famously as the Russian Premier Cherdenko in &#8220;Command and Conquer: Red Alert 3&#8221; (dusting off his Russian accent from the John McTiernan Cold War thriller &#8220;The Hunt for Red October&#8221;). In perhaps the hammiest line Curry would ever deliver, he asserted that he would flee to &#8220;the one place not corrupted by capitalism…. SPACE!&#8221;</p>

<p>But his first love was always the stage, and he&#8217;d return to it several times throughout his career. His first Tony nomination was as Mozart beside Ian McKellan&#8217;s Salieri in a production of Peter Shaffer&#8217;s &#8220;Amadeus&#8221;; in 1992, he earned another for &#8220;My Favorite Year&#8221; on Broadway. He originated the role of King Arthur in &#8220;Spamalot,&#8221; which snagged him his third nom in 2005.&nbsp;</p>



<p>Curry would work steadily in film, TV, theater, and a variety of other mediums until 2012, after a sudden stroke during a massage gave him mobility issues on his left side. He would use a wheelchair until his passing. Even so, Curry greeted his new condition with his signature humor, and went on to refer to his disabled left hand as &#8220;Teddy.&#8221; He still did the occasional voice work, including taking a stint as Emperor Palpatine in &#8220;The Clone Wars.&#8221; But his 2025 memoir &#8220;Vagabond&#8221; sums up Curry&#8217;s life as only he can tell it: ribald, witty, deliciously dry, and filled with absolute pleasure.</p>



<p>In a <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nymOjoq-psE" rel="noopener" target="_blank">late-in-life interview with Ben Mankiewicz</a> for CBS Sunday Morning, Curry said of his impending passing, &#8220;I don&#8217;t fear death. I try to avoid it [laughs] as I think we all do. But I suspect that in the end, I will welcome it… I think it may be comforting to go bye-bye, and I want to earn the privilege of not being here. I&#8217;m not galloping towards it, but I think I may well embrace it.&#8221; Wherever he may be, I hope he sees blue skies through the tears in his eyes.&nbsp;</p>
