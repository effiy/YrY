<img src="https://img.pastemagazine.com/wp-content/avuploads/2026/08/26161343/finding-emily-header.jpg" /><p><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Finding Emily</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, the feature debut from director Alicia MacDonald, announces itself with an unlikely mash-up. A fake quote from Sigmund Freud graces the screen as &#8220;Blue Monday&#8221; by New Order pulses in the background. &#8220;Love is a form of temporary psychosis,&#8221; reads a young woman&#8217;s voice as Bernard Sumner croons &#8220;How does it feel / To treat me like you do?&#8221; This cursory engagement with psychology and music doesn&#8217;t ever deepen as Rachel Hirons&#8217; screenplay gets to know its protagonists: Emily (Angourie Rice), an American student at Manchester City University investigating the self-sabotaging instincts of the lovelorn, and Owen, (Spike Fearn), a sound engineer hell-bent on finding a woman he had a missed connection with.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Despite what the film&#8217;s title might have you believe, Rice&#8217;s Emily is not the subject of Owen&#8217;s search. After meeting a waifish beauty in a fairy costume (Sadie Soverall, a &#8220;literal manic pixie dream girl&#8221;) at the on-campus venue he works at, Owen asks for her number. &#8220;I&#8217;m Emily,&#8221; she yells over the DJ&#8217;s speaker-blowing set before typing in her contact. Her friends rush her off, citing a nearby birthday party they need to hit. But when Owen tries to text her the next day, his message goes undelivered. He then notices that her number is missing a crucial digit, and immediately makes it his quest to locate the girl he fell for.&nbsp;</span></p><!-- inlinecontent_1 --> </p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">As he begins (quite shadily) asking around about Emily—whose last name he obviously doesn&#8217;t know—a student says that his description sounds like Emily Raine (Rice), who is currently in a psych seminar led by cranky Professor Westlake (Prasanna Puwanarajah). Owen calls her out of class, only to appear instantly crestfallen. Though a similar beauty—flowing hair, clear skin, whimsical style—this Emily is simply not the one. Having witnessed Owen&#8217;s near-obsessive need to find this girl, Emily reasons that she might finally have a case study to center her fast-approaching thesis essay around. Hell, she might even be able to secure a prestigious, and visa-extending, research position as a result.</span></p><!-- admarker --> 
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Emily clearly bends some ethical parameters in order to drive Owen toward grand gestures, which involve a mass-email scandal, public love song performance, and subsequent social media virality. But the pair&#8217;s connection somehow continues to grow alongside Owen&#8217;s campus-wide infamy. The catch is that he is completely unaware of Emily&#8217;s ulterior motive, believing instead that his devout mission is teaching her to see love through a less cynical lens. He knows that her own paramour, Tristan (Timothy Innes), isn&#8217;t giving her the attention she craves; they exist on two ends of a spectrum: a man who&#8217;s willing to make a fool of himself in order to chase the girl of his dreams, and a woman actively embarrassing herself by pursuing a co-ed who&#8217;s totally indifferent to her.&nbsp;</span></p><!-- RevContent --> <div id="revcontent-hidden"> <!-- revisit --><!-- inlinecontent_2 --> </p><!-- admarker --> 
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">There could have easily been a pointed commentary here about the pitfalls of modern love, rife as it is with parasocial projection and demeaning situationships, but </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Finding Emily</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> chooses instead to lampoon, of all things, how knee-jerk reactions from feminists might unduly mischaracterize a nice guy. In relation to the email scandal, wherein over 300 MCU students named Emily receive a message from Owen, one such recipient begins a campus-wide movement to condemn what she considers a breach of security. This Emily (Kat Ronney) has brightly-dyed hair, dresses like she&#8217;s in a <a href="https://www.pastemagazine.com/music/bikini-kill/bikini-kill-reunite-for-jenn-pellys-raincoats-book">Bikini Kill</a> cover band, and takes it upon herself to start a Take Back The Night-style protest in order to condemn the anonymous &#8220;email guy&#8221; as a potential predator. Her character exists to be the butt of a joke, the target of misogynistic jocks and the callous Dean (Minnie Driver) who, despite being too young, claims to have &#8220;burned her bras&#8221; as a young feminist.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">But Riot Grrrl Emily isn&#8217;t the only one who, in the film&#8217;s eyes, takes women&#8217;s rights too far. After our main Emily gets hit in the mouth by a rando during a concert, a drunk woman sees her bloody lip and squares up to deck Owen, believing him to be a domestic abuser. In another scene, a podcast host does everything in her power to frame Owen as a creepy incel. Had </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Finding Emily </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">fleshed out Owen&#8217;s faults a bit more—demonstrating that it really </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">is </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">super weird</span> <span style="font-weight: 400;">of him to go about finding his crush this way—the film&#8217;s comedic streak would have been more defined, juxtaposing his manic pursuit with straightwoman Emily&#8217;s psychoanalytical perspective. Instead, the gags come from women being over-eager to catch a predator, unable to rationalize that the guy they&#8217;re accosting is actually a harmless lovefool, which makes the tone feel bleakly regressive.</span></p><!-- admarker --> 
<p><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Finding Emily</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> clearly wants to situate itself in a <a href="https://www.avclub.com/search?q=When+Romance+Met+Comedy">bygone era of rom-coms</a>, evinced by anecdotes about Owen&#8217;s lost love being &#8220;too cool for social media&#8221; and needledrops that lean more New Wave than new. But what made those earlier films successful was distinctive characters and palpable on-screen chemistry. Fearn admittedly does a bit more here (his body language, including a penchant for neck nuzzling, is the dreamiest part of the film), but neither Owen nor Emily are given the room to ardently express their passions. The most we get are sob stories about dead/estranged mothers, which are so narratively similar that it&#8217;s weird not to make more of a point of it. If we don&#8217;t gain any insight into the protagonist&#8217;s inner lives, how can we root for them to declare a love that feels hollow?</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">For a film that argues that we don&#8217;t take the time to appreciate people&#8217;s nuances—from those we&#8217;ve just met to those we&#8217;ve known our whole lives—it&#8217;s odd that its central characters are given little more to distinguish themselves than a thick Manchester accent and an ill-conceived finger tattoo. Even Manchester itself feels hollow in its presentation, a shame considering one of the film&#8217;s most charming scenes involves a bar crawl through the queer nightlife quarter, which is replete with deliciously bitchy non-binary bartenders. Otherwise void of convincing characters converging in a specific time and place, </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Finding Emily</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> is utterly lost searching for itself.&nbsp;</span></p>
