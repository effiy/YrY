<p class="has-drop-cap wp-block-paragraph">Three years after a <a href="https://www.statnews.com/2023/07/19/marc-tessier-lavigne-stanford-president-resignation/">closely watched research misconduct investigation</a> of Stanford&#x2019;s then-president, Marc Tessier-Lavigne, led to his resignation, the prominent neuroscientist revealed new details this week from his research and travel in an attempt to refute allegations that he published fraudulent results and orchestrated a cover-up.&#xa0;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">These allegations found new life this year with the release of &#x201c;<a href="https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/760317/how-to-rule-the-world-by-theo-baker/">How to Rule the World</a>,&#x201d; a book by former Stanford Daily reporter Theo Baker, who presents Tessier-Lavigne as a case study in Silicon Valley&#x2019;s culture of corner-cutting. A key part of that story revolves around a major paper on neurodegeneration that Tessier-Lavigne co-authored in 2009, while he was a top scientist at the South San Francisco biotech Genentech.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The former university head on Wednesday <a href="https://tessier-lavigne-lab.stanford.edu/news/addressing-false-narrative-about-my-nature-2009-paper">posted</a> a more than 3,000-word response to the book on his Stanford lab website, alongside emails with former colleagues meant to bolster his defense of the study, published in the journal Nature.&#xa0;</p><p><a href="https://www.statnews.com/2026/08/27/marc-tessier-lavigne-responds-theo-baker-book-misconduct-stanford-genentech/?utm_campaign=rss">Continue to STAT+ to read the full story&hellip;</a></p>
