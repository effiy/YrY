<p><img alt="The Sun Never Sets" class="attachment-thumbnail-large size-thumbnail-large wp-post-image" height="480" src="https://www.slantmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/sunneversets-720x480.jpg" width="720" /></p><p>There’s a brief, metatextual moment in <em>The Sun Never Sets</em> where writer-director Joe Swanberg, who appears in a small role in the film, is glimpsed through a window as his character exits a scene, ceding the stage to his lead actors. As his films are largely improvised, Swanberg invests fully in his collaborators’ ability to create something interesting on the fly, while they, in turn, place faith in him as he leads them into the unknown.</p>
<p>In narrative terms, <em>The Sun Never Sets</em> is one of Swanberg’s most tightly controlled films, yet it retains the naturalistic rhythm of his more freewheeling outings. The plot has its rom-com contrivances, but the performances ground the action with emotional authenticity. As Wendy, a construction worker from Anchorage, Alaska, Dakota Fanning walks a convincingly fine line between confidence and ambivalence, while the warm, easy charisma of Jake Johnson disguises the less lovable qualities of his character that come increasingly to the fore.</p>

<p>At 31, Wendy has watched one friend after another get married and have children. Her boyfriend of two years, Jack (Johnson), is older, divorced, and a parent already, and he’s made clear that he doesn’t want any more kids. Wendy, for her part, seems content with their arrangement: Jack is a successful financial advisor, his kids Jude and Abby (Kincaid Zatalokin and Charlie Golike) adore her, and even his ex, Elizabeth (Anna Konkle), approves of her.</p>

<p>One day, Jack sits Wendy down and tells her they need to spend some time apart, sending her on a kind of romantic rumspringa. Fearing that Wendy will look back in 10 years and regret missing the opportunity to have kids of her own, Jack insists that she date other people and figure out what she really wants before returning to him, should she decide to.</p>

<p>It’s unclear if this is a sneaky maneuver intended to sever their relationship permanently, or if Jack truly is that profoundly selfless—and there’s something a tad infantilizing about the assumption that Wendy hasn’t sufficiently thought through her choices. Wendy plays along only reluctantly, going out on perfunctory dates while pining for the man she’s already grown to love. It’s on one of Wendy’s dates that she runs into an ex, Chuck (Cory Michael Smith), who had left Anchorage but is now back in town to start up an air taxi business.</p>
<p>Soon, the former lovers pick up where they left off, and Wendy begins to envision a life with a man she’d previously left due to some vaguely alluded-to “behavior” and “bullshit.” The previously supportive (and cocky) Jack, realizing he may lose Wendy, becomes supplicant, even backtracking on his no-more-kids stance if it means getting her back. And so begins the emotional push and pull between these two characters, a succession of exceedingly childish jealousy-baiting that serves as the through line of <em>The Sun Never Sets</em>.</p>


<p>As the third point in this love triangle, Chuck never fully comes alive, his character schematically sketched as the opposite of his romantic rival. Where Jack, a transplant from New York, views the misty Alaskan landscape through the windows of his designer home, the comparatively rugged Chuck joins Wendy on spontaneous camping trips; Jack’s financial security stands in contrast with Chuck’s inability to pass a credit check when looking for an apartment. Wendy’s dilemma, though fraught with relatable complications, would be more believable if her options weren’t diametrically opposed in such an obviously calculated way.</p>
<p>Swanberg doesn’t explore much new thematic terrain here, but the scenic splendor of our 49th state invigorates the usually homebound auteur’s visual sense. This isn’t the first time he’s shot outside of Chicago—a city he’s portrayed in loving detail across many films—but Anchorage provides a fresh opportunity to address the ways his characters relate to their environment. Unlike in, say, Christopher Nolan’s Alaska-set thriller <a href="/film/insomnia/"><em>Insomnia</em></a>, where the ever-present sun takes on heavy symbolic weight, the phenomenon of perpetual daylight is acknowledged only in passing in the film, serving more as a textural element than a thematic one.</p>

<p>When Swanberg began working with Netflix last decade, he successfully translated the fundamentals of his idiosyncratic ethos to a more commercial venue—both in <a href="/film/win-it-all/"><em>Win It All</em></a>, his somewhat conventional film about gambling addiction, and in his three-season chronicle of millennial life in the Windy City, <a href="/tv/easy-season-three-review-joe-swanberg/"><em>Easy</em></a>. Although <em>The Sun Never Sets</em> wasn’t made for Netflix, it feels ready-made for the streaming giant in all the wrong ways, from the generic pop songs that pepper the soundtrack to the blandness of the storytelling, which lacks the live-wire energy of even Swanberg’s most mainstream efforts, including his similarly straightforward romantic drama <a href="/film/drinking-buddies/"><em>Drinking Buddies</em></a>. The dulled edge of <em>The Sun Never Sets</em> speaks, for the moment, to a striving for maturity and seriousness rather than the true embodiment of either.</p>
