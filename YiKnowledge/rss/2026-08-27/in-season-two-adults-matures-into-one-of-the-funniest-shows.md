<img src="https://img.pastemagazine.com/wp-content/avuploads/2026/08/26170009/adults-season-2-1.jpg" /><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">It’s a rallying cry in our current phase of the streaming era: TV seasons need more episodes. Ensemble comedies, in particular, are getting shortchanged, with even established shows receiving the type of paltry, six- to 10-episode orders that were once reserved for midseason and summer replacements. Imagine if famous, took-a-while-to-find-themselves cases like </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Seinfeld</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Parks And Recreation</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, and the American </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Office</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> were canceled with as many episodes under their belt as the recently 86’d </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Deli Boys</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Mid-Century Modern</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">,</span> <span style="font-weight: 400;">and </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Not Suitable For Work</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">? Fewer episodes don&#8217;t allow the space required to flesh out wacky protagonists and their wackier circumstances.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">And, as is the case with FX’s </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Adults</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, it can leave you wanting so much more. If the series was a hangout sitcom </span><a href="https://www.avclub.com/adults-review-fx-tv-sitcom"><span style="font-weight: 400;">with enough promise</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> in its debut last year, then in its sophomore run, it blooms into a hysterical and self-assured comedy—further proof that giving a show the room to grow is beneficial. It&#8217;s just too bad that, once again, there are only eight half-hour installments to enjoy it in.&nbsp;</span></p><!-- inlinecontent_1 --> </p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Helmed by former </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Tonight Show </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">writers and husband-wife duo Rebecca Shaw and Ben Kronengold, </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Adults</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> embraces the inherent awkwardness and painfulness of being a twentysomething in this (or any) day and age. On top of an unstable job market, shoddy dating apps and prospects, and the intoxicating lure of social media, their characters are dealing with the timeless fear of growing up without knowing who you truly are, and having to learn the ropes while being judged for your mistakes. Shaw, Kronengold, and team grasp that the responsibilities of adulthood are inevitable, and it sucks. At least having overly-dependent pals—whom you live with rent-free, no less—can make the journey palatable and entertaining, especially if it&#8217;s as fun a crew as Issa (Amita Rao), Anton (Owen Thiele), Paul Baker (Jack Innanen), Samir (Malik Elassal), and Billie (Lucy Freyer). This charming quintet cements </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Adults</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">’ status</span> <span style="font-weight: 400;">as TV’s strongest and funniest Gen Z sitcom.&nbsp;</span></p><!-- admarker --> 
<p><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Adults </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">prioritizes its humor above all, so even when the subject matter of season two feels played out, the execution still makes it a delight. The new episodes borrow various elements from one of </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Adults</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">’ spiritual predecessors, </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Friends</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">: There&#8217;s a Janice-like annoying ex in one episode; in another, Issa gets hired at Anton&#8217;s corporate workplace to help him out of a jam, only to realize she can thrive in the office as well. There&#8217;s also Billie&#8217;s case of mistaken identity/false ID card, </span>which feels pulled out of a <span style="font-weight: 400;">Monica plotline (an unplanned dance performance is involved as well).&nbsp;</span></p><!-- RevContent --> <div id="revcontent-hidden"> <!-- revisit --><!-- inlinecontent_2 --> </p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">But what most connects </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Adults </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">to </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Friends</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> are the distinct personalities of its main characters, aided by performers who sharpen their comedic skills in season two. At first glance, it can seem like Rao is just playing a mix of Lisa Kudrow&#8217;s Phoebe and Ilana Glazer&#8217;s </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Broad City </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">persona, but she brings plenty of warmth to Issa&#8217;s free-spirited nature, delivering yet another breakout performance in season two. Similarly, Thiele digs deeper into Anton following the introduction of his older sister, played by an aptly cast Raven-Symoné. Innanen, Elassal, and Freyer all get well-defined arcs that bring more dimension to their characters as well.&nbsp;</span></p><!-- admarker --> 
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Season two is made all the more compelling by how it invests in the roommates as a group. They have so much fun spending time together that it&#8217;s impossible not to laugh with or at them from the comfort of your own couch. Take episode four, which plays out almost in real time (shades of “</span><a href="https://www.avclub.com/tv-in-a-bottle-19-great-tv-episodes-largely-confined-t-1798220786"><span style="font-weight: 400;">The One Where No One’s Ready</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">”) because the gang has about 30 minutes to rid their house of a raccoon before the acid they </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">just</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> ingested kicks in. This sets off a chain of events whose repercussions are gradually felt in the back half of the season, but it&#8217;s also just a ridiculous and hijinks-filled episode—multiple animals are involved and a foursome may or may not take place during this time.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Season two addresses a few of the complaints from season one, filling in the gaps of Issa, Anton, Paul Baker, Samir, and Billie&#8217;s respective family lives and careers, including how they make money or what kind of support system they might have outside of this core group. It means Issa tries to figure out if she wants to be a mom, and Samir and Billie take major life decisions. And, yes, the Paul Baker and Anton will-they/won&#8217;t-they remains an enticing throughline.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">It might only be eight episodes long, but </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Adults</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">’ second season</span> <span style="font-weight: 400;">takes advantage of that limited time to polish its tone and hit us with amusing running gags, surprisingly moving friendship dilemmas (like Issa slowly realizing that her boyfriend and her BFF truly might be into each other!), and, best of all, genuine laugh-out-loud moments. (A particular line delivery from Rao in episode seven will be etched in your brain permanently—the scene and the build-up to it are just that funny.) Everything about </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Adults</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">: the performances, the script, executive producer Nick Kroll&#8217;s blink-and-you&#8217;ll-miss-it role—snaps into place this year, resulting in a hilarious season two. Rarely has a modern TV comedy demanded the opportunity to try 22 episodes per season.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><b><i>Adults </i></b><b>season two premieres August 27 on Hulu and FX.</b></p>
