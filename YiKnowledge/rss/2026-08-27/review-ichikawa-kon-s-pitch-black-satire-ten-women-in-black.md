<p><img alt="Ten Women in Black" class="attachment-thumbnail-large size-thumbnail-large wp-post-image" height="480" src="https://www.slantmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/tenwomeninblackbr-720x480.jpg" width="720" /></p><p><img alt="Ten Women in Black" class="alignleft wp-image-50018619" src="/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/tenwomeninblackbr.jpg" width="315" />A pitch-black satire of Japanese society when the nation was riding the crest of the postwar economic miracle, Ichikawa Kon’s <em>Ten Women in Black</em> resists easy categorization. The 1961 film opens like a 1930s Hollywood melodrama, with a title sequence in which each actor nods and smiles at the camera, before abruptly shifting to the visual style of film noir in the first scene. Such jarring aesthetic shifts are characteristic of the film.</p>
<p>The titular women—the wife (Yamamoto Fujiko) and nine mistresses of television producer Kaze (Funakoshi Eiji)—have murder on their minds. But their quest for bloodthirsty vengeance against their shared lover very pointedly serves to highlight their own emotional and spiritual emptiness. </p>
<p>Written by Wada Natto, Ichikawa&#8217;s wife and frequent collaborator, the film is perched on the knife edge between tragedy, thriller, and farce. It also has a deep empathy for its characters, positioning them as alienated cogs in the machine of Japan’s rapid modernization in the early 1960s—a period that coincided with the boom in television’s popularity. (Kaze plucks all of his mistresses, who are either actresses or behind-the-camera talent, from within the industry.)</p>
<p>Rather than simply vilifying the apathetic Kaze, <em>Ten Women in Black</em> shrewdly balances its critique of gender, placing equal blame on the women who flock to him and enable his crude behavior. In one of the film’s funnier sequences, the mistresses even come to agree that Kaze is completely unremarkable, realizing that they fell for him out of convenience rather than true connection. The women may get the last laugh in a fitting and humorous moment of emasculation, but the film clearly presents men and women alike as suffering from the same social malaise and detachment that plagued much of Japanese society in the early 1960s.</p>
<h3>Image/Sound</h3>
<p>Radiance’s 1080p transfer of a 4K restoration looks magnificent throughout. Much of <em>Ten Women in Black</em> is lensed like a film noir, and the deep blacks and high contrast ratio are impressive in highlighting cinematographer Kobayashi Setsuo’s skillful use of shadows and darkened negative spaces. The image is also quite rich in detail, evident in everything from the fabrics of the costumes to the intricacies of the actors’ facial expressions, while grain is consistently present and evenly distributed. The mono audio is more than adequate, with the all-important dialogue, including some overlapping lines, presented cleanly and clearly.</p>
<h3>Extras</h3>
<p>Iwai Shinji’s 2006 feature-length documentary <em>The Kon Ichikawa Story</em> is the highlight of the extras collected for this release. It serves as a perfect primer for those who are only familiar with Ichikawa’s <a href="/dvd/the-burmese-harp-dvd/"><em>The Burmese Harp</em></a> and <a href="/dvd/fires-on-the-plain-4k-uhd-blu-ray-review-ichikawa-kon/"><em>Fires on the Plain</em></a>, highlighting many of his more overlooked works and the importance of his collaborations with his wife, screenwriter Wada Natto. Using intertitles rather than voiceover, the documentary takes a distinctive approach to tracing the arc of the filmmaker’s career. We also get a new interview Pizzicato Five musician Konishi Yasuharu, who discusses his love of the film, which he helped re-release in 1997. Lastly, there’s a booklet with a new essay by critic Yuka Kimbara, who writes about the film’s visual style and the working relationship between Ichikawa and Wada.</p>
<h3>Overall</h3>
<p>Radiance’s release sheds welcome light on Ichikawa Kon’s work outside of his anti-war classics.</p>
