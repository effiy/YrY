<p><strong>June 12, 2026</strong></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Our shop is in an old service station in a charming alpine town nestled within Utah’s Wasatch Mountains. Here’s the </span><span style="font-weight: 400;">oomph</span><span style="font-weight: 400;"> of it: Avery, the only person who works here besides Galen and me, sells coffee in a waiting room we’ve converted into a bar. In the first garage bay, Galen sells skateboards and enormous pairs of shorts. In the second garage bay, I sell books and art supplies and an assortment of Korean-formulated sunscreens. Some people have been calling the shop maximalist, but I’m actually quite particular about the goods we hawk.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Sarah’s in from New York to help with our grand opening, and she prices our whole drinks menu and sets up the receipt printer. I’ve known her since I was nineteen, and she’s been doing stuff that I don’t want to do for fifteen years now. Once, during the pandemic, we went glamping near Moab and almost died overnight from carbon monoxide poisoning caused by a backfiring tent stove. I thought it was because we’d eaten too much bread pudding before tucking in. She got us two free nights in an upgraded safari tent. </span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The first book I sell on our first day in business is a used copy of </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Fart Proudly: Writings of Benjamin Franklin You Never Read in School</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, for seven dollars. It goes home with a high school English teacher on a daytime date. The next is a used copy of </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Land of Little Rain</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> by Mary Austin, also for seven dollars, to a </span><span style="font-weight: 400;">studious young college student</span><span style="font-weight: 400;">. The third is a copy of Stephanie LaCava’s </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">I Fear My Pain Interests You</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, which goes to a girl who pays with her own money even though her parents are present. She seems to be around thirteen, and I worry for a moment that she’s not old enough to read a book with so many lecherous older men skulking through its pages. But nobody ever monitored my reading when I was that age, and I turned out pretty much fine. I read a lot of Janet Evanovich’s bounty hunter series.</span><span id="more-174719"></span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">We look at the sales report at the end of the day. The only thing that&#8217;s moved more units than iced lattes are rolls of washi tape printed with bears playing the drums.</span></p>
<p><b>June 13</b></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Turns out the people can’t get enough fly-fishing guides from 1973.</span></p>
<div class="wp-caption alignnone" id="attachment_174725" style="width: 1034px;"><img alt="" class="size-large wp-image-174725" height="962" src="https://www.theparisreview.org/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/fishing-scaled-e1786479402852-1024x962.jpeg" width="1024" /><p class="wp-caption-text" id="caption-attachment-174725">Photograph by Claire Carusillo.</p></div>
<p><b>June 15</b></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Galen’s been obsessed with the song “Reno” by Doug Supernaw for the past six months. Neither of us has been to Reno, but I think it would be funny to get married there. Last fall we went to West Wendover, a bleached-out casino town just over the border in Nevada, on our way to see Nancy Holt’s </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Sun Tunnels</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">. A guy from New York City offered us “herb” inside one of the tubes, and I accepted. There wasn’t any good souvenir shopping.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The song “Reno” is about a woman paramour who is apparently as callous and cruel as the city. (“You know the lady’s a lot like Reno / She ain’t got a heart and she don’t care when you’re down / Just like the lights of a casino / She’ll pull you in and play with you awhile / But there ain’t no way to win / I know she’s a lot like Reno.”)</span> <span style="font-weight: 400;">We read that Reno’s mayor banned the song from radio play in 1993. Supernaw shot back. It wasn’t personal: Reno was the only city he knew that rhymed with </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">casino</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">. That’s just how the chips fall sometimes, brother.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">In the shop, Galen talks to a family visiting from Reno and plays the song from the iPad. They don’t seem to recognize it. The only song he listens to more than “Reno” is “Brigham Young,” which was recorded in the sixties by a folk singer involved in the Salt Lake music scene named Rosalie Sorrels. Written in 1870, Sorrels’s version goes like this:</span></p>
<blockquote><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Brigham, Brigham Young<br />
</span><span style="font-weight: 400;">It’s a miracle he survived<br />
</span><span style="font-weight: 400;">With his roaring rams and his pretty little lambs<br />
</span><span style="font-weight: 400;">And his five and forty wives</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Number forty-five’s about sixteen<br />
</span>Number one is sixty and three<br />
And among such a riot how he ever keeps ’em quiet<br />
Is a downright mystery to me<br />
For they cackle and claw and jaw, jaw, jaw<br />
Each one has a different desire</p></blockquote>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I later learn that Rosalie Sorrels passed away in 2017 at her daughter’s home in Reno.</span></p>
<p><b>June 19</b></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">On Fridays and Saturdays for the past year, Sprinter vans of women in midmarket prairie dresses get dropped off in the center of town and beeline to a farm store owned by a beautiful former ballerina with millions of Instagram followers. </span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Her arrival in our small town last summer alarmed and dismayed me: I wanted to be the most notorious adult blond in the area. I am thirty-four, unmarried, and cohabitating with a forty-year-old skate rat; when we moved here from New York, for the first time in my unremarkable-but-peaceful-enough life, that was enough to make for a scandalous backstory.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The ballerina’s also-blond acolytes have made parking at the nearby post office an all-day project. But now I kinda like having them all around. And the ballerina seems pretty committed to the farming thing, even if there have been a few missteps with regard to raw milk processing.</span></p>
<p><b>June 20</b></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Eight skaters and one beautiful dog in an arm cast tumble out of a diesel truck. The dog is named Laika. They drove up from Salt Lake to take in a bit of mountain air and buy some of our branded merchandise. The shop grows anarchic and clangorous with so many boxy tees and “Yeah, man&#8221;s flying around. They eventually leave us for sandwiches and the nearby creek. It’s hot out. While peeing, I watch a just-uploaded Instagram video of them ripping cigs and baptizing one another in a stone reflection pool a customer of ours built into the riverbed.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">An hour later, Max, a straggler in their crew, shows up with a baby raccoon he’s rescued. Because of a skate emergency, he can’t stay long, so he nestles the baby in a Home Depot box out back. Galen keeps showing the raccoon to the children visiting the shop, and for about thirty seconds, I try to put a matronly stop to it, </span><span style="font-weight: 400;">jaw-jaw-jawing</span><span style="font-weight: 400;"> about how word will get out that we’re operating an illegal petting zoo on our ninth day in business. But then I, too, catch baby-raccoon fever, and watch Rigby take an hour to eat a quarter of a banana.</span></p>
<div class="wp-caption aligncenter" id="attachment_174726" style="width: 646px;"><img alt="" class=" wp-image-174726" height="524" src="https://www.theparisreview.org/blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/img-2454-scaled-e1786479595247-1024x844.jpeg" width="636" /><p class="wp-caption-text" id="caption-attachment-174726">Photograph by Claire Carusillo.</p></div>
<p><b>June 21</b></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">We’ve been invited to our first party in Midway, at a remarkable home with a kitchen-island-size cheese plate and a DJ barn. During cocktail hour, we discover that most of the attendees left the Mormon Church in the past decade or so. They’re all forthcoming about their personal lives and the fact that they’re learning how to socialize with drink for the first time in their midfifties. Galen doesn’t drink, but I have three White Claws and talk to so many interesting dads.</span><span style="font-weight: 400;"> </span></p>
<p><b>June 24</b></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Orange smoke from forest fires is enveloping our valley, but incidentally, it makes the pink-stained plywood in the book room glow.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I stay up too late reading Kaitlin Tiffany’s feminist, Warren Commission–questioning tome </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Housewives Underground</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">. I recommend it to Mary Ann, a former children’s librarian who moved here from Dallas. I don’t tell her that back in the day Kaitlin and I worked on different floors of the same office, and that she and Lizzie Plaugic cold-Slacked me, asking me to join their writing group, possibly changing the trajectory of my entire life.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Mary Ann tells me she was there the day Kennedy was assassinated, working admin as a college student at Parkland Hospital. Of course, her memories of the weeks when the Texas governor John Connally, who was allegedly punctured by the same magic bullet that had just killed Kennedy, was legislating from his hospital bed don’t jive with what the Warren Commission reported. She says she was part of the team that procured a phone line for him. </span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I text Kaitlin and ask her, as a friend, not an author or an expert, what she thinks happened that day. She asks me my theories. I don’t feel comfortable sharing them until I learn more about Fidel Castro’s household staff at the Habana Hilton.</span></p>
<p><b>June 25</b></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">An email hits our shop inbox from our hacky sack manufacturer, an empath. It begins:</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">“Galen—I hate that I have to write you this email.”</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The custom-crocheted sacks have been held up in customs.</span></p>
<p><b> June 26</b></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Smoke everywhere, still. A huge sign is erected on Main Street alerting us to a thousand-dollar fine for lighting fireworks. In the past eight summers of living with Mars, our Jindo dog, I’ve come to hate them anyway. He’s jumpy and has seemingly learned to open locks with his mind.</span><span style="font-weight: 400;"> </span></p>
<p><b>July 1</b></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Elliott and Rebekah invite us to a barbecue at their house in Wallsburg, a tiny farm town tucked into a mountain pass halfway to Provo. We’ve become obsessed with Wallsburg town lore because (a) Butch Cassidy’s gang used to hide out there, allegedly, and (b) its streets now seem to be governed by packs of semiferal dogs. </span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The three hundred residents of Wallsburg are currently mobilizing against a newly created “preliminary municipality pilot program,” which gives access to two developers in Utah each calendar year to establish new towns on undeveloped land without the voting consent of nearby neighbors. Elliott and Rebekah used to run a hyperlocal newspaper called the </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Little Valley Dispatch</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, where they got to the bottom of such matters. Now, they’re focusing on raising two children and being software engineers.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">We eat burgers made from a Jersey cow that used to be their across-the-street neighbor. I’m warned against approaching Mr. Handsome, their adult ram, because he’s a literalist. Mars, who was also invited to the barbecue, won’t stop hissing at their cat Maomao.</span></p>
<p><b>July 4</b></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Now I’m avoiding the mayor’s calls and texts. He’s got a great deal of books on silver mining, a personal interest of mine, but we can’t seem to find the time to get over to his house to look at them.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">We approve Mars’s request to work from home. This is the polite way of saying that he won’t stop barking at small children and for now is not fit to be a shop dog. Later, we discover that he ate part of our bedroom window shade.</span><span style="font-weight: 400;"> </span></p>
<p><b>July 6</b></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Four-sixths of my immediate family are in Utah to help with the shop. Alex and Ani, my brother and my sister-in-law, are off somewhere being Swedish businessmen. Julia, my sister, who designed the shop interior for us, flew in from Denver and affixes hand-painted </span><span style="font-weight: 400;"><small>STAFF PICKS</small></span><span style="font-weight: 400;"> and </span><span style="font-weight: 400;"><small>EMPLOYEES ONLY</small></span><span style="font-weight: 400;"> signs to fixtures and walls. She suggests a </span><span style="font-weight: 400;"><small>DAD’S PICKS</small></span><span style="font-weight: 400;"> sign, as our dad has just donated twenty-five David Baldacci books to our collection. He sits in the corner eating an enormous sandwich while reading Stanley Tucci’s </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">What I Ate in One Year.</span></i></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Mom gets behind the bar with Avery and takes payments. Galen even overheard her asking, “Would you like room for cream?” to a guy who ordered a coffee. Total natural.</span><span style="font-weight: 400;"> </span></p>
<p><b>July 7</b></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Day off. At the pool, we pass around Mom’s Kindle (font size 3,000) to read David Sedaris’s essay on Lily, the purple-haired teen avatar who hosts a podcast on Duolingo, in </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Land and Its People</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">. Julia and I have been using Duolingo to relearn Spanish, and we both think Lily should ditch her K-pop-obsessed bestie Zari—she seems a little immature.</span></p>
<p><b>July 10</b></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Brandon the physical therapist comes in wearing the same shirt as Galen. It says </span><span style="font-weight: 400;"><small>I&#8217;M CRYING BECAUSE I LOVE SKATEBOARDING SO MUCH.</small> </span></p>
<p><b>July 11</b></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Avery, Galen, and I go to the crick with Charlie and Ozge after we close up the shop. To celebrate one month in business, we show them a little local secret called “taking the autobahn.” The way I like to do it is to wrap a gnawed pool noodle around my waist and jump into the river to be washed away by the current until the riverbed gets shallow enough to scrape against my ass cheeks. Then you walk back up the river, against the current, and do it again until you start bleeding.</span></p>
<p><b>July 12</b></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">We sell a brand of skater-owned hot sauce called Satan’s Drain Cleaner, and we’ve been asked if we were a Satanist business. I honestly hadn’t thought about it. Probably not.</span></p>
