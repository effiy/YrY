<p><img alt="Eclipse Series 49: Five Radical Documentaries by Kazuo Hara and Sachiko Kobayashi" class="attachment-thumbnail-large size-thumbnail-large wp-post-image" height="480" src="https://www.slantmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/eclipseseries49-720x480.jpg" width="720" /></p><p>Hara Kazuo’s documentaries, produced by his wife Kobayashi Sachiko, are as fearless and combative as the human subjects at their center. And while the couple’s willingness to unflinchingly examine the actions of these subjects often leads them to capture people pushing, and at times crossing, the boundaries of social norms and conventions, it’s always in service of working toward justice and equality for social outsiders.</p>
<p>Hara’s 1972 debut, <em>Goodbye CP</em>, follows members of the disability activist group Green Lawn, all of whom have cerebral palsy. The documentary makes no attempt to valorize or evoke pity for anyone on screen. Hara presents a warts-and-all portrait of his subjects, from group leader Yokota Hiroshi as he moves about Tokyo on his hands and knees, refusing to use a wheelchair against his wife’s wishes, to the many others who discuss their troubling sexual histories. Through it all, the film never loses touch with their humanity.</p>

<p><em>Extreme Private Eros: Love Song 1974</em> finds Hara shifting to a more personal mode of filmmaking. Following his ex-wife, radical feminist Takeda Miyuki, in the years after their divorce, the aptly titled film is often distressingly intimate, offering glimpses into Takeda’s sexual escapades with both men and women as she yearns for self-realization.</p>

<p>Seemingly nothing was off-limits to the camera, including Takeda’s intense fights with various partners and the birth of her second child. By not shying away from any verbal or emotional conflict, <em>Extreme Private Eros</em> shatters the cinematic barrier between private and public, challenging Japanese audiences to confront things they’d rather ignore.</p>

<p>That ignorance is very much in the crosshairs of <a href="/film/the-emperors-naked-army-marches-on/"><em>The Emperor’s Naked Army Marches On</em></a>, Hara’s magnum opus. In the 1987 documentary classic, Hara and Kobayashi’s subject, Okuzaki Kenzō, is relentlessly determined to get to the bottom of atrocities committed by the Japanese military in World War II. His forthrightness—and his unflagging need to dig up the past in search of truth and justice—puts him at particular odds with older generations.</p>
<p>Okuzaki—who served time in prison in 1970 for firing pachinko balls at Emperor Shōwa and then again in 1976 for distributing pornographic images of the emperor—appears as an agent of chaos, refusing to allow guilty military leaders to hide behind platitudes like allowing the dead to rest in peace. Whether or not Okuzaki is a madman is left up to the viewer, but the extent of the atrocities that have been covered up helps explain his excessive behavior, from beating up a weakened older man to shooting another man’s son.</p>

<p>With 1994’s <em>A Dedicated Life</em>, Hara further exhibits his fascination with mavericks and iconoclasts, following controversial author Inoue Mitsuharu for the last four years of his life. We are witness not only to the novelist’s womanizing ways and harsh methodology with his writing students, but also to his confrontations with his deteriorating body as it’s plagued by cancer.</p>

<p>This interest in physical decline also powers the final film in the Criterion Collection’s set devoted to Hara and Kobayashi’s work, 2016’s <em>Sennan Asbestos Disaster</em>. Its sociopolitical indignation is consistent with that of <em>Goodbye CP</em>, tracing various lawsuits against the Japanese government for knowingly exposing workers and townspeople in Osaka to asbestos from local factories. Over its 214-minute runtime, the film meticulously details both anti-Korean and classist impulses behind the government’s malfeasance and the physical ramifications that many of the plaintiffs suffered. And befitting Hara and Kobayashi’s shared, unwavering interest in the disenfranchised, it’s a necessarily challenging watch.</p>

</a> is available on Blu-ray on August 25 from the Criterion Collection</em>.</p>
