<p><strong>(edited July 1st) I feel like most of you are missing the point of my piece. Which means that I am a poor writer. Allow me to clarify: I don&#8217;t know if Clickhouse is &#8220;winning the observability wars&#8221; or not, and I don&#8217;t care how many observability companies are backed by columnar storage. (At this point, presumably all of them are.) But despite adopting better storage systems, they go on cargo culting the &#8220;three pillars&#8221; model instead of treating telemetry like data. Which forces their customers to store the same data over and over again in different formats, which destroys the fabric of your telemetry, and is <a href="https://charitydotwtf.substack.com/p/your-data-is-made-powerful-by-context">worth less than the sum of its parts</a>. </strong></p><p><strong>All of this is terrible for customers. A better storage engine can&#8217;t beat bad architecture. (/edit)</strong></p><p>We interrupt our regularly scheduled series of posts on AI norms and values to bring you this incredible piece from <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/mathew-duggan-36a39618/">Mat Duggan</a>, &#8220;<a href="https://matduggan.com/clickhouse-is-winning-the-observability-wars/">Why Clickhouse Is Winning the Observability Wars</a>,&#8221; which you should go read right now. Go! I&#8217;ll wait.</p><p>Mat starts by describing the experience every developer starts with, logging on the command line &#8212; &#8220;the observability equivalent of a first kiss, that ruins you for everything after&#8221; &#8212; followed by the descent into hell as four services becomes forty, becomes four hundred, and the impossibility of satisfying stakeholders in engineering, data, customer support, and executives, who all want different impossible things before breakfast.</p><div class="captioned-image-container"><figure><a class="image-link image2" href="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!8pQz!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F213a1650-4732-4c62-9874-0ddb1a7478e6_1873x187.png" target="_blank"><div class="image2-inset"><source type="image/webp" /><img alt="" class="sizing-normal" height="145" src="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!8pQz!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F213a1650-4732-4c62-9874-0ddb1a7478e6_1873x187.png" width="1456" /><div></div></div></a></figure></div><p>As Mat says, at 1TB a day, every modern observability stack is fine. Pick something and be productive. But at 10TB/day, they all become unmanageable &#8212; all, he says, except Clickhouse.</p><p>(My favorite part of the rant, because I am a bad person and/or an SRE, is where he meticulously details exactly how Elastic, LGTM, and Datadog [see errata posted at the end] fall apart at scale. This is a boy with scar tissue, respect.)</p><h2>Few observability tools can handle 10TB/day</h2><p>It&#8217;s hardly news that data tools tend to start off easy to run, but require teams to run at scale. Clickhouse, though &#8212; let&#8217;s hear Mat gush:</p><blockquote><p>&#8220;Every other observability backend I&#8217;ve worked with mutates as it grows... ClickHouse at 10 TB a day looks like ClickHouse at 1 TB a day with more shards. That&#8217;s it. That&#8217;s the pitch. That&#8217;s the whole reason I&#8217;m writing this.&#8221;</p></blockquote><p><span>Mat admits that Clickhouse has a small tax up front, but worth it since a tiny bit of upfront effort buys you SO MUCH EASE down the road, as you scale indefinitely, with high cardinality data and no messy schema lock-ins or performance cliffs.</span></p><p><span>Wow, this is starting to sound like a Clickhouse ad, isn&#8217;t it? Why am I boosting Clickhouse's reputation like this?</span></p><div class="captioned-image-container"><figure><a class="image-link image2" href="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!eZR6!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F3ae0bf1c-8595-4608-87f4-b1a4081a174e_1870x163.png" target="_blank"><div class="image2-inset"><source type="image/webp" /><img alt="" class="sizing-normal" height="127" src="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!eZR6!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F3ae0bf1c-8595-4608-87f4-b1a4081a174e_1870x163.png" title="" width="1456" /><div></div></div></a></figure></div><p><span>Because it isn't just fucking Clickhouse, you numbnuts.</span></p><h2><span>Observability backed by columnar storage is a different class of tool than the three pillars</span></h2><p><span>Mat&#8217;s piece isn&#8217;t actually about who is &#8220;winning&#8221; the observability wars, it&#8217;s about how Mat just discovered what it's like using observability powered by a columnar storage engine, and it is </span><em><span>blowing his everloving mind</span></em><span>. </span></p><p><span>And it should. It is genuinely better, and this is genuinely exciting! </span></p><p><span>And we have been talking about this for the past </span><em><span>ten years</span></em><span>.</span><a class="footnote-anchor" href="#footnote-1" id="footnote-anchor-1" target="_self">1</a><span> How is this still news to people? </span></p><p><span>We wrote about this </span><em><span>at length</span></em><span> in the first edition of &#8220;</span><a href="https://www.amazon.com/Observability-Engineering-Achieving-Production-Excellence/dp/1492076449"><span>Observability Engineering</span></a><span>&#8221;, devoting over a tenth of the book to this topic. It isn&#8217;t until multiple vendors link arms and say the same thing together that people sit up and note that the landscape really has changed. </p><p>I always assumed that would happen naturally once more observability companies were designed and built on columnar storage. We would align on some shared technical vocabulary due to our keen shared interest in clarifying the landscape for buyers, to help them understand how much <em>better</em> it is. The upstarts vs the incumbents. The shiny new way vs the kludgey, slow, painful old way.</p><div class="captioned-image-container"><figure><a class="image-link image2" href="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!yECZ!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F97ad3d33-bc54-4685-9e68-f4b686d7eb1d_1297x257.png" target="_blank"><div class="image2-inset"><source type="image/webp" /><img alt="" class="sizing-normal" height="257" src="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!yECZ!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F97ad3d33-bc54-4685-9e68-f4b686d7eb1d_1297x257.png" title="" width="1297" /><div></div></div></a></figure></div><p>Well, I was half right. Every observability company founded post-2019 has been built on top of a columnar store, did you know that? But not only have they not linked arms with us, they keep insisting there&#8217;s nothing all that new or different about what they&#8217;re building. They&#8217;re all selling &#8220;Datadog, but cheaper.&#8221; </p><h2>Why are newer observability vendors nerfing their own products and obscuring the difference?</h2><p>As a consequence, most of these newer observability tools are, despite being built on columnar storage engines, not that different from Datadog. </p><p>Same old three pillars. Same old problems with cardinality and scale, give or take. They are doing this because the weakness that they sense in Datadog is all price.</p><p>The weakness I sense in Datadog is all product. </p><p>Yes, it&#8217;s cheaper to run on columnar storage. But lower price is a consequence of better architecture, and honestly, it&#8217;s one of the least interesting consequences. Infra logs and metrics are commodities, but observability for your own products, your own code? <a href="https://charity.wtf/p/the-cost-crisis-in-observability-tooling">That should be an investment</a>.</p><div class="captioned-image-container"><figure><a class="image-link image2" href="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!weuC!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F9024733e-b245-4afb-b947-904a0fa3f476_1871x159.png" target="_blank"><div class="image2-inset"><source type="image/webp" /><img alt="" class="sizing-normal" height="124" src="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!weuC!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F9024733e-b245-4afb-b947-904a0fa3f476_1871x159.png" title="" width="1456" /><div></div></div></a></figure></div><p>Everybody thinks Datadog is expensive because they choose to be expensive. This is true in part. But they also don&#8217;t really have a choice. Their business model has grown up around a thirty-year-old architecture, and they are fully locked in to it.</p><h2>Will AI be enough to finally shake off the death grip of the three pillars?</h2><p>As AI starts accounting for a larger share of workloads, people are beginning to realize that the trace is all that matters. <a href="https://www.langchain.com/blog/in-software-the-code-documents-the-app-in-ai-the-traces-do">The trace</a> &#8212; or <a href="https://kislayverma.com/programming/publish-events-not-logs/">wide, structured</a> <a href="https://isburmistrov.substack.com/p/all-you-need-is-wide-events-not-metrics?open=false">canonical logs</a>, <a href="https://alok87.in/blogs/observability-wide-events">one or the other</a> &#8212; <a href="https://x.com/joshclemm/status/2010169487778295939">is all you need</a>. </p><p>One powerful, context rich data set is worth more than the sum of its pillars, because <a href="https://charitydotwtf.substack.com/p/your-data-is-made-powerful-by-context">relationships are what makes data valuable</a>. Slice it up into pillars, and you destroy that value for good.</p><div class="captioned-image-container"><figure><a class="image-link image2" href="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!-pXS!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F059b5b8f-264f-4527-9cea-a4ea8704426a_1281x217.png" target="_blank"><div class="image2-inset"><source type="image/webp" /><img alt="" class="sizing-normal" height="217" src="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!-pXS!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F059b5b8f-264f-4527-9cea-a4ea8704426a_1281x217.png" title="" width="1281" /><div></div></div></a></figure></div><p>One way or another, people are gonna figure this out, and that poses an enormous threat to Datadog&#8217;s business model, which relies on storing (and charging for) the same data over and over and over and over again in different formats, then storing (and charging for) links between datasets, and all the attendant fees and costs.</p><p>Or maybe people <em>won&#8217;t</em> figure it out. God knows I never thought the swindle could go on this long.</p><h2>Is Clickhouse winning the observability wars? God I hope so</h2><p>Clickhouse has used the term &#8220;<a href="https://clickhouse.com/blog/ai-redrawing-database-market">observability 2.0</a>&#8221; <a href="https://clickhouse.com/resources/engineering/observability">two times</a> by my count, making them the only other vendor who has. Why is Clickhouse willing to link arms, kinda, but no one else has? </p><div class="captioned-image-container"><figure><a class="image-link image2" href="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!-Ku5!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F03d3556a-855b-4cba-96ce-4c27b0668919_1871x189.png" target="_blank"><div class="image2-inset"><source type="image/webp" /><img alt="" class="sizing-normal" height="147" src="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!-Ku5!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F03d3556a-855b-4cba-96ce-4c27b0668919_1871x189.png" width="1456" /><div></div></div></a></figure></div><p>My theory is because they are a database company, not an observability company, and therefore less attached to marketing themselves as a cheaper Datadog. Unlike most observability vendors, they have decided to prioritize building something that solves customer problems over building something easy to market and sell.</p><p><s>Clickhouse, if you&#8217;re reading this, I would love to coordinate more. Call me.</s> OMG this was not an invitation for every clickhouse partner, vendor, and third party startup to reach out, <em>please stop</em>.</p><p><span>So yeah. If you want to run your own logs clusters, you should use </span><a href="http://clickhouse.com"><span>Clickhouse</span></a><span>. If you don&#8217;t want to DBA your own clusters or deal with the overhead (which scales linearly, as Mat points out, but is nontrivial), you should use </span><a href="http://honeycomb.io"><span>Honeycomb</span></a><span>.</span></p><p><span>It really is a whole new world. </span><a href="https://matduggan.com/clickhouse-is-winning-the-observability-wars/"><span>Just ask Mat Duggan</span></a><span>.</span></p><p>~charity</p><p></p><p>Errata: As written, my essay suggests that Datadog is not on columnar storage. This is false. They ARE on columnar storage &#8212; a truly impressive engine called Husky &#8212; and have been since 2022. However, the architecture has not changed. They collect metrics, logs, traces, exceptions, errors, profiling data, and every other type of signal separately. You pay to store it separately, again and again, and then you pay for every bit you&#8217;d like to correlate across any pair of datasets.</p><p>That&#8217;s what I mean, when I say they&#8217;re running on an ancient architecture, and it is astronomically expensive and objectively worse from a product perspective.</p><p>As I&#8217;ve written before: your data is made powerful by context. The richer your data, the more exponentially, combinatorially powerful it becomes. When you break it apart at write time, you cripple its capacity forever.</p><p>This is fine for infrastructure logs and metrics &#8212; the exhaust pipe of third party software. It is not fit for your crown jewels, your own source code, the telemetry you use to make sense of your own product and customers. It is both cheaper and exponentially more powerful to store that telemetry in a single source of truth, then derive individual metrics, logs, traces, errors, etc from that singular source.</p><p>My apologies to Datadog. I am not annoyed at Datadog in the slightest &#8212; I have a great deal of respect for their engineering org and go to market machine. I am annoyed at newer observability vendors who could do better and choose not to.</p><div class="footnote"><a class="footnote-number" contenteditable="false" href="#footnote-anchor-1" id="footnote-1" target="_self">1</a><div class="footnote-content"><p>Further references (far from comprehensive):</p><ul><li><p>A post on the <a href="https://charity.wtf/p/the-cost-crisis-in-observability-tooling">cost crisis in observability</a>, which tackles the three pillars architecture vs the columnar-backed o11y 2.0 model.</p></li><li><p>We started calling columnar storage-backed o11y &#8220;<a href="https://charity.wtf/p/is-it-time-to-version-observability-signs-point-to-yes">Observability 2.0</a>&#8220; in 2023.</p></li><li><p>One <a href="https://x.com/mipsytipsy/status/1098781661095813120">long twitter rant</a>, and <a href="https://x.com/mipsytipsy/status/1044668453339172864?s=20">another</a>, and <a href="https://x.com/mipsytipsy/status/1044666259898593282">another</a>.
