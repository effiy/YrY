<p><img alt="Sometimes Your Job is to Get in the Way" class="webfeedsFeaturedVisual" height="418" src="https://randsinrepose.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/chilipalmer.jpg" width="800" /></p><p>The Head of Product, Customer Service, and the CTO of Slack were sitting in the front row at an important conference. This was peak Slack; we were all the buzz, the fastest-growing enterprise software company in history. The conference speakers were singing out praises, and at that precise moment, Slack was down. Again.</p>
<p>I wish I had kept the message the CTO sent me. It approximately read, &#8220;Stop all releases immediately. If we can&#8217;t keep the service up, we shouldn&#8217;t be checking in any code.&#8221;</p>
<p>Game on.</p>
<h2>A Necessary Line in the Sand</h2>
<p><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elmore_Leonard">Elmore Leonard</a> wrote the book <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Get_Shorty">Get Shorty</a> in 1990. Chances are, you know the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Get_Shorty_(film)">movie</a> starring John Travolta better than the book. In the movie, Travolta plays Chili Palmer, a small-time loan shark from Miami. He&#8217;s part of a New York-based crime family, and he ends up traveling to Hollywood, where he inserts himself into the movie-making process.</p>
<p>Chili Palmer loves movies. He has deep knowledge of the history of movies and of Hollywood. The moment he shows up, he&#8217;s educating insiders about the history of Orson Welles&#8217;s Touch of Evil. It&#8217;s never explained where Chili&#8217;s love of movies came from, but it is clear from the beginning that he wants to make movies.</p>
<p>It turns out the skills of a loan shark apply nicely to film production:</p>
<ul>
<li>Find money,</li>
<li>Convincing skeptical people that you have a movie,</li>
<li>Charming humans to help you make a movie, and</li>
<li>Clearly drawing a line in the sand when necessary.</li>
</ul>
<p>Chili Palmer&#8217;s defining characteristic is conveyed by the line he speaks when he draws a clear line in the sand. When asked, &#8220;Who are you?&#8221; Chili replies, &#8220;I&#8217;m the one telling you how it is.&#8221; Reading this line does not do its delivery justice. Spoken just once, it defines his no-nonsense leadership style for the rest of the film.</p>
<p></p>
<h2>Keep Slack Up</h2>
<p>I was the VP of Engineering, so I took the message from the CTO and sent it to the Operations VP, &#8220;Shut down pushes to production.&#8221; I grabbed my Chief of Staff, and we quickly built a list of names we needed in a conference room right now. A combination of VPs and senior leaders who collectively had the best picture of how Slack worked.</p>
<p>&#8220;We&#8217;re meeting here now. Come immediately.&#8221;</p>
<p>Thirty minutes later, with everyone in the room, I stood up and stated, &#8220;Starting today, we are rebuilding our development pipeline to prevent outages. Who wants to start?&#8221;</p>
<p>What started as an eight-hour meeting turned into five different working groups defined by that initial crew, each task a different investigation into how to fix our development process. Those working groups turned into a series of tools and practices that — three months later — completely changed how software was developed, tested, and pushed to production. Downtime didn&#8217;t vanish, but became an anomaly. In hindsight, it&#8217;s obvious that we needed to take drastic action in order to change the engineering culture of the company. Of course, we didn&#8217;t want Slack to go down, so why didn&#8217;t we act sooner?</p>
<p>In the moment when you are running as fast as you can, and everyone is cheering, it&#8217;s hard to see imminent disaster. More importantly, it&#8217;s unclear who is responsible for taking action. <em>Someone, I&#8217;m sure, will say something when it&#8217;s actually really bad. I&#8217;m sure. Right?</em></p>
<p>I recently argued <a href="https://randsinrepose.com/archives/sometimes-your-job-is-to-stay-the-hell-out-of-the-way/">sometimes it&#8217;s your job to stay the hell out of the way</a>. Just use small nudges, a quiet word here and there, but demonstrate trust by staying out of the way. You know what&#8217;s harder? Standing in front of the team and telling them, &#8220;I&#8217;m the one telling you how it is.&#8221;</p>
<p>Chili Palmer&#8217;s line sticks with me because it&#8217;s equal parts charisma and competence. As a leader, you have a burden to tell them how it is. It&#8217;s often a last resort move. It&#8217;s a get-their-attention move. And it&#8217;s not without risk.</p>
<h2>A Cadillac of Minivans</h2>
<p>Early in the movie, Chili arrives at LAX and is driven by a bus to his rental car. It&#8217;s pouring rain, and the lot is empty save for a single minivan. As Chili gets out of the rental bus, he sees the minivan and tells the driver, &#8220;Wait, I ordered a Cadillac.&#8221; To which the bus driver confidently responds, &#8220;That&#8217;s the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oldsmobile_Silhouette">Oldsmobile Silhouette</a>. It&#8217;s the Cadillac of minivans!&#8221; Chili reluctantly gets in the car.</p>
<p>Later in the movie, Chili introduces Danny Devito&#8217;s character, a famous vain method actor, to the minivan. Devito sees the minivan, &#8220;Hey Chili, is this your ride?&#8221;</p>
<p>&#8220;Yeah, I like to sit up high and check everything out. I mean, it is the Cadillac of minivans,&#8221; as he clicks the key and the side door automatically slides open. He delivers the line with the same charisma and competence.</p>
<p>By the end of the movie, much later when Chili has become a movie producer, the closing overhead shot shows the parking lot at a studio&#8230; full of Oldsmobile Silhouettes.</p>
<p>That&#8217;s the choice. That&#8217;s the risk.</p>
<p>Stay the hell out of the way? Or tell them how it is? The discipline isn&#8217;t picking a stance — it&#8217;s knowing which moment you&#8217;re in. Stay out of the way at the wrong moment, and Slack stays down. Step in at the wrong moment and you&#8217;ve sold the team a Cadillac of minivans.</p>
