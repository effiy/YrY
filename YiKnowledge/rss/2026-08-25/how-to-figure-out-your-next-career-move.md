What kind of career progress do I want?</span></strong></h2><p><span>About a year ago, we coached an individual who,</span><strong><span> </span></strong><span>after spending several years at a startup that was running out of funding, assumed his next step was another early role in a venture-backed company.</span></p><p><span>After evaluating his core motivations and reflecting on his growing desire for impact, he found an opportunity to lead the entrepreneurship center at a local university, a job that he previously would have avoided and that none of his peers would have recommended. When he met with the administration, they effectively hired him on the spot, telling him, &#8220;You are exactly who we were looking for.&#8221; He&#8217;s now crushing it in his new role.</span></p><p><span>This story demonstrates the difference between career &#8220;progress&#8221; and &#8220;progression,&#8221; a distinction that is critical to understand when you are at a transition point in your career.</span></p><p><strong><span>Progression</span></strong><span> is the philosophy that underpins most people&#8217;s mental model of careers. You start as an IC, get promoted, and up and up you go on the imaginary career ladder. This mindset will become increasingly less relevant as AI continues its march through knowledge work. Job boundaries and job titles will continue to blur, new job titles will emerge, and companies will struggle to neatly map job descriptions and career pathways into their org chart or KPIs.</span></p><p><strong><span>Progress</span></strong><span>, on the other hand, is contextual and dynamic. It is ultimately a question of what your professional Job to Be Done is, and allows you to chart your own course rather than follow a single, narrow path of progression. Depending on your context, progress may be about autonomy and flexibility, preparing for the future, respect and recognition, or simply collecting a paycheck during a time of instability. It can look like a more traditional step up, but it may also look like a lateral move or even like a step back from the perspective of others&#8212;while it solves the most important problem in your life and gives you space and time to move forward later on.</span></p><p><span>A focus on progress is an active commitment to putting yourself in environments that will bring out your best. It helps you stand out amid the army of applicants all reaching for the same rung on the career ladder. Progress gives you the freedom to see your career in chapters and create space to be </span><a href="https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/what-if-youre-not-supposed-to-have"><span>emergent</span></a><span> rather than assuming you have to write one linear story all at once. To do this, you have to define the progress you&#8217;re seeking right now.</span></p><h3><span>A 10-minute progress exercise</span></h3><p><span>Take a blank page (or, if you&#8217;d like a live template, we made one for you </span><a href="https://try.joinvocation.com/progress-finder"><span>here</span></a><span>) and complete the sentence: </span><strong><span>I want to leave or change my current situation because&#8230;</span></strong></p><p><span>Write down every friction or negative force you&#8217;re experiencing right now, or that you last experienced at work. What career quest am I on?</span></strong></h2><p><span>When you are seriously considering a job change, or when change is forced upon you, it&#8217;s easy to label and hold onto your most immediate feeling, whether that&#8217;s burnout, the excitement of something new, frustration, or disrespect. But underlying these emotions, less-obvious and subconscious factors are working together in a unique combination to animate your core motivation for change. This is your </span><strong><span>career quest</span></strong><span>.</span></p><p><span>Your quest for progress should inform every aspect of your job search, but most people skip this diagnosis and go directly to job boards, answer the recruiter&#8217;s call, or ask their network about job openings. They are halfway out the door before deciding what they even need this move to accomplish.</span></p><p><span>For example, I once coached an individual who was frustrated by the lack of leadership opportunities in his product role at a large tech company. When he was recruited to lead and grow the analytics function at a nearby startup, he felt excited and flattered to finally have the opportunity to grow a team.</span></p><p><span>But as we explored other pushes and pulls acting on him, a more nuanced story emerged. Despite the lack of leadership opportunities, he fundamentally liked his job. He was also in the middle of renovating his home, he wanted to be able to spend more time with his two young kids, and he wasn&#8217;t all that excited about leaving product for analytics.</span></p><p><span>The new role solved his most immediate and apparent problem, but it wouldn&#8217;t offer him the progress he needed both personally and professionally.</span></p><p><span>In our work with professionals looking to make a change, we have identified four common quests for progress that emerge again and again.</span></p><p><span>These quests are not mutually exclusive; you can be on multiple quests simultaneously, though one usually dominates. Even if you have been laid off and the immediate goal is &#8220;I need a job,&#8221; reflecting on these quests can help you decide which opportunities deserve your attention, and which you can avoid.</span></p><h3><span>Quest 1: Get out</span></h3><p><span>You have hit a wall you cannot fix from where you stand. Something about the manager, culture, leadership, or environment has made it hard to succeed or remain healthy. The dominant feeling is escape, and the desire to get out is usually quick, not something you stew on for months at a time.</span></p><p><strong><span>Common pushes:</span></strong></p><ul><li><p><span>Feeling disrespected or not trusted to do great work</span></p></li><li><p><span>Working for a manager who is wearing you down</span></p></li><li><p><span>Losing trust or respect in leadership and/or your peers</span></p></li><li><p><span>Not seeing a place to grow in your current organization</span></p></li></ul><p><strong><span>Common pulls:</span></strong></p><ul><li><p><span>The pulls are often vague: a fresh start, a healthier environment, or simply &#8220;anywhere but here.&#8221;</span></p></li><li><p><span>When someone is looking to get out, they often accept the first available escape but run the risk of landing in the same conditions at a different company.</span></p></li></ul><h3><span>Quest 2: Regain control</span></h3><p><span>Work has swallowed too much of your life, even if you still enjoy the work itself.</span></p><p><span>This quest often follows a change outside work: a new child, caregiving responsibilities, a health issue, or a shift in what you want your life to look like. It can also emerge when a previously manageable role becomes chaotic, or when the way your manager or company leadership is running things no longer works for you.</span></p><p><strong><span>Common pushes:</span></strong></p><ul><li><p><span>Feeling worn down by management</span></p></li><li><p><span>Work is dominating your life and you&#8217;re starting to make sacrifices</span></p></li><li><p><span>Unpredictable demands, or feeling challenged beyond your ability</span></p></li><li><p><span>Little control over your schedule or priorities</span></p></li><li><p><span>A workload that no longer feels sustainable</span></p></li></ul><p><strong><span>Common pulls:</span></strong></p><ul><li><p><span>More time to spend with others</span></p></li><li><p><span>A job location that fits better into your personal life</span></p></li><li><p><span>An employer who properly values your experience and credentials</span></p></li><li><p><span>A scope of responsibility you can sustain</span></p></li></ul><h3><span>Quest 3: Regain alignment</span></h3><p><span>Your job has drifted away from what you do best, what you value, or what you originally agreed to do. You may still be performing well. In some cases, that is part of the problem: you have become useful for work that does not fit you, and you don&#8217;t feel respected.</span></p><p><strong><span>Common pushes:</span></strong></p><ul><li><p><span>Being underused or miscast</span></p></li><li><p><span>Watching your responsibilities move away from your strengths</span></p></li><li><p><span>A growing gap between your values and the company&#8217;s</span></p></li><li><p><span>Feeling unchallenged or bored</span></p></li><li><p><span>Not seeing a clear place to grow inside the organization</span></p></li></ul><p><strong><span>Common pulls:</span></strong></p><ul><li><p><span>Work that uses your full capabilities</span></p></li><li><p><span>Greater alignment with your values</span></p></li><li><p><span>Recognition that matches your contribution</span></p></li><li><p><span>A role built around the things you do unusually well</span></p></li></ul><h3><span>Quest 4: Take the next step</span></h3><p><span>You have completed something meaningful, mastered the role, or reached a point where the work no longer stretches you. Alternatively, you&#8217;ve hit a personal milestone (baby, move, etc.) and want a change. You are not necessarily unhappy. You are simply ready to be challenged and get the support you need to grow.</span></p><p><strong><span>Common pushes:</span></strong></p><ul><li><p><span>Hitting a personal or professional milestone</span></p></li><li><p><span>Feeling unchallenged or bored</span></p></li><li><p><span>Not seeing a clear place to grow</span></p></li><li><p><span>Needing to provide more support for family or loved ones</span></p></li></ul><p><strong><span>Common pulls:</span></strong></p><ul><li><p><span>Feeling like a job is a clear step forward</span></p></li><li><p><span>Joining a tight-knit team you can count on</span></p></li><li><p><span>Developing new skills as part of a stepping stone to something else</span></p></li></ul><h3><span>A quick quest diagnostic</span></h3><p><span>Finish this sentence: </span><strong><span>More than anything, I need my next move to&#8230;</span></strong></p><ul><li><p><span>Get me away from a situation I can no longer tolerate.</span></p></li><li><p><span>Give me control over my time, workload, or life again.</span></p></li><li><p><span>Let me do work that fits my strengths and values.</span></p></li><li><p><span>Give me a new challenge or chapter.</span></p></li></ul><p><span>How you finish the sentence will not capture every part of your situation, but it will usually reveal which quest you may be on&#8212;and also help you label what you </span><em><span>don&#8217;t</span></em><span> need right now.</span></p><h2><strong><span>3. Which work brings out my best?</span></strong></h2><p><span>Most professionals know their skills better than they know the conditions that allow them to do their best work. You take on responsibilities, become known for certain outcomes, develop valuable skills, but are often too busy doing the job to understand the circumstances you need to be successful and feel fulfilled.</span></p><p><span>Then a shiny title or compensation increase appears, and you accept a role that actually makes you miserable&#8212;an outcome common to ICs-turned-managers who realize too late how much they hate management.</span></p><p><span>You need to figure out what you need </span><em><span>before</span></em><span> you switch jobs (or are compelled to switch), not figure it out </span><em><span>by</span></em><span> switching. This is what building your energy profile is all about: it provides a blueprint for evaluating new opportunities, along with a starting point for questions you can ask a hiring manager or peer as you explore open roles.</span></p><h3><span>Build your energy profile</span></h3><p><span>To get started, grab some Post-it notes or spin up a Miro board, and make a simple timeline of roles you&#8217;ve held and the title, company, and duration for each role (including your current role if employed).
