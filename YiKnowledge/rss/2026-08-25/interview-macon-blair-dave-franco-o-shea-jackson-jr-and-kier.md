<p><img alt="Macon Blair, Dave Franco, O&#039;Shea Jackson Jr., and Kiernan Shipka on Idiots" class="attachment-thumbnail-large size-thumbnail-large wp-post-image" height="480" src="https://www.slantmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/interviews_idiots-720x480.jpg" width="720" /></p><p>The title of Macon Blair’s new film, <a href="/film/idiots-review-dave-franco-oshea-jackson-jr/"><em>Idiots</em></a>, may make it easier to market, but the one it bore at its Sundance premiere, <em>The Shitheads</em>, is a better indicator of the central characters’ ineptitude. Actors Dave Franco, O’Shea Jackson Jr., and Kiernan Shipka were keen to keep that spirit alive in their matching press-day garb: pink hoodies with “Shitheads” emblazoned in reverse over the pocket. (Blair, for his part, wore a suit.)</p>
<p>Blair’s madcap comedy follows a road trip on which Franco’s Mark and Jackson’s Davis, two dimwitted employees of a transport service, attempt to take a troubled teen (Mason Thames’s Sheridan) to rehab. What seemed like an easy gig for money quickly spirals out of their control once the depths of their passenger’s psychopathy become apparent on the journey.</p>
<p>Sheridan’s antics bring Mark and Davis into contact with an assortment of wild characters, from a striving stripper (Kiernan Shipka’s Irina) to a wacky SoundCloud rapper (Nicholas Braun’s Pricka Bush da Werewoof). Any scene in <em>Idiots</em> can go from being hilarious to horrifying or heartwarming at a moment’s notice. What awaits Mark and Davis at their drop-off point proves to be more than just a paycheck—it’s also their personal reckoning.</p>
<p>Shortly after the Los Angeles premiere of <em>Idiots</em>, Blair, Franco, Jackson, and Shipka sat down with me to discuss what sold the actors on the script, why playing the grounded reality of a scene helped anchor the film through major tonal swings, and more.</p>


<p><strong>I’m so glad I saw this movie at its Sundance premiere, before anyone could even hint at what was ahead on the journey. For the actors, how were you sold on this story, and how did you react to where it goes?</strong></p>
<p>O’Shea Jackson Jr.: Dave pulled me in. He said there was a role he felt like I could really sink my teeth into, and he knew I’d perform well. I took that as kind of like a chip on my shoulder, some fire under me that one of my good friends believes in me and sees me killing this role. I felt like, “Alright, we gotta do this for Dave,” and then that turned into me learning more about Macon. When it comes to a Macon film, one of the factors that we love is that as soon as you feel like you’re safe, it keeps you [on the edge of] your seat. There’s no period to get comfortable at all. When you can laugh at something from the page, it’s probably going to be dope.</p>
<p>Dave Franco: First off, I’m obsessed with Macon’s movie <a href="/film/blue-ruin/"><em>Blue Ruin</em></a> that he’s the lead actor in. I re-watch that movie every single year. When I saw his <a href="/tv/i-dont-feel-at-home-in-this-world-anymore/"><em>I Don’t Feel at Home in This World Anymore</em></a>, I reached out and was just like, “Dude, I gotta find something [to make] with you because I fucking love what you do.” We’ve been talking basically since then. It’s been almost 10 years, and it finally culminated in this project. It became such an amazing collaboration. And as O’Shea said, every day you’re coming in and doing some insane scene that, on its own, is such a beautiful little short film. As an actor, it’s just such great material to sink your teeth into.</p>
<p>Kiernan Shipka: Yeah, it’s such a crazy script to get because it’s a very wild ride. You can feel that on the page. I admit, Macon, we’d had a little general Zoom maybe two years prior to when I got sent this, and it was like, “Wow, I know this is going to be so cool.” Knowing that this was your brainchild, and that this was going to be a Macon Blair film, made [me realize that this was going to be] amazing. It’s definitely the kind of movie that you want to make with people that you admire and trust, and that’s exactly what this group is.</p>

<p><strong>I know <em>Idiots</em> was less dark at the start of its development in 2017. Is this more serious side of the film something that developed more with time?</strong></p>
<p>Macon Blair: A little bit. There was always an idea that there would be this dark thing lurking in the background of what’s otherwise a broad comedy, and I do think maybe that got accentuated as it went on. But, really, it was a lot of practical stuff. The ages of the characters got a little bit younger. Being so disorganized in their lives started to feel like it made more sense when they were a little bit younger, which was perfect because I knew that Dave was out there and aware of this script. And if his age was right, it made it very easy to go to him first. He got on board, and then, as he mentioned, steered us toward O’Shea and Mason, which really helped shape the ensemble. And the whole movie is the ensemble. There are no big special effects. It’s a small movie, and it lives and dies by the ensemble. And that’s what I’m so proud of about it.</p>
<p>DF: But also, just going off of that, one great thing about all of Macon’s movies is that every single character has so much going on. Even a character that comes in for one scene, there’s a whole history there. There’s some sort of dirty past, and everyone has some fun physical thing too, whether it’s tattoos or just some sort of outfit. Like Nicholas Braun playing Pricka Bush da Werewoof, as represented on this hoodie right here!</p>
<p></p>
<p><strong>This is a movie where a character like that can live alongside a very sincere exploration of faith from Davis and, eventually, Mark. How did that thread enter the movie, and how did you hold onto something so genuine in the emotional core?</strong></p>
<p>OJ: When it came to Davis, I know a lot of people who live by their faith and look to that as their compass moving forward. I’m not necessarily religious, but I just know I believe in God. I’ve had my own conversations. I still think he’s the best writer ever, but has jokes at terrible times.</p>
<p>DF: Who’s that?</p>
<p>OJ: God! [laughs] Sometimes, I don’t need a laugh right now. But I really felt that with him when he would have real conversations. He feels like he’s speaking to the Lord. The vulnerability that I had to show for this role, I couldn’t have done at a different time period in my life. I’m a single Black father in America, trying to make it work, and I’ve lost a lot of people close to me. I had to lift my parents’ spirits after both of them lost their mom. I had real loss and real emotions, and I just let them shine through. And I feel that, as an audience, real recognize real. You can see when it’s genuine, and you get a feeling in your heart part.</p>
<p>DF: O’Shea’s character is the moral compass of this movie, and the person who’s making this movie has that extra depth you might not expect from a movie called <em>Idiots</em>. Whereas my character is the guy with the bicep tattoo of a delicate little bird singing the words “Who farted?” So it’s a very splashy, fun role for me, but at the end of the day, [O’Shea’s] character is bringing that weight [that lets] audiences walk away from this movie and think about their own lives.</p>
<p>OJ: I hella root for Mark.</p>
<p>DF: There we go! Okay!</p>
<p>OJ: You win them over.</p>
<p>DF: I mean, my character starts as a guy who’s skidding into a handicap parking spot. I’m digging myself a big hole from the beginning, and I’m gonna take this as a challenge to win over the audience by the end and make them like this guy.</p>


<p><strong>A moment of honest prayer can turn into a wild drug trip in an instant in <em>Idiots</em>. When you’re finding these tonal shifts within a scene, how do you pull off what O’Shea has called “the Macon switch” and make it feel alive?</strong> </p>
<p>KS: With a director like Macon who has such a vision, and with actors who are so extremely talented, it’s my job to do my job. I know Irina. I know what she wants in this scene. I know how she’s feeling. If my responsibility is myself, and also being present for the other actors, everything is going to sort itself out because everybody’s so good at what they do. I think that’s where you can drop your shoulders and relax a little bit. If everybody cares about what we’re making, everybody is a piece in this puzzle, and if we can all just be our best piece for ourselves and each other, it’s gonna work out. You almost have to not think about all of it and just trust that it’s going to show up a little bit from this person in the scene, a little bit from that thing in that scene. I’m just going to go in, do my thing, and hope that the story will hold all of it. </p>
<p>DF: The “Macon switch” is basically that one of the funniest moments in the movie is immediately followed by one of the darker moments in the movie on purpose. It’s like we were saying earlier: Macon’s gonna pull the rug out from you when you least suspect it. But in terms of just staying within the same tone, the script was so great that you just play the grounded reality of whatever the scene is. No matter how much crazy stuff is happening on the surface, play the grounded version, and that’ll make the tone feel consistent.</p>
<p>OJ: And I just want to pat you on the back, Kiernan. It’s rare to shoot scenes in order, but she has a scene where she’s rallying the troops, saying, “You guys need to lock in, this is what we’ve got to do,” and immediately has to go into a beautiful song at the drop of a hat. On that day, we really had to do it in that order, and [it was impressive] to be able to show that flexibility and range. And she wrote the song! A lot was happening in <em>Idiots</em>, man.</p>
<p>KS: The movie is like this big, wild adventure, and we were on that while we were filming it.  I also feel like [Blair] got a lot of different variations in takes. When we had the time, there were different tonal things that we played with, and then it all comes together in the edit.</p>

<p>MB: Yeah, just so we would have some options, but I think mainly what y’all are saying is about just keeping everything grounded and real. That’s always the best way, because then you don’t have to worry about tone. You’re just reacting to whatever it is in an honest fashion, and that’s the tone. It can be loud and noisy in that way, or it can be soft and sensitive in that way. But you’re all on the same track, so that’s what these folks were doing.</p>
<p>DF: But there could have been a version of this movie that was very heightened, and we could have pushed the humor really far.</p>
<p>MB: Well, 10 years ago, that was the version I was thinking of. Maybe that was a little bit more of a super-broad, honking type of comedy. And then, over time, the sadder version started to make more sense to me.</p>
<p>OJ: The palette of the audience changes over the years. It’s why this movie has that retro feel of like, “Man, I miss movies like this!” It’s not outdated or anything like that, but it scratches an itch you didn’t know you had. When you watch it, it’s like a satisfying ride that you just went on, and it hits you in layers.</p>
