<p class="article-paragraph skip">Signs of the <a href="https://www.popsci.com/science/why-childhood-summers-felt-longer/">waning summer</a> are everywhere. The sun is setting earlier, yellow <a href="https://www.popsci.com/technology/electric-school-bus/">school buses </a>are emerging from hibernation, and <a href="https://www.popsci.com/category/birds/">birds</a> are showing up on weather radar once again. The fall migration is in full swing and we humans can follow along with <a href="https://birdcast.org/">BirdCast.</a>&nbsp;</p>



<p class="article-paragraph skip">Over the next two nights (August 25 and 26), BirdCast predicts that over 200 million birds will begin their long journey towards their head for their wintering grounds. Over <a href="https://www.allaboutbirds.org/news/the-basics-how-why-and-where-of-bird-migration/">300North American bird species are long-distance migrants</a>, meaning they will travel hundreds to thousands of miles annually. These migrations can take days to weeks, depending on the species</p>



<p class="article-paragraph skip">BirdCast’s daily <a href="https://birdcast.org/migration-tools/migration-forecast-maps/">forecast maps</a> show their predicted nighttime migration patterns that begin roughly three hours after sunset. The brighter and warmer colors represent more birds expected overhead. They are also updated every six hours.</p>



<p class="article-paragraph skip">On August 25, expect the largest concentration of birds around Minnesota, North Dakota, and South Dakota.&nbsp;</p>

<p class="article-paragraph skip">If you are curious which bird species may be traveling through your area, the dashboard is integrated with <a href="https://ebird.org/home?continue">Cornell’s eBird database</a>. This database uses common bird sightings from the same time in previous years to make predictions.</p>



<p class="article-paragraph skip">To <a href="https://birdcast.org/migration-tools/migration-forecast-maps/">make these maps</a>, BirdCast uses weather data from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA), since their radar picks up large groups. It also uses weather forecasts to predict these nightly migration hotspots. A live map will also go up every night, so users can zero in on what might be going on in the skies where they live. BirdCast is made possible by participating scientists from Cornell University, the University of Massachusetts Amherst, Purdue University, and the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, and other contributors.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-to-migrate-or-not-to-migrate">To migrate, or not to migrate</h2>



<p class="article-paragraph skip">Typically, <a href="https://www.popsci.com/environment/why-do-birds-migrate/">birds migrate</a> for two reasons: nests and food. In the Northern Hemisphere, this means heading north during warmer months, when there are more insects and budding plant life to eat. During the spring, many species are also rushing to mate and nest.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="article-paragraph skip">Ahead of the colder months, some will return south to avoid freezing temperatures. Earlier in August, a famous, <a href="https://www.popsci.com/environment/footless-piping-plover-phoenix-maine-florida/">toeless piping plover (<em>Charadrius melodus</em>) named Phoenix</a> was spotted back in Florida, after spending the summer over 1,000 miles away in Maine.&nbsp;</p>



<figure class="wp-block-image size-full is-resized"><img alt="a small shorebird stands on a the beach" class="wp-image-781789" height="596" src="https://www.popsci.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/phoenix-piping-plover-florida-august.jpeg?strip=all&#038;quality=85" style="width: 721px; height: auto;" width="721" /><figcaption class="wp-element-caption">After spending several months in Maine, Phoenix the Footless was spotted back in Florida on August 10. <em>Image: Lorraine Margeson</em>.<br /> </figcaption></figure>



<p class="article-paragraph skip">However, not all birds head south. Many birds, including mallard ducks (<em>Anas platyrhynchos</em>), black-capped chickadees (<em>Poecile atricapillus</em>), and the turkey vulture (<em>Cathartes aura</em>) <a href="https://www.audubon.org/magazine/7-birds-who-will-never-leave-you-and-1-really-ought">don’t migrate </a>and fare just fine in snowy conditions.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="article-paragraph skip">At least 4,000 bird species (or roughly 40 percent of world’s known birds) are regular migrants. The Arctic tern wins the award for <a href="https://www.audubon.org/news/9-awesome-facts-about-bird-migration">longest migration of any bird in the world</a>. These can fly almost 50,000 miles in one year, when traveling between their wintering ground in the Antarctic north to their breeding grounds in the Arctic.&nbsp;</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading">How to help migrating birds</h2>



<p class="article-paragraph skip">As the migration continues over the next several weeks, there are some <a href="https://www.birds.cornell.edu/home/seven-simple-actions-to-help-birds/">easy ways to help</a> make sure that the birds safely make it to their destinations.&nbsp;</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>Add a film or decals to windows. This can <a href="https://www.homesandgardens.com/solved/methods-stop-birds-flying-into-windows-doors-outdoor-mirrors?utm_source=google&#038;utm_medium=h5d&#038;utm_campaign=h_hg_00016&#038;gad_source=1&#038;gad_campaignid=23933153240&#038;gbraid=0AAAABDiMi82zi_8itxzgkDV3BgmElIzlq&#038;gclid=CjwKCAjw-rTUBhAiEiwADv8gBPa-EUq2wdQ3sUmJYGuul9haDtIJG0DM_rNC6llrAxe2WNn56O5A9hoCYA0QAvD_BwE">help keep birds from colliding</a> with the glass.&nbsp;</li>



<li>Plant native plants and avoid pesticides when possible. This gives them safe places to land and eat.&nbsp;</li>



<li>Keep cats indoors. This is good advice in general, as housecats <a href="https://www.popsci.com/environment/outdoor-cats-spread-diseases/">can be destructive to the ecosystem</a>, but rings particularly true during bird migration.&nbsp;&nbsp;</li>



<li><a href="https://www.audubon.org/our-work/cities-and-towns/lights-out">Turn off all excess outdoor lights</a>. Too many lights can impact their navigation</li>
</ul>
