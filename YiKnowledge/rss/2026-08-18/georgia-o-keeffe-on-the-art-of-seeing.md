<h3>&#8220;To see takes time, like to have a friend takes time.&#8221;</h3>
<hr />
<p><a href="https://www.amazon.com/Georgia-OKeeffe-Elizabeth-Hutton-Turner/dp/0943044243/?tag=braipick-20" target="_blank"><img alt="Georgia O&#8217;Keeffe on the Art of Seeing" class="cover alignright size-medium" height="313" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/okeeffe_thepoetryofthings.jpg?fit=254%2C313&amp;ssl=1" width="254" /></a></p><p>In her stunning autobiographical reflection on <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2015/09/09/virginia-woolf-cotton-wool-moments-of-being/">the moment she understood what it means to be an artist</a>, Virginia Woolf beheld the cosmos of connections in a single flower. Decades later, the Nobel-winning physicist Richard Feynman offered a different, complementary lens on the art of seeing through his now-famous monologue known as <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2013/01/01/ode-to-a-flower-richard-feynman/">&#8220;Ode to a Flower.&#8221;</a></p>
<p>Before Feynman, before Woolf, another titan of the creative spirit found a powerful metaphor for how we experience the world &#8212; how we see it, and how we don&#8217;t &#8212; in a flower.</p>
<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" id="attachment_66259"><img alt="" class="size-full wp-image-66259" height="823" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/GeorgiaOkeeffe_RedCanna_1924.jpg?resize=680%2C823&#038;ssl=1" width="680" /><figcaption class="wp-caption-text">Georgia O&#8217;Keeffe, <em>Red Canna</em>, 1924 (Georgia O&#8217;Keeffe Museum)</figcaption></figure>
<p><em>&#8220;I found that I could say things with colour and shapes that I couldn&#8217;t say in any other way things that I had no words for,&#8221;</em> <strong>Georgia O&#8217;Keeffe</strong> (November 15, 1887&ndash;March 6, 1986) wrote in the foreword to a catalog for an exhibition of her work two decades before she became the first female artist honored with a retrospective at the Museum of Modern Art &#8212; a triumph largely predicated on her arresting large-scale paintings of flowers, magnified and abstracted to radiate uncommon emotional intensity haloed by awe. Although art critics consistently insisted that O&#8217;Keeffe&#8217;s depictions of flowers were her commentary on women&#8217;s sexuality, the artist herself resolutely denied these interpretations. For her, they were her commentary on seeing &#8212; a magnifying lens for the attention. Painting these close-ups was a way of learning to look, a way of removing the blinders with which we gallop through the world, slowing down, shedding our notions and concepts of things, and taking things in as they really are. </p>
<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" id="attachment_66260"><img alt="" class="size-full wp-image-66260" height="453" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/GeorgiaOkeeffe_RufusHolsinger.jpg?resize=680%2C453&#038;ssl=1" width="680" /><figcaption class="wp-caption-text">Georgia O&#8217;Keeffe by Rufus Holsinger, 1915 (Albert &#038; Shirley Small Special Collections Library)</figcaption></figure>
<p>In a passage originally published in the exhibition catalog <em>An American Place</em> &#8212; which also gave us O&#8217;Keeffe&#8217;s <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2018/05/17/two-hundred-years-of-blue/">serenade to blue</a> &#8212; and later cited in <a href="https://www.amazon.com/Georgia-OKeeffe-Elizabeth-Hutton-Turner/dp/0943044243/?tag=braipick-20" target="_blank"><strong><em>Georgia O&#8217;Keeffe: The Poetry of Things</em></strong></a> (<a href="http://www.worldcat.org/title/georgia-okeeffe-the-poetry-of-things/oclc/757564061&#038;referer=brief_results" target="_blank"><em>public library</em></a>), she writes:</p>
<blockquote><p>A flower is relatively small. Everyone has many associations with a flower &#8212; the idea of flowers. You put out your hand to touch the flower &#8212; lean forward to smell it &#8212; maybe touch it with your lips almost without thinking &#8212; or give it to someone to please them. Still &#8212; in a way &#8212; nobody sees a flower &#8212; really &#8212; it is so small &#8212; we haven&#8217;t time &#8212; and to see takes time, like to have a friend takes time. If I could paint the flower exactly as I see it no one would see what I see because I would paint it small like the flower is small.</p>
<p>So I said to myself &#8212; I&#8217;ll paint what I see &#8212; what the flower is to me but I&#8217;ll paint it big and they will be surprised into taking time to look at it &#8212; I will make even busy New-Yorkers take time to see what I see of flowers. </p>
<p>Well &#8212; I made you take time to look at what I saw and when you took time to really notice my flower, you hung all your own associations with flowers on my flower and you write about my flower as if I think and see what you think and see of the flower &#8212; and I don&#8217;t.</p></blockquote>
<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" id="attachment_66261"><img alt="" class="size-full wp-image-66261" height="1092" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/GeorgiaOkeeffe1925.jpg?resize=680%2C1092&#038;ssl=1" width="680" /><figcaption class="wp-caption-text">Georgia O&#8217;Keeffe, <em>Grey Lines with Black, Blue and Yellow</em>, 1923 (Georgia O&#8217;Keeffe Museum)</figcaption></figure>
<p>Complement with O&#8217;Keeffe on <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2014/09/17/georgia-o-keeffe-letters-anita-pollitzer/">setting priorities</a>, <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2014/12/08/georgia-okeeffe-sherwood-anderson-letters/">success, public opinion, and what it means to be an artist</a>, and her <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2011/10/28/my-faraway-one-love-letters-georgia-okeeffe-alfred-stieglitz/">passionate love letters</a> to Alfred Stieglitz, then revisit cognitive scientist Alexandra Horowitz on <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2013/08/12/on-looking-eleven-walks-with-expert-eyes/">the art of looking</a>, Annie Dillard on <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2015/03/04/annie-dillard-pilgrim-at-tinker-creek-seeing/">the secret to seeing</a>, philosopher Martin Buber on <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2018/09/11/martin-buber-tree/">what a tree can teach us about seeing others as they truly are</a>, John Ruskin on <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2015/07/10/john-ruskin-drawing/">how drawing trains you to see more clearly and live with greater presence</a>, and Emily Dickinson&#8217;s <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2017/05/23/emily-dickinson-herbarium/">astounding herbarium</a> &#8212; a forgotten masterpiece of attention at the intersection of poetry and science.</p>
