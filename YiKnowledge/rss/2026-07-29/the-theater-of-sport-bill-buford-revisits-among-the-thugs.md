<p><em>This month, we’re rolling out a new web series, One Book, which is more or less what it sounds like—an interview with an author about the making of just one of their books, not because that book has just been published (it could be decades old) or is in the news (though we’d be thrilled if it was), but because it is very good. This is the first installment. — Tarpley Hitt, online editor.</em></p>
<div style="text-align: center;">***</div>
<p><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">In 1984, Bill Buford, an American literary editor in his late twenties, began devoting his spare time to following “football hooligans”—a distinctive tribe of fans known for committing acts of violence and vandalism on match days. English soccer in those days was associated with brutality on the “terraces” (the standing area at stadiums) and in the streets before and after matches. Hooliganism was the so-called British disease, a source of national shame. Buford started going around with the Manchester United “firm,” or gang, known as the ICJ, or the Inter-City Jibbers. Later, he sought danger at a disco organized by the far-right group the National Front, and while traveling among</span></i> <i><span style="font-weight: 400;">England&#8217;s supporters at the World Cup.</span></i><span id="more-174589"></span></p>
<p><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Buford’s time in England had begun at a different social altitude. A Marshall Scholarship sent him from Berkeley to undertake a second B.A. at King&#8217;s College Cambridge, a grand establishment associated with members of the Bloomsbury Group, such as E. M. Forster and John Maynard Keynes. Buford fell in with the student journalism society, and worked to turn a failing undergraduate magazine, </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Granta<em>,</em></span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> into a leading journal of international writing. </span></i></p>
<p><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">At a glance, Buford’s two identities were chalk and cheese. But in his work overseeing </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Granta</span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, he displayed an appetite for danger, mischief, drama, and grit. The experiences Buford wrote up in </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Among the Thugs</span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> coincided with issues of </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Granta</span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> that featured a portrait of life on the road with the Rolling Stones in the late sixties (a piece he compared to Michael Herr’s writing on Vietnam), James Fenton’s account of the Snap Revolution in the Philippines, and a new subgenre of American fiction, represented by Raymond Carver and others, which Buford saw as being defined by “a curious, dirty realism about the belly-side of contemporary life.”</span></i></p>
<p><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Buford’s own curiosity took him to Britain’s belly-side during some of its darkest days. They weren’t to last forever—at least not in that form. In 1985, following a stadium disaster in Brussels, partly caused by Liverpool fans, English teams received an indefinite ban on playing in European competitions</span></i><i>. </i><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The most popular British broadsheet, the </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Sunday Times, </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">having</span></i> <i><span style="font-weight: 400;">recently</span></i> <i><span style="font-weight: 400;">lamented English soccer’s status as a “slum sport” played in “slum stadiums” and mostly attended by “slum people,” expressed the hope that in five years’ time it could “cease to be a pariah” and turn into “a family sport</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">.</span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">” Leaving aside the questionable choice of terms, this is more or less what happened. Buford’s account climaxes with a riot in Sardinia, during the 1990 World Cup in Italy—an event widely viewed as the turning point in the fortunes of English soccer. </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Among the Thugs</span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, published in 1991, after six years’ labor, bore ambivalent witness to an era that was finally fading.</span></i></p>
<p><!--more--></p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">INTERVIEWER</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The origin of </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Among the Thugs</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> is recounted in the opening pages. Your first encounter with the phenomenon of soccer “hooliganism” came when you were waiting on a station platform in Wales and you saw a train rammed with chanting fans on their way home from a match. When did you start to think that this visceral experience had the makings of a book?</span></p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">BILL BUFORD</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">It evolved. When I saw that train, I realized that even though football violence had been widely reported in a sensationalistic way, I had no idea of its intensity. Soon afterward, I went to a publishing party—it’s referenced in the book. I was talking to Salman Rushdie and Mario Vargas Llosa, and I described the scene. They asked if I’d been to a match. As it happened, the only match I’d been to was U.S.A.-Mexico in Mexico City, which the U.S.A. lost 8–0. So we all went to a Tottenham Hotspur game—this was at the end of the 1982–1983 season—and it just reinforced the delight and the power of the crowd in all its different manifestations.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I came away thinking that I’d commission somebody to write it for </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Granta</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">. My first idea completely belly flopped. Adam Mars-Jones had written a piece called “Structural Anthropology,” and it was so witty—I thought, Adam can write about football hooligans. In fact, he would probably have had a hard time being accepted. His education, his delightful Englishness, his sophisticated accent. It was a rough lads’ culture and he would have stood out in a way that would have made it difficult to get access. I had no baggage. I was an American. If anything, people would ask, “What’s this American doing here?” I was a natural observer. But that’s later. </span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I then happened to be having lunch with an editor at the </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Sunday Times </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">in London, Eric Jacobs. An old-school-type geezer, but very supportive of literary endeavors. This was early in 1984. I think I mentioned a Celtic–Rangers match coming up, and he said, “You should do it. You should write this.” </span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">He gave me some cash for expenses. So I went to the Rangers match. And not long after that I got on a train for the match between Juventus and Manchester United. And that was where I met Mick, who had that memorable line when I introduced myself and said I was a journalist. And he said—what was it? </span></p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">INTERVIEWER</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;"> “All Americans are wankers … and all journalists are cunts.”  </span></p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">BUFORD</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">That’s it. That was him verbatim. Poor sod. He’s dead now. </span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">It was only a piece, but in my inevitable way—I’m still afflicted by this today—I felt I needed to go deep, deep, deep to get what was good. I started going to all kinds of matches just to get to know the game, to get to know the crowds. It wasn’t inconvenient. Most of my research was done on Saturdays, so I wasn’t missed in the office.</span></p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">INTERVIEWER</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">How did</span> <span style="font-weight: 400;">you</span> <span style="font-weight: 400;">envisage</span> <span style="font-weight: 400;">what you were trying to achieve?</span><b> </b></p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">BUFORD</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">What I recognized on that first train on that Welsh station platform was that what was being reported in the tabloids was so at odds with what it really was. I think that’s largely because nobody went in there to look at it or thought of it as a story. </span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I began to learn how widespread the crowd violence was. It was almost like it was the thing that everybody wanted to do. I came to think of it as basically a style thing or a fashion thing, in the same way that at different times, especially in Britain, there have been epochs of lad culture, like the Mods and Rockers, riding Vespas or Lambrette. Now they were wearing Lacoste shirts and beating the shit out of each other. Why? Because it was what the lads then did. It was fun. </span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">This was the time of Margaret Thatcher—and the assumption was that they were the disenfranchised looking to vent unhappiness or their disgust with the state of things, and were obviously impoverished. In fact, nothing could be further from the truth. There certainly were people who were disenfranchised, and there were a few people who were not so stable in the head—no question. But the basic culture was a very confident lads’ culture where most of the people were making money and had money to spend. A lot of them were builders. There were some lawyers. There were painters. There were some drug dealers. There were quite a few crooks. There was my good friend Robert, who was a very accomplished pickpocket and with whom I am in touch today. In fact, I owe him a phone call.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">But in 1985, there was the Heysel disaster, and the</span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Sunday Times</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> wanted the piece. I thought, I’m not ready. I realized that I was really not an instant journalist. So I got an agent and the agent said, “I’ll deal with them. And then what you need to do is make a book proposal and write thirty pages and a description of what you’re going to do next.” </span></p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">INTERVIEWER</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">You had come to the United Kingdom—to Cambridge—to do some academic work. Did you have literary ambitions?</span></p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">BUFORD</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I got my scholarship as a Shakespearean and I did indulge that—to the extent that, in a year, I saw a hundred Shakespeare productions. I saw a hundred Shakespeare productions and went to two lectures.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">But what I wanted to be was a writer. I did a little bit of writing for the student magazines. A guy who became one of my best friends, Pete de Bolla, was running a student magazine—</span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Granta.</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> At that time, it was calling itself something else. Anyway, Pete wanted to do an issue on “the theory of the subject.”</span></p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">INTERVIEWER</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Referring to the ideas of people like Lacan and Althusser?</span></p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">BUFORD</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Yeah, that was the time. But I had this other idea of these American writers who I’d never read until I got to the UK. When I was studying in the States, I’d just been locked into Shakespeare. I’d learned Latin and worked on an edition of the Sonnets. So I was discovering American fiction in Britain. Some of my fellow Americans there were quite ambitious literary types and they were alluding to writers that I’d never read, like William Gass, William Gaddis, and Susan Sontag.</span></p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">INTERVIEWER</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">How important in the writing of the book is the first person—and not just the first-person observer, but the participant observer, the collusive observer? Part of the story is you becoming part of the world.</span></p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">BUFORD</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">A thug. Yeah. I had no idea about how this first person would develop. I regarded my role as an observer. But my role was to get as close as I possibly could to the experience of violence, because that was what the story was. I didn’t ever want to participate in the violence because, to an extent, I regarded that position as immoral. A proper journalistic position is, you don’t commit the crime. </span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">But I did want to get close, close, close. There’s a scene in the book where Sammy is doing a head count, because they’re about to surprise a train, and he counts me. I felt that was exactly right. I would be close enough that I’d be among the people.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I do like the first person as long as my personal experience contributes to understanding the story. I did a piece about sweatshops in New York, and I used the first person because, when I was in Berkeley, I worked as a cutter in a sweatshop. I was making ideologically acceptable outdoor jackets and sleeping bags. I had a long table—like a hundred and eighty feet or something—and I’d trace the patterns and then I’d run back to the end and cut, cut, cut, cut. For me, it was great. I got paid by the piece and I got exercise.</span></p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">INTERVIEWER</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">One shouldn’t extrapolate too much from what was in </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Granta</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, but if I think about some of the writing that appeared there during the period you started thinking about hooligans, there was Raymond Carver and Richard Ford in the “Dirty Realism” issue and the excerpt from Martin Amis’s </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Money</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">—work steeped in illuminating detail about male myopia or hotheadedness. Do you think that has been one of the central pillars of your taste and interest?</span></p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">BUFORD</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">If so, in an ancillary way. I think male behavior is perplexing and disruptive and often a good story.  </span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I remember the first time I read Ray’s stories and I thought the poetic simplicity of the prose was completely at odds with the stuff that I’d done in the earliest issues of editing </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Granta</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, which was postmodernist or post-postmodernist. I was proud to publish William Gass in the first issue we did, because there was a kind of intensity of reflection that I found very compelling. But most of the other stuff, I kind of hated. I hated the self-consciousness. I hated the literary artifact. I hated stories of looking for the author. And then Ray came along and it was so direct and clean and felt. I thought, Wow, this is like drinking clean water after you’ve had nothing but filth. This is like air.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I remember somehow I got a bank to sponsor inviting these American writers over—Ray, Toby Wolff, Richard Ford. And there were a lot of male themes in Richard Ford. The evening was mesmerizing. I wasn’t seeking it out. It was just more I was being an editor and found that there was something I was looking for in the writing that I wasn’t getting. </span></p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">INTERVIEWER</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">But you were also drawn to what you call literary narrative journalism. What do you mean by that?</span></p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">BUFORD</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Driven by narrative. It was shaped like a literary work, but not for any other arty reasons, just good nonfiction in the tradition of a Peter Matthiessen or some of the gonzo journalists.</span></p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">INTERVIEWER</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Did you find a tradition of serious nonfiction in Britain?</span></p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">BUFORD</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">In fact, even more serious. A lot of it was then called travel writing. But you had Colin Thubron, Ian Jack is another example, James Fenton is an example. Bruce Chatwin. All those people are doing a kind of narrative writing.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">In many ways, the guy who became my model was a Pole, Ryszard Kapuściński. I went to see him in Warsaw in ’89, before the wall came down, and when I walked in the streets with him, everybody knew him. If the hotel was booked up, he’d say, “But I’m Ryszard Kapuściński.” They’d kick someone out. I did a long interview with him in the magazine, which for me held many important principles, including the fundamental one that characterized his life—that he was the only foreign correspondent for the Polish Press Agency and so the only one on the block. </span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Poland was then under the communist regime. </span><span style="font-weight: 400;">It was before technology and everything else, and he was the only reporter who’d been to Africa, who’d been to Asia, who’d been to a revolution—like a sailor coming back with stories, pure discovery, full of events that other people had never heard—“Well, I saw these strange animals with black and yellow spots and long tongues and they ate food out of high trees.”</span></p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">INTERVIEWER </span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Did you feel that you were a lone voyager in the hooligan world?</span></p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">BUFORD</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Yes, sometimes, and it was a privilege. And a burden. I can cite an example. I was invited to a dinner at Mario Vargas Llosa’s apartment in London. He had all these established literary figures around his table. And I’d come straight from—I don’t know if I’d call it a riot—but a street fight after a Chelsea–Manchester United match. I showed up a little late and very sweaty because in the aftermath of the match, I fell in with the Man United brigade. And they had this strategy of zigzagging around blocks so they could surprise the Chelsea fans. But it was just street violence and I kept getting hit by beer cans. They’d just fly out of nowhere and hit me on the side of the head—fortunately, always the soft part of the beer can. Well, after midway through the dinner, I just said, “I had an interesting afternoon.” And it all came out. </span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">It was Germaine Greer who stopped me. Sorry, this is very name-heavy, but I was running a literary magazine and I was in this world. Germaine said I was talking about it too much. She said, “You’re just telling your story. Stop telling your story. Write your story. Every day, get up at six in the morning and I’ll send you breakfast.” She didn’t send breakfast, but I did start getting up by six in the morning. </span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I remember I was then writing about Juventus and the riots in Turin—a father’s being beaten up with his children, the drama of it and the sound of his being hit and then kicked by rubber-soled sneakers. It was all so vivid, and so violent, that I began having wild, upsetting dreams of these poor Torinesi getting hurt, just before I woke up and went to my keyboard. But that kind of launched the book.</span></p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">INTERVIEWER</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">In a weird way, from the perspective of the reading public, one of the things that you were going off to see and bring back in the form of a book was football. People did not write books about football in Britain in the 1980s, even negative ones really. </span></p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">BUFORD</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I would say I was writing about supporters, and that was met with vitriolic disdain by other journalists. But, having said that, I’m also a big believer deep down in the theater of sport, and I think it needs its Aristotles in the way that tragedy did. It’s fantastic drama. All sport has that. And it’s occurring in front of live audiences in all the continents of the world in many different variations. Being in New York when the Knicks won their championship. I mean, wow, wow, wow. It was so intense. It’s a great form of human expression, our most exciting theater. </span></p>
