<p>When I first <a href="https://toucharcade.com/2023/08/18/best-boomer-shooters-on-steam-deck-2023/">wrote about boomer shooters</a> last year on Steam Deck and also <a href="https://toucharcade.com/2023/09/28/best-boomer-shooters-on-switch-nintendo-eshop-2023/">on Switch</a>, aside from New Blood and Nightdive, the most common name was <a href="https://www.hulshult.com/">Andrew Hulshult</a> who has done some amazing music over the years. He recently was involved with the <a href="https://toucharcade.com/2024/08/13/doom-doom-ii-swith-vs-ps5-xbox-steam-deck-pc-mobile-iphone-android-compared-features-mods/">DOOM + DOOM II re-release</a> that included his IDKFA soundtrack with new music for DOOM II, and having wanted to interview him for a while now, I finally had a chance to chat with him on call for a few hours to discuss game soundtracks, composition, bands he likes, guitar strings, pickups, cold brew coffee, his first film soundtrack, games he&#8217;s playing, and a lot more. This interview was done on video call and then it was transcribed and edited for brevity. Just like my interview with <a href="https://toucharcade.com/2024/03/28/new-blood-interactive-2024-interview-dave-oshry-faith-switch-fallen-aces-steam-early-access-release-console-ports-health-vinyl-posters/">Dave Oshry from New Blood</a>, this one was more casual than usual, and this is likely the longest interview on TouchArcade so strap in and grab a cold brew. </p>
<p><img alt="" class="aligncenter size-full wp-image-329073" height="2160" src="//cdn.toucharcade.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/andrew-hulshult-interview-2024.jpg" width="3840" /></p>
<p><strong>TouchArcade (TA): </strong>So for those unaware, tell us a little bit about yourself and what you do.</p>
<p><strong>Andrew Hulshult (AH): </strong>Yeah, my name&#8217;s Andrew Hulshult. I&#8217;m a composer and sound designer for mainly video games, but I&#8217;m starting to move over into film as well. I like to just write music by myself sometimes when it&#8217;s not for a game or film. But that&#8217;s primarily what I do. I work in the game and film industry doing sound design, soundtracks, and sometimes voice acting.</p>
<p><strong>TA: </strong>How did you end up working on the canceled Duke Nukem project and also Rise of the Triad 2013?</p>
<p><strong>AH: </strong>Duke Nukem 3D Reloaded was actually just kind of like, I think that was 2010. That&#8217;s, I&#8217;m reaching back here a little bit. So I think Frederik at the time, Frederik Schreiber, the guy who runs 3D Realms now, or I think he still does, basically he was remaking maps in the middle of Unreal Engine 3 of like some of the original Duke 3D maps and posting them on Gearbox forums, which I think one of them that got a lot of hits was like all of the 3D renders of like Hollywood Holocaust, which is like E1M1 for Duke 3D. That caught my attention as well. I was like, wow, that looks really neat. You know, like I reached out on the forums, I was like, hey, do you need any music? Maybe this would be fun. Like, I really liked Duke 3D back in the day. And he said, yeah, sure. You know, like if you want to remake some of the stuff and hand it off to me.</p>
<p>So I just did some of that. I just had gear laying around and I wanted to kind of learn how to do this anyway. So I just dove in head first and started kind of remaking some of the old Duke 3D tracks. That kind of spawned into, you know, not a lot, not a lot was done in Duke 3D Reloaded. It was just like, you know, like some odds and ends stuff. Somehow that turned into Apogee, Terry Nagy, head hunting us and saying, hey, I have an IP that I&#8217;d really like you to work on called Rise of the Triad if you guys want to give it a try. And he had an investor with him at the time who was interested named Dave Oshry And this was a very long time ago. And it&#8217;s crazy. Like all those people now are like, you know, on the, we were all on the forefront of like the retro FPS revival stuff.</p>
<p>But basically after Duke 3D Reloaded, yeah, Apogee came, said, hey, we&#8217;re interested. And Fred said yes on our side. And we started making Rise of the Triad 2013.</p>

<p><strong>TA: </strong>I remember in a prior interview you did, you mentioned how when you were doing the 3D realm stuff, you weren&#8217;t aware of how much you were in demand in the industry. So when that door closed, suddenly you had like a, like thousands of opportunities and stuff like that. But, and obviously you&#8217;ve gone on to do some of like some huge titles since then. Obviously Doom Eternal DLC is the one which a lot of people think about you. For me, it&#8217;s like other titles like Nightmare Reaper and Dusk and stuff like that. I want to know how you&#8217;ve changed as a musician and as a professional from back then to now.</p>
<p><strong>AH: </strong>Oh, that&#8217;s a great question. Man, starting off in the industry with, you know, doing the whole stuff with Interceptor, like where we were, what we were just talking about, it was a completely fresh experience as a musician. So you don&#8217;t know what you&#8217;re getting into. And then a lot of times you&#8217;re like, I don&#8217;t even know what I should be getting paid, you know, like you have an idea, but you&#8217;re not, you don&#8217;t know. And so like wading through those waters is interesting and also dangerous territory. But the stuff that I have learned has been all from, you know, tripping on agreements as you go forward. You sign an agreement, you go for it, you make the money, and on the back end you&#8217;re either like, this didn&#8217;t really work out, or you&#8217;re like, hey, this worked out, we need to make sure that we do this again, you know.</p>
<p>It&#8217;s learning the entire time. Because this is the thing that I feel like musicians get hung up on a lot, which is, you know, they just, they want to make really, really awesome, beautiful art for games. And like, there&#8217;s absolutely nothing wrong with that. You should, that should be the core principle that you do. But you have to make sure that you get paid so that you can continue to do it. Because otherwise you burn yourself out and you don&#8217;t want to work in the industry anymore. And that, to go back to what you were talking about with, I didn&#8217;t know how much, like how in demand I was. I was right at that point. I was about to walk away from everything right after the whole stuff with 3D Realms. I was done with games. I was like, you know what, like there&#8217;s not a lot of avenues here and I&#8217;m just getting more bitter as time goes on. And the last handful of things I&#8217;ve worked on have just bombed so why am I putting all this effort into this?</p>

<p>I should try and do something else. And I didn&#8217;t realize until I stepped out from 3D Realms how many people wanted to hire me. And like, it&#8217;s one of those weird things where like, I&#8217;m sure you can probably relate. When you have a job, like a day job, if you&#8217;ve ever just held like a simple, simple day job, you can get sucked into that entire cycle of that job and nothing else matters around.</p>
<p>So an example of this would be, I worked for a music retailer for about 15 years, okay. I was doing well for them. They wanted to give me my own stores. I was working on management. And the cycle that comes with that where you get so wrapped up in the business of that makes you lose focus of lots of other things in life. And that&#8217;s kind of what happened to me while I was working with 3D Realms. That&#8217;s nothing bad about them. It&#8217;s just when you&#8217;re working for a company rather than working for yourself, it turns into that.</p>
<p>From the start to now has been like just a crazy learning process. You do have to walk on landmines. You do have to get blown up a couple of times until you figure out what works and what doesn&#8217;t. Stepping out from 3D Realms after they said, hey, we don&#8217;t have the cash to pay you. It&#8217;s like, oh, okay, I guess I&#8217;ll go figure this out now. And then all of a sudden, DUSK, you know, like that&#8217;s literally the next thing that and AMID EVIL were the very next things that I worked on. It was crazy.</p>

<p><strong>TA: </strong>Obviously you get a lot of questions about game music, but what is like, since you brought up this whole thing about how you&#8217;ve changed, what is the biggest misconception that people both in the industry and like the players have about video games music right now?</p>
<p><strong>AH: </strong>The biggest misconception. Oh, that anybody can do it and it&#8217;s a small part. <em>laughs</em> It&#8217;s like, you can&#8217;t just throw anything in there. Like, man, it&#8217;s I&#8217;d say that from a public like standpoint of I don&#8217;t play games that much and I&#8217;m casual kind of thing, some of my friends and some of my family are like, you have the easiest job. I&#8217;m like, you have no f***ing idea. You know, walk in my shoes for a day and let&#8217;s talk again. Yeah, it&#8217;s really difficult because you, you really have to trick yourself into getting into whatever atmosphere or whatever world someone else has painted already. Right. Like they&#8217;ve already built everything out. This is their vision. You have to step into it and you have to step into it with respect to their design philosophies. And you also have to have the confidence to say, well, this is what I think we should do and why we should do it. And there&#8217;s a lot of, there&#8217;s a lot of social confidence that&#8217;s needed to do a gig like this.</p>
<p>It&#8217;s complex in a lot of different ways. The art side of it is hard enough, like pulling shit out of thin air, is difficult already, but then, um, explaining to people why you want to go this direction and sometimes even arguing to get that direction can be a hassle in and of itself.</p>
<p>So yeah, I&#8217;d say the biggest misconception is that it&#8217;s easy and that it&#8217;s not easy. I swear to God, so many times my friends are like, ah, you&#8217;ve got the easiest job. I&#8217;m like, no, man, I live, I, I threw a hundred pound boxes from 6 AM to 4 PM. And sometimes I miss that. </p>

I&#8217;ve just sent you a tweet right now, which I wanted to reference. This is quite an old one (linked above). Well, I actually didn&#8217;t know much about this soundtrack until recently. I had heard about it and I had heard that a lot of my friends bought that release. They said that it wasn&#8217;t that great. I played the original on DOS but not the 2013 version, so I didn&#8217;t really hear it all in-game until the Ludicrous Edition and when folks made a big deal about it having Andrew Hulshult&#8217;s music. I decided I wanted to properly try it then. I wanted to talk about your thought process between like, you know, redoing those tracks and coming up with your own flare on them, because anyone who, at least any fan of yours, if they listen to that, they know it&#8217;s your music. It&#8217;s not like, oh, this is just like him doing a cover of someone else. It still feels like it&#8217;s you.</p>
<p><strong>AH: </strong>The first and foremost thing was to make sure you respect your elders. I wanted to make sure that whatever I did obviously served the fanbase and by proxy served everything that Lee Jackson had written. Lee Jackson and Bobby Prince, as far as I&#8217;m concerned, that&#8217;s the de facto composers for FPS. The originators. You&#8217;re already stepping into something well established back in the day. People are very familiar with this. If you f*** it up, you are toast. That was my first professional gig. Stepping into that kind of pressure. </p>
<p>Just really what it came to, it was all really natural. I heard these songs and I saw the game and what they&#8217;re making and I&#8217;m like it is kinda jank, but it is fun. It is ridiculous. ROTT is so ridiculous. I was like man, I was talking to Dave and Fred about it. What if we did like rock and metal stuff predominantly? That&#8217;s the kind of music I listen to all the time. They said let&#8217;s try it. Even Terry was like you should do it.</p>

The first one I did as a demo to work on it was &#8220;Goin Down the Fast Way" and I remember I handed that to Terry. Terry is awesome and he has been great to me for my entire career. Even if it is stepping into someone else&#8217;s shoes, I have to remember that the source material comes first, then you put your influences on. I never even thought about that that is where that started and also like I mean I gosh that&#8217;s if I think about it, that was 2013 is the end of 2013 is when development started on that, and it originally started as a Duke Nukem game before the lawsuit, and I had an eight string by that time. I was tuning down big time at that point, and there&#8217;s a lot of stuff that never made it maybe I&#8217;ll put that up on Twitter at some point but there&#8217;s a lot of stuff that never never showed up on it because it was a little too aggressive you know but that stuff would later show up in DOOM you know. <em>laughs</em></p>

<p><strong>TA: </strong>When just discovering your music many years ago, everything was really good metal, and this was probably around the time I was mainly listening to metal before I started broadening my horizons. I went through this phase when I was learning guitar and I started listening to more Dream Theater and then got a seven string guitar, and eventually got into Meshuggah. It got me thinking about how when I listen to your music now, you manage doing unique things for each game and make the songs fit the game properly. It isn&#8217;t just metal anymore so there&#8217;s no worry about being typecast. Did you have that fear that everyone is just going to expect metal from you when you&#8217;re behind a specific soundtrack?</p>
<p><strong>AH: </strong>Oh man. You just saying that just out loud makes me wow. I still worry about that sometimes. There are moments where I&#8217;m like am I getting typecast at this point, and you saying, yeah I don&#8217;t have to worry about that anymore, I&#8217;m like oh thank god I heard it from someone else where they were you know like, because I don&#8217;t want to be like I don&#8217;t want to be like straight up known as metal guy you know like I love metal I love playing it I&#8217;ll make those records all day long as people as long as people will listen to it and even if they won&#8217;t I&#8217;ll probably make them, but yeah I like to paint with different brushes like especially when I get older, like I really enjoy mixing sound design with guitars. </p>
<p>I like mixing sound design with orchestral instruments, I like just going full hand on my Eurorack synth stuff over here and just getting lost. As a musician I&#8217;m fortunate enough at this point where I can just experiment, and I know I can find, I have the confidence now after working on all these games and having some success with it where I know if I get lost I can find a way out because I&#8217;ll find something that I like, and chances are if I like it, I can probably show it to some people who will dig it as well. I&#8217;m so glad to hear that you&#8217;re like I know that I can get some variety. <em>laughs</em> </p>
<p><strong>TA: </strong>I mean if someone just heard DUSK and I&#8217;d say if they heard DUSK even that is pretty different. I mean if someone&#8217;s not into metal maybe they think dusk sounds the same as bombshell but you can totally tell that DUSK was trying to be like this middle ground of what you like and Nine Inch Nails&#8217; Quake. I think it has one of my favorite guitar tones in gaming right now, and it&#8217;s still pretty timeless. I want to move to AMID EVIL. I love the soundtrack to the main game, but I want to ask about the DLC for two reasons. Now the first thing is kind of like a personal topic because I believe you were going through a family emergency during the time when you were recording the soundtrack, right? When I was playing Dragon Quest VIII on the 3DS and my grandfather was in the hospital before he passed away, whenever I think about Dragon Quest VIII now it reminds me of that. So do you go through the same thing with the AMID EVIL DLC music?</p>
<p><strong>AH: </strong>I don&#8217;t think I&#8217;ve been able to sit down and digest it that way yet. But I know exactly what you&#8217;re talking about and I&#8217;m sure that is what I will absolutely go through. Yeah, to harp back to what you&#8217;re talking about, to what you&#8217;re referencing, my father had a heart attack. And it was just out of nowhere. And it was on, gosh, I think it was on New Year&#8217;s Eve. It was like 9 PM on New Year&#8217;s Eve. It was the year before it was released And I was halfway, I was like midway through working on that soundtrack. And it just scared the shit out of me. As anybody would. Your father&#8217;s dying And he ended up having like a, all four valves around his heart were like 90-95% clogged. So yeah, he had to have a quadruple bypass. And thankfully, We have a really, really good heart hospital right next to us. And they&#8217;re at an age now where they&#8217;re on Medicare. I think it&#8217;s Medicare. I can&#8217;t remember. It&#8217;s like once you&#8217;re past 64 at a certain age in this country, you can get on Medicare. And you know, like health stuff isn&#8217;t as much of a nightmare as it normally is. But they got great care, got taken care of. But it was months. </p>
<p>It took months for all that stuff to happen. And there&#8217;s like so many things that happened in between that. Like this was right in the middle of COVID. So like whenever he got, it was right in the middle of the big Omicron spike for the world. So as soon as he got admitted, he couldn&#8217;t get to an actual emergency room. He had to sit in another room for like two or three days and they actually had to sedate him for a few days before they could transfer him to another hospital to get looked at. Like it was just crazy. And I was out of my mind. I was crazy at that point. And so once they got to a point where they said we&#8217;re going to do surgery and he came out of surgery, I just needed something to occupy myself. Otherwise, I was going to self-destruct.</p>
<p>So I started writing a lot on the AMID EVIL stuff and started really finding that old like playing with a band and writing songs for you kind of vibe going in. And a lot of that stuff in a AMID EVIL, the stuff that&#8217;s like got a lot of energy behind it, that is pretty personal for me. There&#8217;s a lot of emotions on that soundtrack. There&#8217;s also something else that I haven&#8217;t talked about in public yet and I&#8217;m not going to talk about it here that I started working on that has a lot of that as well. And that&#8217;ll still be a while before that pops up. But I&#8217;d say that that was my main outlet for a while. You have something to look forward to. </p>
<p>But yeah, like that soundtrack let&#8217;s wrap it up. That soundtrack did have a lot of tension and a lot of emotions tied to my father almost passing away and me trying to wrap my head around that. You&#8217;re right. I hadn&#8217;t thought about that.</p>

<p><strong>TA: </strong>You&#8217;ve accomplished a lot in your career so far with recognizable music. <p><strong>AH: </strong>Every day is just like I don&#8217;t know. If you&#8217;re not teaching yourself something every day, this is my train of thought. So if I feel like the drum kit I&#8217;ve used like two or three times on two to three different records, if I&#8217;m like that feels stale. <p>It&#8217;s kind of like limiting yourself, like putting yourself in a box that&#8217;s really important as an artist to make sure that you are working within a certain scope of things and going hey, you know, here&#8217;s something that&#8217;s completely unfamiliar, get used to it and this is what you have to work with. <p><strong>AH: </strong>A day right now looks like about, a typical day is about anywhere between 6 to 7 hours of sleep which is about what I need I&#8217;ve found out, like it&#8217;s like somewhere around there, sometimes 8 and then randomly I&#8217;ll have days where it&#8217;s like, oh you need like 11 hours I don&#8217;t know why, but I feel fine waking up completely recharged with like 6 and a half to 7 and a half hours of sleep. So what I&#8217;ll do every morning is I&#8217;ll wake up, I&#8217;ll go take a shower immediately, like that&#8217;s the only way I can start my day is like I feel like I&#8217;ve got to it feels like I&#8217;m washing off yesterday I don&#8217;t know how else to explain that and then I gotta have a coffee and then I gotta start writing things on a whiteboard that&#8217;s right over beside me to the left because otherwise I&#8217;m just gonna spin my wheels all day long and think, oh I should do this, I should do that, I&#8217;ll be thinking about everything I need to do and then never actually do anything.
