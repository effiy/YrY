# The Second Curve

> **As an** executive, **I want to** second curve, **so that** strategy aligned.

> Any business has a lifecycle; the second curve must be started before the first peaks, or you will be left behind by the times.

## Summary

- Proposed by Charles Handy (2015); businesses follow an S-curve: start → accelerate → peak → decline
- Key timing: start the second curve while the first is still rising but before it peaks — at this point there are resources, time, and error-tolerance budget
- Classic cases: Netflix (DVD → streaming → original content → games), Apple (Mac → iPod → iPhone → Watch)
- Counter-example: Kodak actually invented the digital camera but was held hostage by the film business; went bankrupt in 2012

## Core viewpoints

- **The second curve must start before the first curve peaks, and the window of opportunity is narrower than it appears.** The optimal moment to start the second curve is while the first curve is still rising — when revenues are growing, margins are healthy, and the organization has the resources, time, and error-tolerance budget to experiment. Once the peak passes, declining revenues trigger cost-cutting, talent attrition, and risk aversion — the exact opposite of the conditions needed to incubate a new business. The timing signal is not "when is the first curve declining?" but "when is the first curve's growth rate decelerating?"

- **Run dual tracks; do not switch — the second curve is incubated alongside the first, not after it.** The 70/30 resource allocation (70% first curve, 30% second curve) is a structural commitment, not a temporary experiment. The second curve team must be protected from the first curve's operational pressures: separate P&L, separate leadership, separate incentives. When the first curve hits a rough quarter, the natural organizational response is to pull resources from the second curve — and the structural separation exists to prevent that.

- **Self-disruption is harder than external disruption because the existing organization is the obstacle.** The first curve's success creates cultural inertia, stakeholder expectations, and incentive structures that all favor optimizing the existing business over cannibalizing it. The second curve requires top-down leadership commitment — the CEO must personally protect the second curve team from the first curve's immune response. Long-term incentives (equity vesting over 5+ years, second-curve-specific bonuses) must align the second curve team's rewards with the second curve's success, not the first curve's quarterly earnings.

- **The second curve is not a diversification strategy — it is a survival strategy for industries undergoing structural change.** Kodak invented the digital camera but was held hostage by the film business's margins. The lesson is not "Kodak should have diversified" — it is "Kodak's film business was going to zero regardless, and the second curve was the only path to survival." The second curve framework applies specifically when the first curve's decline is structural and inevitable, not cyclical and temporary.

- **The chasm between the first and second curve is where most transformations fail — the transition requires deliberate management of the "valley of death."** During the chasm period, the first curve is declining and the second curve is not yet profitable. The organization is burning cash from both sides. The chasm management plan must specify: how much cash reserve is allocated to the transition, what triggers the full shift from first to second curve, and what the organization does if the second curve fails to achieve escape velocity within the allocated window.

### Key cases

**Netflix**: DVD mail → streaming → original content → games / adjacent businesses. Each next curve started before the previous curve peaked, sacrificing short-term profit for long-term survival.

**Apple**: Mac → iPod / iTunes → iPhone → Watch / Services.

**Kodak (counter-example)**: First curve was film; should have started digital photography. Actually invented the digital camera, but was held hostage by the film business; only realized after the peak passed, went bankrupt in 2012.

## Action recommendations

1. **Diagnose first-curve position**: where on the S-curve? Is it near the peak?
2. **Find second-curve candidates**: 3-5 directions, with 1-2 as primary
3. **Resource dual-track**: keep 70% on the first curve, 30% on the second
4. **Experiment with second curve**: small-scale, low-commitment, fast validation
5. **Transition**: once the second curve is validated, gradually increase resources; do not resist when the first curve declines
6. **New first curve**: the second curve becomes the new core business
7. **Retrospective cadence**: track the first-curve decline and second-curve growth rhythm



- Starting the second curve only at peak → no resources left; must start before the peak
- Second curve conflicts with the first → opposing internal interests; culturally accept self-disruption
- Investing all resources in the second → first curve collapses too fast; balance at 70/30
- Going all-in without experiments → high risk; validate small-scale then scale
- Too many second curves → opening 5 at once; keep 1-2 as primary
- Reluctant to abandon the first → cultural / stakeholder capture; top-down push + long-term incentives

## Anti-patterns

- **Using the 70/30 resource split as a rigid formula rather than a dynamic allocation that responds to the first curve's acceleration or deceleration.** A first curve that is still accelerating at 40% year-over-year growth can afford to invest less in the second curve (80/20) because the first curve is generating excess resources. A first curve that is decelerating at 5% growth needs to invest more aggressively in the second curve (60/40) because the window is closing. The split must be a function of the first curve's growth rate, not a fixed number.
- **Selecting second-curve candidates based on their adjacency to the first curve rather than their potential to cannibalize it.** A second curve that is adjacent to the first curve (e.g., a premium version of the existing product) is comfortable because it leverages existing capabilities. It is also the easiest for competitors to copy and the least likely to create a new market. The second curve should be evaluated on its potential to make the first curve obsolete, not on its comfort level for the existing organization.
- **Assigning the second-curve initiative to the same leadership team that runs the first curve, without a separate P&L and decision-making authority.** The first-curve leadership team is incentivized to protect the first curve's revenue, market share, and headcount. When the second curve threatens to cannibalize the first, the leaders face a conflict of interest. The second-curve team must have a separate P&L, a separate leader who reports to the CEO (not to the first-curve leader), and the authority to compete with the first curve for resources.
- **Measuring second-curve progress by the same metrics used for the first curve.** The first curve is measured by revenue, market share, and profit margin. The second curve in its early stages should be measured by learning velocity, customer discovery interviews completed, and hypotheses validated per month. Applying first-curve metrics to the second curve kills it: the second curve will always look like a bad investment compared to the first curve's mature economics.
- **Waiting for the first curve to show undeniable signs of decline (revenue drop, customer churn) before starting the second curve.** By the time revenue drops, the company is already in a defensive posture -- cutting costs, reducing headcount, and protecting the core business. There is no appetite for risky investment. The second curve must be started when the first curve is still growing, when the organization has the confidence and resources to invest in an uncertain future. The signal to start is not the first curve's decline but the first curve's deceleration of growth rate.
