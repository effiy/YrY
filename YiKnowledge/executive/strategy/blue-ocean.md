# Blue Ocean Strategy

> **As an** executive, **I want to** blue ocean, **so that** strategy aligned.

> Rather than fighting in the red ocean, redefine market boundaries and create new demand — create rather than compete.

## Summary

- Proposed by W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne (2005)
- Red ocean = existing markets with fierce competition and price wars; blue ocean = uncontested new markets
- Core tools: the ERRC four-action framework + the strategy canvas
- Six paths redefine market boundaries: cross-industry / cross-strategic-group / cross-buyer-chain / cross-complement / cross-functional-emotional / cross-time

## Core viewpoints

- **Value innovation — the simultaneous pursuit of differentiation and low cost — is the defining mechanism of blue ocean strategy, not a trade-off to be managed.** Traditional strategy assumes that differentiation costs more and low cost means commoditization. Blue ocean breaks this trade-off by eliminating and reducing factors the industry competes on while raising and creating factors the industry has never offered. The ERRC framework is not a menu of options — all four actions must be taken together, or the strategy collapses into either high-cost differentiation or low-value commoditization.

- **Blue ocean is not differentiation, and confusing the two is the most common strategic error.** Differentiation stays within the existing market boundaries and competes on the same factors as incumbents, just with a different mix. Blue ocean redefines the market boundaries entirely — it creates demand that did not previously exist. A differentiated product in a red ocean still competes for the same customers; a blue ocean product creates new customers who were non-consumers of the industry.

- **The strategy canvas is a diagnostic tool, not a presentation slide — its value is in revealing the convergence of competitive factors.** When every competitor's strategy canvas shows the same profile, the industry is trapped in a red ocean of imitation. The strategy canvas makes the convergence visible: all competitors invest in the same factors, compete on the same dimensions, and ignore the same non-customers. The first step of blue ocean strategy is to draw the current strategy canvas and ask: "which factors has the industry taken for granted that add no value to customers?"

- **The six paths framework is the engine of market-boundary reconstruction, not a brainstorming exercise.** Each path — cross-industry, cross-strategic-group, cross-buyer-chain, cross-complementary-product, cross-functional-emotional-orientation, cross-time — is a systematic lens for identifying uncontested market space. The paths are not intuitive; they require deliberate research into adjacent industries, alternative strategic groups, and the chain of buyers who influence purchasing decisions. A blue ocean identified through one path is more defensible than one identified through intuition alone.

- **Execution of blue ocean strategy requires overcoming four organizational hurdles: cognitive, resource, motivational, and political.** The cognitive hurdle is the belief that the current red ocean is the only viable market. The resource hurdle is the assumption that transformation requires more resources. The motivational hurdle is the inertia of the existing organization. The political hurdle is the resistance of stakeholders who benefit from the status quo. Tipping-point leadership — focusing on factors of disproportionate influence — is the mechanism for overcoming all four hurdles simultaneously.

## Key information

### Concept breakdown: ERRC four-action framework

| Action | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Eliminate | factors the industry takes for granted but actually add no value |
| Reduce | factors well above the industry standard, lowered |
| Raise | factors well below the industry standard, lifted |
| Create | factors the industry has never offered |

### Concept breakdown: Strategy Canvas

- X axis: industry competitive factors
- Y axis: high/low per factor
- Draw your own curve and competitors' curves, compare the shape differences
- A blue-ocean product curve usually differs markedly in shape from a red-ocean one

### Concept breakdown: six paths to redefine the market

1. **Cross-industry**: look at substitute industries (e.g. cinema = restaurant = theater)
2. **Cross-strategic-group**: find a position between high-price and low-price
3. **Cross-buyer-chain**: from user to decision-maker to influencer
4. **Cross-complement**: merge complementary offerings
5. **Cross-functional / emotional**: pivot from emotional to functional or vice versa
6. **Cross-time**: capture early signals of trends

### Key cases

**Cirque du Soleil**: eliminated animal acts and star performers → reduced costs → raised artistry, music, story → created dramatic adult themes. Moved from traditional circus (red ocean) to the new "circus + theater" market (blue ocean).

**Yellow Tail wine**: eliminated vintage and complex terminology → reduced flavor intensity and wine variety → raised drinkability and fun → created retail experience and simple labels. Moved from fine wine (red ocean) to the "easy-drinking beverage" market (blue ocean).

### Comparison with other frameworks

| Framework | Lens | Relationship with blue ocean |
|---|---|---|
| Porter's Five Forces | red-ocean competition | blue ocean is the anti-Five-Forces |
| Business Model Canvas | business-model description | a blue-ocean strategy can be filled into a BMC |
| JTBD | what the user wants to get done | blue ocean asks the Job first, not constrained by existing solutions |
| Second Curve | leapfrog innovation | blue ocean is the concrete method for the second curve |

### Applicable scenarios

- Existing markets are red-ocean-ized, profit margins squeezed
- You have the ability to redefine the market
- Startup phase looking for direction

## Action recommendations

1. **Draw the current strategy canvas**: your own + competitors' curves
2. **Run ERRC four steps**: for each factor ask whether to eliminate / reduce / raise / create
3. **Draw the blue-ocean strategy canvas**: target the new curve
4. **Validate**: user interviews, prototype testing (skipping validation equals "an ocean of no one")
5. **Execute**: transition from red ocean to blue ocean; gradual switch, not all at once
6. **Update the strategy canvas yearly**: the curve evolves with the market

## Anti-patterns

- **Treating any form of differentiation as a blue ocean strategy.** Adding a new feature, improving the user interface, or offering a premium tier is differentiation within the existing market, not blue ocean creation. True blue ocean strategy requires redefining the market boundary itself -- changing which factors the industry competes on, not just improving performance on existing factors. A product that is 20% better on the same competitive dimensions is still in the red ocean.
- **Running only the Raise and Create steps of the ERRC framework while skipping Eliminate and Reduce.** The temptation to add value without cutting cost is strong because cutting feels like sacrifice. But a blue ocean strategy that only adds features without eliminating cost-driving factors produces a product that is both more expensive to build and harder to sell. The Eliminate and Reduce steps are what make the economics work -- without them, the strategy is just feature creep dressed up as innovation.
- **Skipping demand validation and assuming the blue ocean exists because the strategy canvas looks different.** A strategy canvas with a divergent curve proves the idea is different, not that anyone wants it. The most dangerous outcome of a blue ocean exercise is a beautifully differentiated product that solves a problem nobody has. The new value curve must be tested with real customers through interviews, prototypes, and willingness-to-pay experiments before committing resources.
- **Abandoning the red ocean business entirely before the blue ocean is validated.** The first curve still generates the revenue that funds the second. Jumping fully into the blue ocean without a transition plan starves the new venture of resources and destroys the existing business. The correct pattern is dual-track: maintain the red ocean business while building the blue ocean venture, then transition gradually as the new business proves itself.
- **Applying blue ocean strategy to every product decision instead of reserving it for strategic inflection points.** Not every product update needs to redefine the market. Most product work is incremental improvement within an existing market, and that is perfectly valid. Reserving blue ocean thinking for the moments when the current market is genuinely exhausted -- profit margins are compressing, competitors are converging, and customer growth is stalling -- prevents the framework from becoming a distraction from the core business.
