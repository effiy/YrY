# Annual Strategic Planning

> **As an** executive, **I want to** run a structured annual strategic planning process, **so that** the company's vision translates into a prioritized, resourced, and accountable plan for the year ahead.

> Annual planning is the most important recurring strategic activity. Done well, it aligns the organization, allocates resources to the highest-impact opportunities, and creates accountability. Done poorly, it produces a wish list of unprioritized projects that nobody owns and nobody reviews.

## Summary

- Annual planning is the bridge between strategy (direction) and execution (quarterly OKRs). Without it, strategy is aspirational and execution is reactive.
- The process should start 8-10 weeks before the fiscal year begins and involve four phases: strategic review → portfolio prioritization → resource allocation → commitment and cascade
- Key output: A prioritized portfolio of initiatives with owners, success metrics, resource requirements, and quarterly milestones
- Critical success factor: The planning process must force trade-offs. If everything is a priority, nothing is.
- Common failure mode: Planning is treated as a finance exercise (budget allocation) rather than a strategy exercise (opportunity selection)

## Core viewpoints

### 1. Planning is about making choices, not making lists

The primary value of annual planning is forcing explicit trade-offs. Every initiative that is prioritized means another initiative is deprioritized or killed. If the planning process doesn't produce a "won't do" list as long as the "will do" list, it hasn't been rigorous enough.

### 2. Start with strategy, not with last year's plan

The most common planning anti-pattern is "last year's plan + 10%." This perpetuates existing resource allocation regardless of strategic shifts. Start each planning cycle with a strategic review: what changed in the market, what did we learn, what strategic assumptions were validated or invalidated.

### 3. Resource allocation is the moment of truth

The planning process is abstract until resources are allocated. Until headcount, budget, and leadership attention are assigned to specific initiatives, the plan is a wish list. Resource allocation should be zero-based: start from scratch each year rather than adjusting last year's allocation.

### 4. The planning calendar must be realistic

A rushed planning process produces shallow analysis and political decisions. An overly long process produces planning fatigue and missed market opportunities. 8-10 weeks is the sweet spot: enough time for analysis and debate, not so long that the market moves past you.

### 5. Planning outputs must cascade to team-level OKRs

The annual plan is useless if teams don't know what it means for them. Within 2-4 weeks of plan finalization, every team should have quarterly OKRs that trace directly to plan initiatives. This cascade is the primary mechanism for organizational alignment.

## Key info

### Four-phase planning process

| Phase | Duration | Key Activities | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Strategic Review** | Weeks 1-2 | Market analysis, competitor review, strategy refresh, SWOT update | Strategic context document |
| **2. Portfolio Prioritization** | Weeks 3-5 | Initiative proposals, business case review, prioritization workshop | Ranked initiative portfolio |
| **3. Resource Allocation** | Weeks 6-7 | Headcount planning, budget allocation, capacity check | Resourced plan with owners |
| **4. Commitment & Cascade** | Weeks 8-10 | Board/leadership approval, team OKR cascade, communication | Approved plan + team OKRs |

### Prioritization criteria (weighted scoring)

| Criterion | Weight | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Strategic alignment | 30% | How directly does this initiative advance our strategic priorities? |
| Revenue/Impact potential | 25% | Expected revenue, cost savings, or strategic optionality created |
| Execution feasibility | 20% | Do we have the talent, technology, and organizational capacity? |
| Urgency | 15% | Is there a time-bound opportunity or threat? |
| Learning value | 10% | Does this initiative teach us something we need to know? |

### Planning calendar template

| Week | Activity | Participants |
|---|---|---|
| W1-W2 | Market & competitive review | Strategy team, product leaders |
| W3 | Strategic assumptions workshop | Executive team |
| W4-W5 | Initiative proposals submitted | All department heads |
| W6 | Portfolio prioritization workshop | Executive team |
| W7 | Resource allocation & trade-off decisions | Executive team + Finance |
| W8 | Plan draft review | Executive team |
| W9 | Board review & approval | Board, CEO |
| W10 | Team OKR cascade | All managers |

## Action recommendations

1. **Start 8-10 weeks before fiscal year**: Block the executive team's calendar for the four key workshops. Treat planning as the most important work during this period.
2. **Use weighted scoring for prioritization**: Don't rely on advocacy and politics. Score every initiative against the same criteria, debate the scores, and let the ranking drive decisions.
3. **Produce a "won't do" list**: Explicitly list the initiatives that were considered but not prioritized. This is the most honest signal of strategic discipline.
4. **Allocate 20% capacity for the unexpected**: Don't plan to 100% capacity. Leave buffer for emergent opportunities and inevitable surprises.
5. **Cascade within 2 weeks of plan approval**: Every team leader must translate plan initiatives into team OKRs. Review the cascade for alignment.

## Anti-patterns

- **Last year + 10%**: Starting from last year's allocation rather than zero-based. This institutionalizes past decisions regardless of current relevance.
- **No trade-offs**: Everything is a priority. The plan is a list of all ideas, not a prioritised set of choices.
- **Finance-driven planning**: Planning is treated as a budget exercise rather than a strategy exercise. Finance allocates; strategy decides.
- **No cascade**: The plan is approved but never translated into team-level OKRs. Execution continues as before.
- **Planning as a one-time event**: The plan is filed away after approval and never reviewed. Quarterly business reviews should track plan progress.
- **Over-planning**: Trying to plan every detail of the year. The plan should specify what and why, not exactly how. Leave room for tactical adaptation.
