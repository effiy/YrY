# Why I'm Writing Rachel's Ramblings: Thinking Out Loud in Public

> **As a** technical leader, **I want to** overcome perfectionism and publish ideas early, **so that** patterns and hypotheses I observe across the industry can be shared and refined before they are fully formed.

## Summary

- Rachel Laycock, Global CTO of Thoughtworks, identifies three reasons she has not been writing: overthinking, moving too fast to the next idea, and convincing herself writing needs to be more polished than it does.
- Her brain works like a knowledge graph -- constant associations and pattern matching -- which is valuable in conversation but useless if never written down.
- "Rachel's Ramblings" is an experiment in fast, imperfect, thinking-out-loud writing, naming ideas early rather than waiting for them to be fully formed.
- The series will cover the future of software, how AI changes development for engineers and leaders, and how platforms, agents, and people actually work together.

## Core viewpoints

### 1. The bottleneck is not having ideas, it is the perfectionism that prevents publishing them
Laycock has ideas -- actual patterns, hypotheses, things she sees across clients and teams. The problem is not a lack of material but the self-imposed requirement that ideas be fully polished before publication. This is a common trap for senior technical leaders who are used to producing finished work.

### 2. Writing is a forcing function for thinking
The act of writing turns internal pattern-matching into something that exists outside the head, where it can be examined, challenged, and refined. Laycock recognizes that her knowledge-graph thinking style is valuable in conversations and strategy but leaves no trace -- writing is the mechanism to make it durable and shareable.

### 3. Fast, imperfect writing is a valid alternative to polished publications
The experiment is explicitly "fast, imperfect, thinking out loud." This is a deliberate stance against the academic and corporate writing norms that demand polished, fully-formed arguments. The bet is that the speed of sharing and the value of early feedback outweigh the cost of imperfection.

### 4. Naming ideas early creates a shared vocabulary before consensus forms
Laycock's strategy of naming ideas before they are fully validated serves a second purpose: it gives the community a handle to discuss an emerging pattern. Once a concept has a name, people can reference it, argue about it, and refine it. Without a name, the pattern remains trapped in individual conversations. The act of naming is itself a contribution to the field, even if the fully-formed analysis comes later.

### 5. The "ramblings" format is a leadership act, not a personal indulgence
Publishing fast, imperfect thoughts is not just about the author's creative process -- it is a deliberate signal to the organization that exploration and intellectual risk-taking are valued. When a CTO models the behavior of sharing half-formed ideas, it gives permission to everyone else to do the same. The format is the message: speed of learning matters more than polish of presentation.

## Key info

- The series will cover: the future of software, how AI changes software development for engineers/leaders/organizations, how platforms and agents and people work together, and how to manage the reality of a demanding CTO role.
- The writing is explicitly positioned as evolving -- "some of it will be wrong, some of it will evolve."
- The catalyst was the Future of Software conference, where Laycock realized she had ideas worth sharing but was not writing them down.

## Action recommendations

1. If you are a senior technical leader who has not been writing, identify which of the three barriers (overthinking, speed, perfectionism) is blocking you and address it directly.
2. Start a "ramblings" practice: publish fast, imperfect thoughts on patterns you observe. Name ideas early. Accept that some will be wrong.
3. Use public writing as a forcing function for your own thinking, not just as a communication tool for others.

## Anti-patterns

- **Waiting until ideas are fully formed before sharing them.** The feedback from early sharing is what helps ideas form.

- **Treating every publication as a finished product.** The "ramblings" format explicitly embraces evolution and imperfection.

- **Assuming that being too busy is the real reason for not writing.** The deeper reasons are usually psychological -- overthinking, perfectionism, or the habit of moving to the next idea too quickly.

- **Confusing the "ramblings" format with a license to publish without rigor.** Fast and imperfect does not mean careless. The ideas should be genuine observations, not speculation dressed as insight. The format lowers the bar for polish, not for honesty.

- **Writing without a clear audience in mind.** The "ramblings" format works because Laycock has a clear audience: other technical leaders who are wrestling with the same questions. Writing into the void produces ramblings that are genuinely useless. The format requires a reader who shares the context and cares about the questions.
