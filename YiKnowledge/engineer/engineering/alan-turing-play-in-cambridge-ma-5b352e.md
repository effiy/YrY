## Core viewpoints

### 1. Alan Turing's legacy spans both computing and cryptography

Turing's contributions to theoretical computer science (the Turing machine, the concept of computability) and his practical code-breaking work at Bletchley Park are both foundational to the modern world. The play covers both aspects of his life.

### 2. Cultural engagement with computing history matters

Theatre and other cultural forms that engage with the history of computing help connect technical work to its human context. Understanding the people behind the ideas enriches the practice of engineering.

### 3. Historical figures in computing are underutilized as teaching tools

Turing's story -- his theoretical breakthroughs, his wartime code-breaking, and his persecution -- teaches more about the nature of engineering than most textbooks. His work demonstrates that the most impactful contributions often come from crossing disciplinary boundaries. Using historical narratives in onboarding and training creates emotional resonance that pure technical instruction lacks.

### 4. The personal cost of institutional failure is part of computing history

Turing's prosecution for homosexuality and his subsequent death are not footnotes to his technical achievements -- they are central to understanding how institutions can fail the people who contribute most to them. The play's inclusion of this dimension is not merely biographical; it is a reminder that the field's history includes both triumphs and tragedies that shape who enters and stays in the field.

### 5. Cultural artifacts like plays preserve computing history differently than documentation

A play about Turing reaches audiences who would never read a computer science textbook. It humanizes the field and makes it accessible to non-engineers. For an industry that struggles with public understanding and trust, cultural engagement through theatre, film, and literature is a form of outreach that technical documentation cannot achieve.

## Anti-patterns

- **Treating computing history as irrelevant to modern practice.** Dismissing the history of computing as "just history" ignores the patterns that repeat across decades. Turing's work on computability directly informs modern understanding of what algorithms can and cannot do. History is not nostalgia -- it is pattern recognition.

- **Separating the person from the work when teaching computing history.** Teaching Turing's technical contributions without acknowledging his personal story erases the human cost of institutional failure. Engineers who only learn about the Turing machine but not about Turing's prosecution risk internalizing the idea that institutional injustice is irrelevant to the field.

- **Assuming that cultural engagement with computing is only for outreach.** Theatre and film about computing history are not just for recruiting non-engineers. Experienced practitioners benefit from seeing their field reflected in cultural forms. It provides perspective that day-to-day engineering work cannot -- a reminder of the larger context in which the field operates.

- **Overlooking local cultural events as professional development.** A play about Turing in Cambridge, MA is a professional development opportunity, not just entertainment. Engineers who only consume technical content miss the broader context that shapes the field. Local cultural events related to computing history are low-cost, high-impact ways to stay connected to the field's roots.

- **Reducing computing history to a list of "firsts" and dates.** The standard narrative of computing history -- first computer, first program, first network -- is a timeline, not an understanding. Turing's story is compelling because it shows the interplay between theory, practice, war, and personal tragedy. Historical engagement should focus on the human and intellectual dynamics, not just the chronology.
