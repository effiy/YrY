# Effective One-on-One Meetings

> **As a** manager or team lead, **I want to** run effective one-on-one meetings that are not status updates, **so that** I build trust with my reports, coach their growth, and create accountability for their development.

> The one-on-one is the most important meeting on a manager's calendar. It is the only meeting dedicated entirely to one person's growth, challenges, and career. When done well, it is the highest-leverage hour of the week. When done poorly, it is a dreaded status update that both parties resent.

## Summary

- The one-on-one is the report's meeting, not the manager's. The agenda should be driven by the report. The manager's role is to listen, ask questions, and coach -- not to receive a status update.
- The core purpose of a one-on-one is trust-building, coaching, and alignment. Status updates, project tracking, and tactical problem-solving belong in other meetings (standups, sprint planning, async updates).
- Effective one-on-ones follow a rhythm: weekly for new reports (first 3 months), bi-weekly for experienced reports, monthly for senior leaders. The cadence is non-negotiable; cancellations communicate that the report is not a priority.
- The manager's toolkit for one-on-ones: open-ended questions, active listening, and the discipline to not solve the report's problems for them. Coaching is about asking questions that lead the report to their own solutions.
- Skip-level one-on-ones (manager of manager meeting with skip-level reports) have a different purpose: gathering unfiltered organizational feedback, identifying systemic issues, and coaching senior leaders on their management.

## Core viewpoints

### 1. The one-on-one is the report's meeting, not the manager's

The most common failure mode is the manager using the one-on-one to extract a status update. This turns the meeting into a chore for the report and a missed opportunity for the manager. The report should own the agenda. A simple opening question ("What would be most useful to discuss today?") sets the right frame. If the report has nothing to discuss, the manager should have backup questions ready about growth, challenges, and career.

### 2. Trust is the output, not the input

Managers often think they need trust before having honest conversations. The reverse is true: honest conversations build trust. The manager must go first: share a mistake, admit uncertainty, ask for feedback on their own management. Vulnerability from the manager creates the safety for the report to be vulnerable in return. Trust is built one conversation at a time, over months, not in a single meeting.

### 3. Coaching is asking, not telling

When a report brings a problem, the manager's instinct is to solve it. "Here's what you should do." This is directing, not coaching. Coaching is: "What have you tried? What do you think the options are? What's the worst that could happen? What would you do if you weren't afraid?" The goal of coaching is to build the report's problem-solving capability, not to solve the problem for them. The manager should only switch to directing when the report is genuinely stuck or the stakes are too high for learning-by-doing.

### 4. Skip-level one-on-ones serve a different purpose

Skip-level meetings (manager-of-manager meeting with individual contributors) are not the same as direct-report one-on-ones. Their purpose is: (1) gathering unfiltered data about organizational health, (2) identifying whether the middle manager is effective, and (3) providing career perspective from a more senior leader. Skip-levels should happen quarterly, not weekly. The rules: never override the middle manager's decisions, never share confidential information about the middle manager, and always close the loop by sharing themes (not individuals) back to the middle manager.

## Key info

### Manager's one-on-one template

**Frequency**: Weekly (30-45 minutes) for new reports, bi-weekly for experienced reports.

**Structure** (30 minutes):

| Time | Section | Purpose | Example questions |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0-5 min | Check-in | Human connection, set the tone | "How are you doing, really?" "What's on your mind outside of work?" |
| 5-15 min | Report's agenda | The report's topics: challenges, ideas, decisions, concerns | "What's the most important thing to discuss today?" "What's been keeping you up at night?" |
| 15-20 min | Coaching | Deeper exploration of one topic | "What have you tried so far?" "What would success look like?" "What's the hardest part?" |
| 20-25 min | Manager's topics | Feedback, context, alignment | "Here's something I noticed this week..." "Here's some context you might not have..." |
| 25-30 min | Career & growth | Long-term development | "What skill do you want to build this quarter?" "Where do you want to be in 2 years?" |
| 30 min | Action items | Commitments and follow-up | "What will you do before our next meeting?" "What should I follow up on?" |

**The manager's question bank**:

**Opening questions:**
- "What would be most useful to discuss today?"
- "What's been on your mind that we haven't talked about?"
- "What's the biggest challenge you're facing right now?"

**Coaching questions:**
- "What have you already tried?"
- "What do you think the options are?"
- "What's holding you back?"
- "If you weren't afraid, what would you do?"
- "What would success look like for this?"

**Feedback questions:**
- "What's something I should start doing?"
- "What's something I should stop doing?"
- "What's something I should continue doing?"
- "Is there anything you need from me that you're not getting?"

**Career questions:**
- "What skill are you most excited to develop?"
- "What kind of work gives you the most energy?"
- "What kind of work drains you?"
- "Where do you want to be in 2 years? What's the gap between here and there?"

### Skip-level one-on-one guide

**Frequency**: Quarterly, 30 minutes per skip-level report.

**Purpose**: Gather unfiltered organizational feedback, not manage the IC directly.

**Structure**:

| Time | Section | Purpose | Example questions |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0-5 min | Introduction | Set context and expectations | "This is a chance for me to hear directly from you. Nothing you share will be attributed to you individually." |
| 5-15 min | Team health | How is the team functioning? | "What's going well on the team?" "What's not going well?" "How effective are team meetings?" |
| 15-20 min | Manager effectiveness | How is the middle manager doing? | "How would you describe your manager's style?" "What could your manager do better?" |
| 20-25 min | Career and growth | Long-term perspective | "What's your career goal?" "Are you getting the growth opportunities you need?" |
| 25-30 min | Organizational feedback | Systemic issues | "If you could change one thing about how we work, what would it be?" |

**Rules for skip-level one-on-ones:**
1. Never override the middle manager's decisions. The skip-level is for listening, not directing.
2. Never share confidential information about the middle manager. Do not say "your manager is being considered for a promotion" or "we're concerned about your manager's performance."
3. Close the loop with the middle manager. Share themes, not individuals. "I heard that the team is frustrated with the sprint planning process" not "Alice said you're bad at sprint planning."
4. Do not create a shadow reporting structure. The skip-level is for sensing, not managing. The middle manager remains the report's manager.

### When to cancel vs. when to reschedule

**Do cancel (and do not reschedule) when:**
- The report has nothing to discuss and you have no feedback or coaching topics. (Rare, but if it happens consistently, the cadence is too frequent.)
- You are on PTO and the report has no urgent topics.

**Do NOT cancel when:**
- You are "too busy." Canceling a one-on-one because you are busy communicates that the report is not a priority. Reschedule instead.
- The report says "I don't have anything." This is a signal that the relationship needs investment. The manager should have backup questions ready.
- You are having a bad day. Your mood is not the report's responsibility. Show up and be present.

### One-on-one cadence by report type

| Report type | Cadence | Duration | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| New report (first 3 months) | Weekly | 30-45 min | Onboarding, role clarity, early feedback, building trust |
| Experienced report (3-12 months) | Bi-weekly | 30 min | Coaching, growth, challenges, alignment |
| Senior report (1+ year) | Bi-weekly or monthly | 30 min | Career development, organizational context, strategic alignment |
| High-performer | Bi-weekly | 30 min | Growth opportunities, stretch assignments, retention |
| Under-performer | Weekly | 30-45 min | Clear expectations, specific feedback, improvement plan |
| Remote report | Weekly | 30 min | Connection, isolation check, async communication hygiene |

## Action recommendations

1. Set a recurring one-on-one for each direct report. Weekly for new reports, bi-weekly for experienced reports. Never cancel; always reschedule if needed.
2. Prepare for each one-on-one with a shared document (Google Doc, Notion) where both parties add agenda items throughout the week. The report adds items first; the manager adds items before the meeting.
3. Start every one-on-one with a human check-in, not a work question. "How are you?" is not a cliche; it is the most important question.
4. In coaching conversations, ask at least three questions before offering any advice. The goal is to build the report's capability, not to solve the problem for them.
5. Give feedback in every one-on-one. If you only give feedback during performance reviews, you are failing as a manager. Feedback should be specific, timely, and actionable.
6. Do quarterly skip-level one-on-ones with each skip-level report. Share themes (not individuals) back to the middle manager.
7. Document action items and follow up at the next one-on-one. Accountability is built on follow-through.
8. Ask for feedback on your own management at least once per quarter. "What could I do better as your manager?"

## Anti-patterns

- **The one-on-one as a status update** -- the most common and most damaging anti-pattern. Status updates belong in standups, sprint reviews, or async channels. The one-on-one is for the person, not the project.
- **Canceling one-on-ones when busy** -- communicates that the report is the lowest priority. If you are too busy for your reports, you are too busy to be a manager.
- **The manager dominating the conversation** -- if the manager is talking more than 30% of the time, the balance is wrong. The one-on-one is the report's meeting.
- **Saving all feedback for performance reviews** -- if the report hears about a problem for the first time in a performance review, the manager has failed. Feedback should be continuous and timely.
- **Solving problems instead of coaching** -- "here's what you should do" is faster in the short term but builds dependency. Coaching builds capability. Direct when necessary; coach by default.
- **Skip-levels that undermine the middle manager** -- making decisions, giving direction, or sharing confidential information in skip-levels destroys the middle manager's authority and trust.
- **No follow-up on action items** -- talking about problems without committing to actions and following up is therapy, not management. One-on-ones should produce commitments and accountability.
